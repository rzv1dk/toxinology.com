## Clinical Effects

Fatal outcome potentially possible

There is no clinical information on stings by most cone shell species and it is likely that many are not capable of injuring humans. The following is based on experience with those few species known to have caused envenoming in humans. It should not be assumed that this species' will show such severe effects, but should envenoming occur, it is most likely to fit the pattern described below.

Cone snail stings are potentially painful, but in many cases the sting causes only minor pain and may even not be noticed. When pain is present it is occasionally moderately severe. Often numbness develops around the sting area. There may be bleeding from around the sting site, Local swelling is possible, but not common.

The most important symptoms are systemic. A variable time after the sting, but potentially quite rapidly, in less than 60 mins, progressive flaccid paralysis develops, with ptosis, dysarthria, dysphagia, limb weakness and respiratory paralysis, resulting in hypoxia, coma or death in severe cases. The progression from first signs to full respiratory paralysis varies from less than 60 mins to many hours. Not all cases will progress from minor flaccid paralysis to full paralysis. Reversal of paralysis may take several hours, occasionally several days.

### Special Risk Groups
#### Children

Risk to children from local or systemic envenoming is similar in nature to the risk for adults, but a childs smaller body mass implies that the comparitive venom concentration will be higher, so more severe envenoming is likely, the increased risk being inversely proportional to the size of the child.

#### Pregnancy

Potential risk to foetus from systemic envenoming, similar to risk to mother

#### Elderly

The higher incidence of pre-existing diseases in the elderly and associated frailty place them at higher risk should envenoming occur. The nature and extent of increased risk will largely be determined by the nature and extent of pre-existing disease and by any medications used to treat such disease.

## First Aid

Keep victim still, apply pressure immobilisation bandage & splint, gently wash bite area, transport to medical care

First aid for Conus (cone snail) stings

1. After ensuring the patient and onlookers have moved out of range of further strikes by the cone snail, the bitten person should be reassured and persuaded to lie down and remain still. Some will be terrified, fearing sudden death and, in this mood, they may behave irrationally or even hysterically. The basis for reassurance is the fact that many cone snail stings do not result in envenoming and death is a rare outcome.
1. The sting wound should not be tampered with in any way. Wiping it once with a damp cloth to remove surface venom is unlikely to do much harm (or good) but the wound must not be massaged.
1. All rings or other jewellery on the bitten limb, especially on fingers, should be removed, as they may act as tourniquets if oedema develops.
1. If the bite is on a limb, a broad bandage (even torn strips of clothing or pantyhose) should be applied over the bitten area at moderate pressure (as for a sprain; not so tight circulation is impaired), then extended to cover as much of the bitten limb as possible, including fingers or toes, going over the top of clothing rather than risking excessive limb movement by removing clothing. The bitten limb should then be immobilised as effectively as possible using an extemporised splint or sling.
1. If there is any impairment of vital functions, such as problems with respiration, airway, circulation, heart function, these must be supported as a priority. In particular, for bites causing flaccid paralysis, including respiratory paralysis, both airway and respiration may be impaired, requiring urgent and prolonged treatment, which may include the mouth to mask (mouth to mouth) technique of expired air transfer. Seek urgent medical attention.
1. Do not use Tourniquets, cut, suck or scarify the wound or apply chemicals or electric shock.
1. Avoid peroral intake, absolutely no alcohol. No sedatives outside hospital. If there will be considerable delay before reaching medical aid, measured in several hours to days, then give clear fluids by mouth to prevent dehydration.
1. If the offending cone snail has been killed it should be brought with the patient for identification, but be careful to avoid touching a still living cone snail, as it can sting anyone attempting to pick it up. There are no safe places to hold a cone snail. If in doubt, either leave it alone or use an object such as a stick to push it into a sealable container.
1. The cone snail sting victim should be transported as quickly and as passively as possible to the nearest place where they can be seen by a medically-trained person (health station, dispensary, clinic or hospital). The bitten limb must not be exercised as muscular contraction will promote systemic absorption of venom. If no motor vehicle or boat is available, the patient can be carried on a stretcher or hurdle, on the pillion or crossbar of a bicycle or on someone's back.
1. Most traditional, and many of the more recently fashionable, first aid measures are useless and potentially dangerous. These include local cauterization, incision, excision, amputation, suction by mouth, vacuum pump or syringe, combined incision and suction ("venom-ex" apparatus), injection or instillation of compounds such as potassium permanganate, phenol (carbolic soap) and trypsin, application of electric shocks or ice (cryotherapy), use of traditional herbal, folk and other remedies including the ingestion of emetic plant products and parts of the snake, multiple incisions, tattooing and so on.

## Medical Treatment

Treatment for significant cone snail envenoming is support of respiratory function and management of hypotension, associated with rapid, potentially lethal flaccid neurotoxic paralysis. No antivenom is available.

While most cone snail stings, even by the few species known to have envenomed humans, will be minor, lack of case data accross the wide range of species and the fact that envenoming can be rapidly lethal, mandate that all cases be urgently assessed and admitted for at least 12-24 hrs, until it is clear no flaccid paralysis has occurred.

No antivenom is available, so treatment is focussed on supporting vital functions. The main risks are potentially rapid and severe flaccid paralysis, including full respiratory paralysis, and possibly secondary hypotension and cardiotoxicity, plus sequelae of major shock, should that occur. The principle risk is respiratoey failure due to paralysis. Intubation and mechanical ventilation may be required, but it appears paralysis is measured in hours, rarely a day or so, not the many days to weeks seen with some paralytic snake venoms.

- ~a-Conotoxin SI [ Syn : ~a-SI , ~a-CTx-SI ]. 13 AA with four cysteine residues. Blocks nicotinic ACh receptors in fish. Zafaralla et al. (1988) ( Ref : R001172 ). Olivera (2002) (Ref: R007848).
- ~a-Conotoxin SIA [ Syn : ~a-SIA , ~a-CTx-SIA ]. 14 AA with four cysteine residues. Blocks nicotinic ACh receptors. Myers et al. (1991) ( Ref : R001173 ). 29 AA O-superfamily Conopeptide with a charge of +4. Bai-Song et al. (1999) (R: R007660). Olivera (2002) (Ref: R007848).
- ~w-Conotoxin. 24 to 29 AA peptides with 3 disulfide bridges. L-type Ca++ channel blockers.

- ~w-Conotoxin SVIB. 26 AA with six cysteine residues and mass 2.739 kDa. Presynaptic Ca++ channel blocker. Lethal to mice upon i.c injection. Wen et al. (2005) (Ref: R007694) Ramilo et al. (1992) (Ref: R007591).

- ~a Conotoxin SO3. 28 AA Bai-Song et al. (1999) (R: R007660) 25 AA Qiuyun et al. (2003) (Ref: R007691) Wen et al. (2005) (Ref: R007694). O-superfamily Conopeptide with a charge of +5 Bai-Song et al. (1999) (Ref: R007660) and mass 2560.1 Da with six cysteines and three disulfide bridges. Qiuyun et al. (2003) (Ref: R007691). Analogous to MVIIA from Conus magus and synthetic SO3 induces trembling and reduced sensitivity to heat injury when injected i.c into mice. Bai-Song et al. (1999) (R: R007660). Non-synthetic SO3 shown to be a potent, non-addictive, analgesic agent in mice with the threshold for tail withdrawal increasing by a factor 100 - 150 after a dose of 1.2 - 2.0 ~mg/kg when exposed to the mechanical stimulus tolerance model. Shown to be 3000-fold and 50-fold more potent than morphine administered by systemic and intrathecal injection respectively. Qiuyun et al. (2003) (Ref: R007691). Inhibits N-type high voltage-activated calcium currents (involved in pain transduction) in a concentration dependant manner. Wen et al. (2005) (Ref: R007694).
- ~a Conotoxin SO4. 37 AA O-superfamily Conopeptide with a charge of +1. Bai-Song et al. (1999) (R: R007660).
- ~a Conotoxin SO5. 37 AA O-superfamily Conopeptide with a charge of +1. Bai-Song et al. (1999) (R: R007660).
- ~a Conotoxin SO6. 36 AA O-superfamily Conopeptide with a charge of -3. Bai-Song et al. (1999) (R: R007660).
- ~kA-Conotoxin SIVA. ~kA-Superfamily Conotoxin termed "Spastic peptide" 30 AA with mass about 4084.5 - 4100 Da and three disulfide bridges. Induces characteristic spastic symptoms when injected into fish, and in frog nerve preparations. Motor nerves shown to respond with repetitive action potentials when a single stimulus applied. Graig et al. (1998) (Ref: R007679) Norton and Olivera (2006) (Ref: R008272).
- CsTx Conotoxin. Glycoprotein with mass 25 kDa. Slows sodium channel inactivation without altering the time course of activation of channels in a voltage-dependant manner. Maximum toxin effects occur at membrane potentials between -100 to -60 mV. Found to have no effect on the binding of saxitoxin or Leiurus ~a scorpion toxin to their receptor sites but enhanced the binding of 20-~a-benzoate to sodium channels at the same concentration levels as its physiological effects. - Conk-S1. 60 AA with six cysteine residues and mass 6929.3 Da . Interacts with shaker voltage-gated potassium channels. Bayrhuber et al. (2005) (Ref: R007696).
- Conotoxin SIIIA. ~m-Conotoxin. 20 AA with six cysteine residues and 3 disulfide bridges and mass 2206.9 Da. ### Crude Venom

Crude Venom : Venom extracts were shown to be toxic to mice ( minimum lethal dose was 11 to 57 mg / kg ), toxic to blennies ( minimum lethal dose was 0.2 to 0.8 mg / kg ), non-toxic to Phyllodoce - worms ( 0.5 mg venom per dose ) and lethal to Melarapha - gastropods ( 850 ~mg venom per dose ) but without causing paralysis. Endean and Rudkin (1965) ( Ref : R000979 ).

Crude Venom : Effects of venom ( 0.001 g / ml and 20 minutes exposure time ) were studied on mouse isolated diaphragm, guinea pig isolated left atria, guinea pig ileum and rabbit isolated aorta. An excitatory effect causing potent rhythmic, transient contractions of guinea pig ileum followed by a potent inhibitory effect and relaxation was elicited. This effect which was abolished by tetrodotoxin and low Na-medium suggesting the contraction of the ileum is due to excitation of cholinergic nerves while relaxation is mediated through non-adrenergic inhibitory nerves. Kobaysahi et al. (1982) (Ref: R007594). A potent excitatory effect followed by a potent inhibitory effect was elicited on guinea pig isolated left atria. A potent excitatory effect was also elicited on mouse diaphragm. Kobayashi et al. (1982) ( Ref : R000949 ).

Crude venom mildly binds to ~a2-adrenergic calf membrane receptors. Czerwiec et al. (1989)(Ref: R007554).

Other Toxins

Arg-Conopressin-S : Lys-Conopressin-S ( "scratcher peptide" ) is a 9 AA peptide with a single disulfide bridge, Mol. Wt. 1,028 and sequence { Cys Ile Ile Arg Asn Cys Pro Arg Gly NH2 }. Intracerebral injection in mice at very low doses caused the mice to scratch at their face ( appeared to "groom their face" ). The scratching response is dose dependant with respect to time of onset and duration of symptoms. Cruz et al. (1987) ( Ref : R001177 ).

### Neurotoxins Channel Toxins

~a-Conotoxin SI : ~a-Conotoxin SI is a 13 AA acid peptide { Ile Cys Cys Asn Pro Ala Cys Gly Pro Lys Tyr Ser Cys NH2 }, Mol Wt = 1,353 with 2 disulfide bridges. Biological activity was tested on mice, frogs and fish by i.p and intracranial injection. In goldfish ( 1 to 1.5 g ), 1-nmol injection caused obvious paralytic effects and death while doses as low as 0.1 nmol caused paralytic effects. Similar effects were observed for ~a-Conotoxin MI ( C. magus ) and ~a-Conotoxin GI ( C. geographus ) but were less potent than ~a-Conotoxin SI. Binding experiments ( Torpedo electric organ as test tissue ) using 125I-~a-bungarotoxin as a probe showed that ~a-Conotoxins SI, M1 and G1 were capable of displacing all specific binding to the Torpedo receptor with similar affinity ( IC50 = 0.42, 0.30 and 0.55 respectively. In marked contrast to the effects of fish ~a-Conotoxin SI was less effective in mice. Intraperitoneal injection of ~a-Conotoxin SI in mice showed little effect at doses from 2 to 10 nmol / mouse ( ~a-Conotoxin MI and ~a-Conotoxin GI routinely caused death in mice within 10 minutes at a dose of 0.2 nmol / mouse ). Similar results were obtained for intracranial injection of mice. Results demonstrate that ~a-Conotoxin SI has relatively low effectiveness on mammalian nicotinic ACh receptors compared to other ~a-Conotoxins but is an effective neurotoxin that blocks nicotinic ACh receptors in fish. Zafaralla et al. (1988) ( Ref : R001172 ).

~a-Conotoxin SIA : ~a-Conotoxin SIA is a 13 AA residue peptide with 2 disulfide bridges, Mol. Wt = 1,455 and sequence { Tyr Cys Cys His Pro Ala Cys Gly Lys Asn Phe Asp Cys NH2 }. Unlike ~a-Conotoxin SI, ~a-Conotoxin SIA exhibits significant biological activity in mice but was not as potent as ~a-Conotoxin MI or ~a-Conotoxin GI. Intraperitoneal injection of 1 nmol of ~a-Conotoxin GI or MI caused death to mice within 10 mins but ~a-Conotoxin SI caused paralysis only. Intraperitoneal injection of 1.6 nmol of ~a-Conotoxin SIA caused death to mice within 90 mins and 5 nmol of ~a-Conotoxin SIA caused death to mice within 6 minutes. A similar ( but more rapid ) response was observed when intracranial injection of toxins was performed. Myers et al. (1991) ( Ref : R001173 ).

~kA-Conotoxin SIVA. ~kA-Superfamily Conotoxin termed "Spastic peptide". Induces characteristic spastic symptoms when injected into fish, and in frog nerve preparations. Motor nerves shown to respond with repetitive action potentials when a single stimulus applied. Graig et al. (1998) (Ref: R007679).

~d-Conotoxin SVIE. O-superfamily Conotoxin 31 AA with six cysteine residues and mass 3320 Da. Potent excitotoxin when injected into mice inducing spastic paralysis and death at higher concentrations. Elicits severe symptoms including twitching of the hind limbs (dose 10 pmol/g) and running in circles before going into spastic paralysis (70 pmol/g). This activity is consistent with delaying inactivation of voltage-gated sodium channels. Slows down inactivation of Na channels by interacting with a conserved hydrophobic triad in the domain-4 voltage sensor of Na channels Leipold et al. (2005) (Ref: R007702). Also shows a striking effect on frog sciatic nerve preparations (2.5 ~mM) inducing a greatly prolonged compound action potential. Bulaj et al. (2001) (Ref: R007684). Slows the time-course of inactivation of ~d sodium currents and shifts the voltage-dependence of activation and steady-state inactivation to more hyperpolarized potentials in dissociated sympathetic frog neurons. West et al. (2005) (Ref: R007816).

Conotoxin SIIIA. ~m-Conotoxin which inhibits TTX-resistant sodium currents in neurons of frog sympathetic and dorsal root ganglia but poorly block action potentials in frog skeletal muscle, which are mediated by TTX-sensitive sodium channels. Bulaj et al. (2005) (Ref: R007703).
