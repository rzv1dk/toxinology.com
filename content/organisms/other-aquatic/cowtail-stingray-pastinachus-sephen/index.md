## Case Studies

Barss P. (1984) Wound necrosis caused by the venom of stingrays. Pathological findings and surgical management. Med J Aust, Vol. 141, pp 854 - 855.

Case Report.
18 yr. old male, fishing with handline, from a dugout canoe in Milne Bay Province (Papua New Guinea), caught what he believed to be a large fish. As he jumped into the water to help lift what turned out to be a stingray (90 cm diameter P. sephen) into the canoe, he was struck on (R) side chest by the sting. At the health centre he was found to have a sucking chest wound to which an occlusive dressing was applied, prior to his transfer to hospital. On examination at hospital he showed severe wound pain, a 1 cm puncture wound in (R) pectoral muscle region but was in satisfactory general condition.

Treatment included surgical exploration of wound (12 hours post injury) with the skin wound excised and the puncture traced through pectoral muscle into chest, between ribs. It was found that penetration into thorax had occurred but no serious damage was sustained to the lung. A 1 cm haemorrhagic area was also noted on visceral pleura with a 6 cm diameter area surrounding the puncture on the pectoral muscle dusky but not bleeding nor contracting. Dead muscle was excised and the wound closed. Commenced local and systemic broad spectrum antibiotic therapy was administered and the wound healed without further complication.

Excised tissue was sent for pathological examination. It was found that muscle was totally necrotic, there was a great deal of haemorrhage, together with an early and heavy infiltration of neutrophils. Several large vessels in the specimen showed acute vasculizing with neutrophils crowded within the tissues of their walls. There was also a heavy infiltration of neutrophils in the skin, and definite collagen necrosis in the septae in the subcutaneous fat layer.

## Clinical Effects

Fatal outcome rare

Stingray injuries vary from trivial local damage and moderate pain, through to major, potentially lethal damage, severe pain and systemic effects. The spectrum of severity is not established for most stingrays, but in general, the larger the stingray, the greater the potential for significant injury. Most injuries occur to the foot or lower leg, when stepping on or near to a stingray. However, especially with large stingrays, injuries can occur to almost any part of the body. Major limb vessels have been lacerated, resulting in exsanguination. The abdomen and chest have been penetrated, also with sometimes lethal consequences, notably where the chest has been penetrated through to the heart. Such rare cases can result in rapid death, but delayed death has also been reported, due to progressive cardiac tamponade or rupture.

These major mechanical trauma problems may dominate the clinical picture and are, in general, of more importance than the effects of envenoming, the latter being limited in virtually all cases to causing intense local pain. The sting wound is usually a laceration, either minor or deep, sometimes damaging underlying structures. Tendons, nerves, blood vessels may all be traumatised or completely severed. The trauma itself will give rise to major pain. The sting tip can break off in the wound and act as a nidus for infection. As the sting occurs in water, infection is always possible and probably common.

Around the sting spine is a primitive venom gland and this venom may cause intense pain, an effect which is heat-labile, as pain is usually reduced or abolished while in warm to hot water.

## First Aid

First aid for Stingray stings

1. The victim should immediately leave the water.
1. Loose broken stings in limb wounds, away from major blood vessels, can be gently removed. If force is required, leave the sting alone. Stings to the chest and abdomen, in general, should be left untouched, as removal may cause further damage and endanger the patient. In particular, stings to the chest wall, near the heart, can prove lethal and inexpert removal of the sting can precipitate rapid collapse and death.
1. The wound should not be rubbed, or stings crushed as this may worsen the local effects of the sting.
1. There may be significant tissue injury and bleeding. Staunch bleeding by application of local pressure. If necessary, apply a bandage to maintain local pressure over the wound. Only if there is severe bleeding, caused by damage to major blood vessels, apply a tourniquet above the wound, to a single-boned part of the limb (thigh for leg; upper arm for arm) to control the bleeding. Tourniquets are effective at controlling bleeding, but in doing so, deprive the limb of oxygen, so cannot be left on for more than 30-45 minutes, or they will cause permanent injury. They therefore require regular release for short periods.
1. The use of immersion in hot water is controversial. Some authorities suggest that immersing the stung limb in hot water may reduce pain. Others suggest it is useless. For stingray injuries, experience suggests hot water immersion is often very effective at providing pain relief and therefore should always be considered. However, it is potentially hazardous, if used incorrectly, as it may cause burn injuries. If used, immerse the opposite limb to that stung in water which is hot, but bearable with prolonged contact. It should not be so hot as to cause pain. Then immerse the stung limb as far as necessary to include the sting area. Keep both limbs in the water for 15-20 minutes, providing there is relief of pain. If there is no relief of pain in this time, abandon the immersion. If pain has subsided, it may return on removal from the water, in which case, reimmerse for a further 15-20 minutes. Repeat this process up to 4 times. If after 1-2 hours, the pain is still severe on removal from hot water, then other forms of pain relief may be required, but these will require medical intervention.
1. Transport the victim to medical care as soon as safely possible, except where the wound is very minor and pain has been easily and fully controlled and the victim has current anti-tetanus immunisation.
1. All stingray wounds to the chest, abdomen, or neck are potentially very serious, even if the wound looks minor and no sting barb is visible. All such cases require urgent hospital assessment.

## Medical Treatment

Stings by marine stingrays are generally associated with significant local pain and physical trauma. Treatment is directed at dealing with these problems, as envenoming is not a major issue (other than any pain-producing effects of the venom).

Initial pain is often responsive to immersion of the stung area in hot (45C, not scalding) water for an hour (ensure no thermal injury occurs). Beyond this, analgesia can be provided by an escalating scale, from oral, through parenteral analgesics, to regional anaesthetic nerve block.

The wound should be inspected for retained sting fragments and these removed. Major bleeding following transection of vessels should be staunched. Beware wounds where the sting has penetrated a body cavity, either the abdomen, or the chest. Penetrating injuries over the heart can prove lethal. Beware early or late haemopericardium.

Stingray wounds can develop late necrosis and secondary infection. Beware tetanus.

### Management in Detail
#### Immediate Effects Management

Immerse stung area in hot (45C, not scalding) water to relieve pain. Further analgesia as required.

#### Approach to Management

First priority is relief of pain and staunching bleeding if major vessel transected. Thereafter symptomatic care, good wound care.
