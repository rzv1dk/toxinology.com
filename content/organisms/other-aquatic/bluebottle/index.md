## Clinical Effects

Fatal outcome rare

The Portuguese man-o-war or bluebottle is a common "jellyfish", usually found in large swarms, drifting on the sea surface, or blown ashore. It is therefore possible for a number of people to be stung in a short time by members of the swarm, as they drift into recreational waters, such as off a beach. Exact numbers of stings per year are unknown, but based on experience with other true jellyfish which swarm near beaches, there may be many thousands of stings each year. While the stings are painful, they are not likely to be lethal through toxicity. However, Physalia stings appear more likely to cause severe allergic ("anaphylactic") reactions, even on first exposure, than other cnidarian stings, and several "anaphylactic" deaths have been reported, as well as severe local reactions, also thought to be allergic in nature.

The typical Physalia sting causes moderate to severe local pain, with typical sting contact marks, often with round areas along the sting track, corresponding to contractions in the tentacles, creating "stinging buttons". Blistering can ocur, but necrosis is uncommon. The pain will usually settle over a period of an hour or more. Extensive stings can cause very severe pain, sufficient to cause collapse.

Systemic effects are not common, but can occur, including headache, vomiting and abdominal pain. Rarely the victim may collapse. Respiratory arrest and cardiac failure or arrest have been reported, in exceptional cases. The cause of this severe systemic reaction remains unclear, but some authorities believe it has an immune basis. In one fatal case the victim suffered a respiratory arrest prior to a cardiac arrest, but it is unclear whether there was major bronchospasm.

There is one most unusual case of brachial artery spasm following a Physalia sting, the spasm responding to intra-arterial reserpine.

### Special Risk Groups
#### Children

Children are at potentially greater risk than adults, because of smaller size, therefore higher venom concentrations.

#### Pregnancy

There is no evidence that venom will directly affect the foetus, but it may be harmed should severe envenoming occur in the mother.

#### Elderly

The elderly or infirm are at greater risk of either primary envenoming or secondary effects such as myocardial ischaemia.

## First Aid

Wash stung area with water to inactivate unfired stinging cells, remove adherrent tentacles, apply a cold pack.

First aid for marine coelenterate injuries (Jellyfish)

1. Remove the victim from the water, at all times being aware of the potential for further stings, both to the victim and rescuers. In areas where box jellyfish may occur, if possible wear protective clothing, such as stinger suits or pantyhose. Be careful not to touch undischarged tentacles on the victim with bare skin, as this may result in the rescuer also being stung.
1. Unless the sting is known or highly likely to be from a box jellyfish or close relative, do not douse the area in vinegar, as this may discharge unfired stinging cells.
1. Apply a cold pack to the stung area. Do not use cold or fresh water, as this may cause stinging cell discharge.
1. Assess vital functions, especially airway, breathing and circulation. Apply respiratory and cardiac support, as appropriate. If there is cardiac arrest, commence full resuscitation in an optimistic fashion and continue until medical care is reached.
1. If available, give oxygen. Entonox (nitrous oxide), if available, may be used for pain.
1. If the victim is in severe pain or shows signs of a significant general reaction, urgently transport the victim to medical care. If the reaction is mild and the pain settles readily, it may not be necessary to seek medical advice, but if in any doubt, seek advice.

## Medical Treatment

Most stings will cause local pain only, requiring analgesia, but beware uncommon systemic reactions, requiring hospitalisation and rare respiratory and cardiac collapse, requiring full resuscitation.

While most Physalia stings will be minor, requiring no more than local analgesia, severe local pain can occur, requiring more vigorous analgesia. Rarely there may be major systemic effects, including respiratory failure and arrest, requiring intubation and ventilation, and cardiac failure or arrest, requiring full resuscitation. For cardiotoxicity, verapamil has been recommended, but this is now highly controversial, with some authorities suggesting verapamil may actually worsen cardiotoxicity. Because of this controversy and the lack of clear clinical evidence of effectiveness, verapamil cannot be recommended at present. There is no antivenom to reverse major systemic toxicity, so management must be reactive and symptomatic.

There is at least one case of peripheral (brachial) atery spasm following a sting; this responded to intra-arterial reserpine.
