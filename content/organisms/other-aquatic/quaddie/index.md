## Antivenom

### Antivenom Therapy

Only antivenoms available are for related species, but should be considered for severe envenoming, particularly life threatening envenoming. However, the effectiveness of antivenom in such cases is unproven and speculative and the relative risks versus benefit should be assessed on a case-by-case basis.

### Known Antivenoms
#### NOTE: Order of antivenoms is not indicitive of preference.

Box jellyfish antivenom

MAuCSL01 by CSL
#### Description

Salt precipitated. Liquid final product 1.63 to 3.74 ml. 20000 units / ampoule. 1 unit neutralizes 0.01 mg venom in vitro.

#### Comments

There is recent evidence that Box Jellyfish Antivenom does successfully neutralise the lethal jellyfish cardiotoxins, but that these toxins enter the circulation so quickly and exert their effect very quickly so that it may be difficult to give antivenom in time to prevent cardiotoxicity. This is not a reason to avoid antivenom use, but does indicate some limitations to effectiveness in rare very severe cases with cardiac dysrhythmia or arrest.

#### Recommended Dose

1 vial (IV), but in severe cases 3 vials (IM) and if cardiac arrest, 3 vials, up to 6 vials.

## Clinical Effects

Rate of fatality unclear, due to inadequate reporting, but fatalities cannot be excluded

The taxonomy of these and related cubomedusan jellyfish is currently in a state of flux. It is possible that jellyfish found in waters to the north of Australia such as Mayalsian and Indonesian waters, may not be Chiropsalmus spp., but rather some new undescribed species of Chironex.

What is known is that potentially severe, even life threatening stings may occur from these jellyfish in this region.

Clinical features may be similar to Australian Chironex fleckeri stings, though generally less severe. Thus there may be instant severe local pain in areas of tentacle contact, with ladder marks and potential for local skin blistering and possible subsequent skin necrosis. In severe stings involving large areas of tentacle contact (perhaps involving > half of one limb in area) there may be rapid onset (3-15 minutes) of systemic envenoming. This may possibly include cardiac arrhythmia/collapse/arrest, similar to severe Chironex fleckeri stings.

Less severe stings may potentially cause respiratory effects, notably ill-defined "respiratory failure", which is probably not true respiratory paralysis. Pulmonary oedema might occur.

However, as for Chironex fleckeri, most stings are likely to cause principally local effects, varying from severe but self-limited pain, through to skin damage along tentacle contact tracks, but without major and specific systemic effects.

### Special Risk Groups
#### Children

Children are at potentially greater risk than adults, because of smaller size, therefore higher venom concentrations.

#### Pregnancy

There is no evidence that venom will directly affect the foetus, but it may be harmed should severe envenoming occur in the mother.

#### Elderly

The elderly or infirm are at greater risk of either primary envenoming or secondary effects such as myocardial ischaemia.

## First Aid

Wash stung area with copious quantities of vinegar, then remove any remaining adherent tentacles carefully. If there is cardiac compromise commence full CPR if indicated and continue optimnistically for a prolonged period, if necessary, for at least 45-60 mins before deciding to cease because of lack of response.

First aid for Australian box-jellyfish or sea wasp (Chironex fleckeri) envenoming

1. Remove the victim from the water, at all times being aware of the potential for further stings, both to the victim and rescuers. If possible wear protective clothing, such as stinger suits or pantyhose. Be careful not to touch undischarged tentacles on the victim with bare skin, as this may result in the rescuer also being stung.
1. Immediately douse the entire stung area with copious quantities of vinegar for at least 30 seconds.
1. Assess vital functions, especially airway, breathing and circulation. Apply respiratory and cardiac support, as appropriate. If there is cardiac arrest, commence full resuscitation in an optimistic fashion and continue until medical care is reached.
1. If there is major envenoming with cardiac complications or arrest or respiratory complications, and CSL Box Jellyfish Antivenom is available at the beach, give 3 ampoules intramuscularly (providing those administering are trained to do so). If suitably trained personnel are available to give antivenom IV, then this is preferable to IM.
1. If available, give oxygen. Entonox (nitrous oxide), if available, may be used for pain.
1. Urgently transport the victim to medical care.

## Medical Treatment

The treatment of severe stings by this and related jellyfish is controversial and uncertain. There is no specific antivenom. In cases with cardiac collapse immediate, prolonged full CPR is appropriate, continued for at least 45-60 minutes, or until return of heart function.

The spectrum of clinical effects for stings by this group of jellyfish are poorly defined, which makes recommendations for treatment difficult. There is limited evidence that some jellyfish in waters in SE Asia (notably around Malaysia and Indonesia) may be clinically and possibly taxonomically similar to Australian box jellyfish, Chironex fleckeri. On this basis the following treatment recommendations are made, but these should be viewed as interim only, awaiting more substantial clinical and research data on which to base future recommendations.

The tentacle contact wounds should be washed with copious amounts of vinegar (if not already done as first aid on the beach) to inactivate remaining unfired nematocysts, then the tentacles should be carefully removed from the skin, preferably using gloved hands (note: it is unclear if or how much sterile medical gloves protect against jellyfish stings).

Provide appropriate and substantial analgesia proportionate with the level of pain, up to and including parenteral analgesia.

Clean the tentacle wounds.

Ensure tetanus immunisation is current.

If there are any systemic effects consider insertion of an IV line plus IV fluids and cardiac monitor the patient.

If there is cardiovascular compromise, notably cardiac arrhythmias or arrest, CPR is appropriate and should be maintained for a prolonged period (at least 45-60 minutes), assuming there is no evidence that the patient has been in cardiac arrest for a prolonged period pre-treatment, thereby likely causing non-recoverable major organ damage. Clinical judgement is required in deciding on how long to maintain CPR in the latter setting.

There is no specific antivenom available and no studies to guide possible use of other antivenoms raised against related species. The only possible antivenom to consider is CSL Ltd (Melbourne, Australia) Box Jellyfish Antivenom (BJAV). Even for the target species, Chironex fleckeri, the role and effectiveness of this antivenom in Australia is controversial, with conflicting research and clinical evidence. Some ongoing research, mostly unpublished at time of writing this CTRW record, may indicate a dose issue, with higher doses being required than currently used. In the setting of a likely or proven cubomedusan jellyfish sting with cardiac collapse/arrhythmia/arrest, where a fatal outcome seems probable, it may be reasonable to consider a trial of BJAV IV at high doses (at least 3 vials, up to 6 vials, possible more), in conjunction with standard CPR. CTRW cannot state whether such use of BJAV will be beneficial in care of such patients and the decision to use BJAV must be made on a case-by-case basis.
