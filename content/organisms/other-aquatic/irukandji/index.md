## Case Studies

Little M., Mulcahy R. and Wenck D.J. (2001) Life-Threatening Cardiac Failure in a Healthy Young Female with Irukandji Syndrome. Anaesth. Intensive Care, Vol. 29, No. 2, pp 178-180.

Case Report.
A 24 yr. old female marine biologist was admitted to Cairns Base Hospital, Australia after having been retrieved by helicopter from the Great Barrier Reef. While snorkeling, she felt sting on (L) arm, axilla and anterior neck. On return to boat, pain worsened in the limb and she also complained of abdominal and back pain. Ice and vinegar was liberally applied to sting site and a pressure immobilization bandage applied to stung limb. During the return flight back and abdominal pain became more severe and she developed diaphoresis and headache.

On arrival at hospital, 3 hrs 30 min after the bite she showed a markedly distressed; pulse of 110 b.p.m and blood pressure - 170/ 110 mm.Hg. Her respiration rate was 16/ min, SpO2 99% (room air). Within 1 hour of admission, this had desaturated to SpO2 - 84%. An obvious welt on (L) arm and neck was evident on removal of pressure immobilization bandage from the arm. Notable significant (L) ventricular dysfunction with tachycardia was also noted with a third heart sound and crackles through lung fields. She was diagnosed as having been stung by a Carukia barnesi (Irukandji syndrome). Initial investigations revealed a normal full blood count; urea and electrolytes; cardiac troponin I. She had an elevated creatinine kinase (CK) of 180U/ L and a chest Xray revealed perihilar shadowing and an electrocardiograph showed global (L) ventricular dysfunction with an ejection fraction of 35%.

Management of the patient included administration of oxygen (high-flow via non-rebreather mask at 15L/ min), pethidine (25 mg aliquots to total 200 mg), promethazine 25 mg (i.v) and fentanyl. On admission to an Intensive Care Unit she was given frusemide, commenced on dobutamine and glyceryl trinitate (GTN) infusions.
Over the following 6 hrs, despite increasing the dose of dobutamine (11 mcg/ kg/ min) and GTN (23.3 mcg/ min) she showed deteriorating cardiovascular function with a B/P of 80/ 60 mm.Hg and respiratory function SpO2 of 89%. She was commenced on adrenaline and bi-level positive pressure non-invasive ventilation at which point dobutamine and GTN was ceased. Non-invasive ventilation was not well tolerated and the patient was intubated. A pulmonary catheter was also inserted soon after intubation. A post intubation chest Xray revealed bilateral alveolar shadowing. The patient was ventilated for 8 days, with FiO2 for up to 5 days. She remained on adrenaline for 72 hrs being given a maximal dose of 20 mcg./ min. for 30 hrs during which her cardiac troponin I peaked at 72 mcg/ L ( normal < 0.7mcg/ L) and CK peaked at 5930 U/ L. Serial ECGs showed improvement in cardiac function, with a return to normal function prior to discharge.

An attempt to collect nematocysts with sticky tape of the sting site and examination with electron microscopy was unsuccessful. A small macerated tentacle was obtained from the sting site however due to maceration could not be accurately identified. However,another local marine biologist was confident that the tentacle did not come from a Carukia barnesi (Irukandji) jellyfish. Finally the patient transferred to a ward 11 days post admission and discharged home on day 14. She suffered no further symptoms of envenomation and after 3 months gradually returned to normal activity.

Cheng A.C., Winkel K.D., Hawdon G.M. and McDonald M. (1999) Irukandji-like syndrome in Victoria. Aust. N. Z. J. Med., Vol. 29, No. 6, pp 835.

Case Report.
A 20 yr. old female presented to Geelong Hospital, Victoria Australia at 2200 hrs. January 1998 with 4 hour history of back ache, limb 'cramping', headache, vomiting, dyspnoea and severe pleuritic chest pain. She had occasionally used a salbutamol inhaler for mild asthma and did not have any risk factors for thromboembolic disease.
On presentation she was distressed, sweaty, agitated and had a pulse of 110 b.p.m, temperature - 37.4°C, blood pressure - 140/ 110 mm.Hg and respiration rate of - 24/ minute. Linear erythematous streaks surrounded by a 2 - 3 cm oedmatous skin area was noted on her calves. Arterial blood gas analysis revealed paO2 88.8 mm.Hg and paCO2 33.0 mm.Hg. An Electrocardiogram and chest Xray were normal.

Dyspnoea and chest pain suggested the possibility of pulmonary embolus, and she was commenced on heparin (i.v). After an hour of treatment with oral paracetamol and aspirin her tachycardia, hypertension, and dyspnoea had settled, but the chest pain persisted. A ventilation perfusion scan the next day suggested a low probability of pulmonary embolus and heparin infusion was then ceased. Upon further questioning the patient explained that she had been swimming near Queenscliff (Victoria) on the previous afternoon and 30 minutes after leaving water had noticed some welts on the back of her calves. She then noted that her constitutional symptoms began less than an hour later. The possibility of jellyfish envenomation was then raised, although she could not recall sighting a jellyfish. Symptoms and signs completely resolved within 24 hours and she was discharged form hospital.

Comment: This case seems typical of the milder Irukandji-like syndromes which have been attributed to various non Carukia jellyfish, such as Carybdea rastoni that may be present in Port Philip Bay. Clinicians should be alert to the possibility of envenomation by jellyfish, and this syndrome should be considered in the differential diagnosis of chest pain in the warmer months throughout coastal Australia.

Little M. and Mulcahy R. (1998) A year's experience of Irukandji envenomation in far north Queensland. Med. J. Aust., Vol. 169, No. 11-12, pp 638-641.

Envenomation by Carukia barnesi can result in an array of systemic symptoms known as Irukandji syndrome. In 1996, 62 people presented to Cairns emergency departments (Australia) with Irukandji envenomation. Of these 57 developed systemic symptoms, and 38 required parenteral narcotics. All patients were discharged home within 24.5 hours, except for 2 who required high-dependence care for pulmonary oedema. Patients were more likely to be stung on hotter days, with lower-than-average rainfall in the past 7 days, and with winds from the north, but less-than-average wind speed. The following outlines a proposed protocol for treating patients with Irukandji envenomation in this region of Australia following an extensive review of reported cases.

Methods:
Databases were searched to identify all patients with possible marine envenomation by jellyfish. Of all charts reviewed only patients who had at least 2 symptoms or signs of Irukandji envenomation were included. Daily weather data from the Bureau of Meteorology in Cairns were also collated.

Results:
The 62 patients ranged in age from 18 months to 56 years, 23 (37%) were younger than 14 years and 29 (47%) were male. No patients presented between the 18th May and 11th October. 47 (76%) patients were stung at coastal locations, 7 (11%) were stung on the Great Barrier Reef, and 5 (8%) on islands just off Cairns. Of 34 patients seen in December 1996 30 (88%) were stung at coastal locations compared with 17 of 26 (65%) between January and May. 39 patients (63%) were stung while swimming inside stinger net enclosures on the beaches.

Signs and symptoms:
57 of the 62 (92%) patients had systemic signs and/ or symptoms of envenomation, 38 (61%) required parenteral narcotics and 2 patients developed pulmonary oedema. Most common signs and symptoms included abdominal cramps (40%), hypertension (50%), Back pain (39%), nausea and vomiting (39%), limb cramps (34%), chest tightness (26%) and marked distress (24%).

Treatment:
Application of Vinegar was not recorded for 9 patients. Of the remaining 53 patients, 43 (81%) had vinegar applied. No notable adverse effects / worsening of symptoms were noted from application of vinegar. 38 (61%) adult patients were given opiates (pethidine and morphine). 14 (61%) children also required opiates. Promethazine was also administered to 16 patients, primarily as anti-emetic. There was a significant reduction in amount of pethidine used when promethazine was administered to adults. On average, 40 mg of pethidine was administered to patients who received promethazine, compared with 140 mg to patients who did not receive promethazine.

Retrievals:
6 (9.7%) patients required aeromedical transfer to Cairns Base Hospital, 5 by helicopter retrieval service and 1 by Royal Flying Doctor Service.

Outcome:
Of 62 patients treated 28 were discharged home within 6 hours of envenomation with minimal or no symptoms. 1 child, who initially received more than 5 mg/ kg. pethidine, re-presented with pain, hypertension and sweating 5 hours after discharge. 35 (56%) patients were admitted to hospital.

Weather:
57 (92%) patients were stung on days hotter than average for the month. 43 (69%) were stung on days with more hours of sunshine than average. 54 (87%) stung on days when 5 mm or less of rain fell during day. 47 (76%) were stung when less than average amount of rain had fallen in past 7 days. 47 (76%) were stung when wind was between north-north-west (NNW) and north-north-east (NNE). During 1996, the prevailing wind was between the NNE and NNW for 99 of 366 days (27% of the time).

Discussion:
In 1996 62 cases of Irukandji envenomation were reported in Cairns, with 2 patients developing pulmonary oedema. This represents the largest number of Irukandji envenomations reported from a single location in one year. Envenomations, usually associated with northerly winds. 47 (76%) of patients were stung on days when the wind was between the NNW and NNE. 35 (56%) patients were stung between 30 November and 19 December 1996. The most common location to be stung was in Palm Cove (17/ 62; 27%), about 25 km. north of Cairns. A higher proportion of people were stung on the Barrier Reef between January and May (9/ 26; 33%) compared with October to December (3/ 37; 8%).

Conclusion:
Very little is known about Carukia barnesi, its life cycle or its toxins. This study demonstrates that it causes significant morbidity, with potentially life-threatening symptoms. In view of the varied nature of patients' presentations, and that 27 (44%) patients were discharged within 2.1 hours, it is possible that there may be more than one type of jellyfish responsible for the Irukandji syndrome. Irukandji presents a significant workload for hospital emergency departments in presentations, retrievals and admissions.

Case Report.
A 28 yr old male who was an experienced diver was diving off Brampton Island, Queensland, Australia. Whilst diving, at depth of 6 metres, he had a feeling of disequilibrium upon which he made a controlled ascent. On surfacing he experienced vague malaise accompanied by aching pain in groin and thighs and numbness in fingers and toes. This rapidly progressed to severe pain in the skin, muscles, face, jaw, testes and lumbar region, with profuse sweating and agitation.

On arrival at hospital 6 hrs later he was experiencing great pain, was tachycardic with heart rate 90 b.p.m and hypertensive with BP 170/ 100 mm.Hg. He was treated with 100% oxygen and pethidine (i.v). There were no diagnostic clues except that symptoms had developed rapidly within minutes of his ascent. Decompression sickness was considered but after consultation with a consultant at Townsville General Hospital Hyperbaric Unit, a dive buddy and a review of his dive history this diagnosis was dismissed. Irukandji syndrome was suggested after the patient recalled seeing a small jellyfish while descending and feeling a slight irritation on his (R) arm. A 3 cm x 4 cm oval-shaped inflamed area was evident on the (R) antecubittal fossa.
He was commenced on a morphine (i.v) infusion over 10 hours. Dull chest pain and lower limb pain persisted but did not require further analgesia. Serum creatinine phosphokinase level peaked at 7317 IU/ L (normal range, 10 - 60 IU/ L) and serum lactate dehydrogenase level reached 349 IU/ L (normal range - 120 - 300 IU/ L). Echocardiography after 2 days showed mild mitral valve regurgitation.

Fenner P.J. and Heazlewood R.J. (1997) Papilloedema and coma in a child: undescribed symptoms of the "Irukandji" syndrome. Med. J. Aust., Vol. 167, No. 11-12, pp 650.

Case Report.
A 7 yr old boy developed Irukandji syndrome after a jellyfish sting in tropical waters off Cairns, Queensland, Australia. He was admitted to hospital with severe systemic symptoms of Irukandji syndrome including generalized muscle cramps, anxiety and sweating. 10 hours later he was still confused; disorientated; tachycardic and tachypnoeic. He also had bilateral crepitations (more marked on (L) ) heard on auscultation and a chest Xray showed increased interstitial markings consistent with pulmonary oedema. He had ++++glycosuria, Dextrostix 130 mg./ 100 ml (80 - 12 mg./ 100 ml.), periodic auditory and visual hallucinations, was pale and in obvious respiratory distress requiring oxygen (6L/ min) to maintain central colour. Examination of his fundi showed blurring of disc margins.

Initial management: Dexamethasone infusion (i.v); intermittent frusemide (i.v). His symptoms persisted through the first 24 hours. By 2nd evening he was more disorientated, unresponsive to commands. He was given 100 ml 20% mannitol (i.v) and within 2 hours had huge diuresis. 3 hours later he was answering questions with a grunt but shortly after was obeying commands and was easily rousable. 10 hours later he was talking freely and answering questions. He subsequently made a complete recovery with no neurological sequelae.

Comment: This case reports papilloedema and unconsciousness associated with Irukandji syndrome. This indicates cerebral oedema, but no cerebral scan was done to confirm this. It is suggest that a patient's level of consciousness be carefully monitored in all serious jellyfish envenomations, and that any deterioration of consciousness be appropriately investigated.

This case highlights two features in the management of patients stung by Irukandji or other apparently innocuous tropical marine animals. Firstly, people should treat minor stings immediately with liberal amounts of vinegar and avoid rubbing or washing an area with fresh water until vinegar has been applied. Secondly, patients who show signs of the Irukandji syndrome should be treated with agents to counter the alpha-adrenergic stimulation early in the course of the syndrome and then carefully observed through subsequent days for any signs of respiratory distress. If available, early Echocardiography should be performed to establish a baseline. Monitoring with central venous lines and inotropic support needs to be considered in all patients who develop dyspnoea."

Case 1.
A previously fit 48 yr old male sustained an unknown jellyfish sting to his (R) upper arm whilst bathing in deep, rough water at Mackay Harbour Beach, Queensland Australia.
He described the sting as painful and remarked seeing some "hair jellies" (Cyanea capillata) in water 15 minutes before the sting.
30 minutes post envenomation he complained of backpain for which he sought medical attention. On examination he was obviously distressed, had severe pain in his back and legs but had no visible sting marks apart from a small patch of erythema on his upper, inner (R) arm. The sting was diagnosed as being from Carukia barnesi and the patient was admitted to hospital.

He was given pethidine 100 mg (i.m), metoclopramide 10 mg (i.v). His pulse was 64 b.p.m, blood pressure 145/ 100 mm.Hg and respiration rate 24/ min. Within 2 hours, the patient was rolling around in bed with severe pain. He was given diazepam (i.v) and settled for further 30 minutes before severe pains recurred. He described severe back and leg pains, coming in waves, building up slowly over several minutes to a maximal peak where they remained for up to 15 min before fading, only to return again 5 min later.
3 hours 30 min post envenomation it was decided to try box jellyfish antivenom. The patient's pulse was 76 b.p.m, blood pressure 180/100 mm.Hg, respiration's 24/ min when 20,000 units in 2.02 ml (1 ampoule) of antivenom in 10 ml. normal saline was administered i.v. Within a couple of minutes his vital signs remained as before however pains had disappeared completely. The patient remained pain-free for 30 minutes before the waves of pain returned as severe as before. A further 20,000 units antivenom was administered (i.v) with the same outcome. He was given pethidine 100 mg(i.v) which helped settle the pain until it finally faded away some 5 hours 30 minutes post envenomation.

Case 2.
A 34 yr old male sustained an unknown jellyfish sting to (L) neck and shoulder whilst installing a boat propeller, at Shute Harbour, north Queensland Australia. The sting site showed erythema but no visible marks. Immediate pain at site was soon followed by increasing backache, abdominal pain, chest "tightness" and leg pains. Pain appeared to come in waves and the patient vomited 3 times. The patient became increasingly distressed and pethidine 200 mg, diazepam 10 mg, Box jellyfish antivenom (3 ampoules) i.v was administered leading to an apparent improvement in the patient. he was transferred to hospital where on examination he was thrashing around in severe pain, showed no apparent skin welts, had a pulse 92 b.p.m, blood pressure 200/ 120 mm.Hg, respiration rate 24/ minute and generalized abdominal rigidity. He was given promethazine 25 mg (i.v), adrenaline 1:1000, 0.25 mg (s.c) and box jellyfish antivenom (1 ampoule) (i.v). He developed unifocal ventricular ectopic beats which persisted for 5 minutes then settled spontaneously. Severe pain persisted despite further pethidine 70 mg (i.v). He was given morphine 8 mg (i.v) and commenced on a morphine infusion which provided some pain relief 2 hours and 30 minutes post envenomation. By the next day he had short runs of idioventricular rhythm and developed a chest infection ascribed to inhalation. This probably occurred during a period of impaired conscious state during the previous night of morphine infusion and settled with antibiotic therapy.

Preliminary studies into jellyfish venom characterization are beginning to suggest that the venoms of different jellyfish species may contain similar antigenic components, however it is tenuous to postulate that such similarities imply antivenom cross-reactivity among jellyfish stings by different species. This case report highlights a case against the use of CSL's specific box jellyfish antivenom for the treatment of "Irukandji syndrome" pending further research. It would be unwise to use Chironex antivenom for any jellyfish sting, other than those by either Chironex fleckeri or Chiropsalmus quadrigatus.

## Clinical Effects

Most cases will survive. Early development of severe hypertension or cardiac dysfunction and pulmonary oedema are suggestive of severe envenoming, with lethal potential.

Irukandji stings are now recognised as potentially able to cause severe or fatal envenoming and are the result of envenoming by a range of jellyfish species, not just Carukia barnesi (eg. Malo spp., Carybdea spp. and others as well). There are clear reports of cases from right across the northern Australian waters, from Broome in the west, through Darwin, to northern Queensland waters. Several species of these carybdeid cubozoans (which are distinct from chirodropid cubozoans such as Chironex fleckeri) are now associated with "Irukandji Syndrome" type envenoming. Most are yet to be clearly identified taxonomically. With the exception of Carukia barnesi, there is no significant published venom research on these species. What is clear is that the clinical syndrome is distinctively different from other jellyfish stings and is associated with a "catecholamine storm" picture.

The epidemiology of irukandji envenoming is not fully ellucidated. The rate of stings may be increasing in northern Australian waters, possibly due, in part, to increasing tourist numbers, therefore increased exposure. Unlike the box jellyfish, where stings predominantly occur in the summer close to beaches and are prevented by nets, irukandji stings can occur at any time of year, though still predominantly in summer (especially December>January>February>March), can occur out to sea, around reefs, as well as close to shore, along beaches, and jellyfish exclusion nets are ineffective because some of the irukandji jellyfish are very small (63% of cases occurred inside stinger safety net areas in one series). Stings are most likely on hot dry days, after a period of dry days, with low wind speed, predominantly from the NNW to NNE (coastal Queensland observations).

A typical sting is initially a minor event that may not even be noticed. The area of contact need only be very small and stings occur from the bell as well as tentacles. It is possible to receive a major sting on the hand or part of the face, therefore even divers in wetsuits with gloves can be envenomed, as there is nearly always some exposed skin around the mask. Stinger suits, while certainly reducing the area available for contact, also cannot be relied on to prevent all significant stings.

The stung area may show a series of contact marks. This area may be only a few cm in diameter. Typically there is some local discomfort and an appearance characterised as "goosebumps". Immediately following the sting, there may be no significant symptoms, thus even if noticed, the sting may be thought to be inconsequential. After a period of time, generally 20-40 mins, the symptoms of systemic envenoming occur. These can be rapidly severe and potentially lethal. In one series, 92% of cases developed systemic envenoming. The victim develops severe muscle and back pains, abdominal cramps, nausea & vomiting and is generally very distressed. There may also be profuse sweating and coughing. The occurrence of systemic effects in one series of 62 cases was; hypertension (50%), abdominal cramps (40%), back pain (39%), nausea & vomiting (39%), limb cramps (34%), chest tightness (26%), marked distress (24%). In more severe cases there may be cardiac dysfunction, cardiac failure and pulmonary oedema. These effects are considered a consequence of a catecholamine storm effect, caused by the venom. The cardiac dysfunction and pulmonary oedema is incompletely understood, but is probably a combination of "leaky alveolar capillary membranes, focal myocarditis and occasionally cardiomyopathy" (Mulcahy & Little, 1997).

Though a number of cases with severe pulmonary oedema have been reported, until recently there were no confirmed deaths attributable to irukandji stings. In the summer of 2002, this changed, with 2 deaths in adult males, both with cerebral haemorrhages associated with severe hypertension, seen soon after envenoming developed. In one of these cases the patient was concurrently on Wafarin following a DVT and this may have been a factor in the haemorrhage, but this cannot be invoked for the other case.

There is a possibility that, as more severe cases are documented, together with identification of the responsible jellyfish, that several distinct subtypes of irukandji syndrome may emerge.

### Special Risk Groups
#### Children

Children are at potentially greater risk than adults, because of smaller size, therefore higher venom concentrations.

#### Pregnancy

There is no evidence that venom will directly affect the foetus, but it may be harmed should severe envenoming occur in the mother.

#### Elderly

The elderly or infirm are at greater risk of either primary envenoming or secondary effects such as myocardial ischaemia. Patients on beta blockers may be at greater risk in managing severe envenoming.

Principal effects are those associated with a catecholamine storm. Victims develop severe back and muscle pain about 20-40 mins after the sting, with abdominal cramps, nausea & vomiting, profuse sweating, marked distress, and in more severe cases can develop cardiac dysfunction, cardiac failure and pulmonary oedema. The latter can be severe. Hypertension is common and may be severe. There are at least two deaths due to intracranial haemorrhages in association with severe hypertension.

## Diagnosis

Minor to moderate sting, delayed development of severe back, muscle, abdominal pain, hypertension, sweating, nausea & vomiting, ± pulmonary oedema & cardiac dysfunction/failure.

## First Aid

Wash stung area with water to inactivate unfired stinging cells, remove adherrent tentacles, apply a cold pack.

First aid for "Irukandji" envenomation

1. As the initial sting may be minor, it may not be noticed, therefore the first indication of the sting may be onset of systemic envenoming, at which point, local first aid may be ineffective.
1. Remove the victim from the water.
1. If the sting is noticed, douse the area with vinegar, then apply a cold pack. It should be noted that although this treatment is recommended by some authorities, it has not yet been proven as effective.
1. If there is developing muscle or back pain, urgent transport to a medical facility is required. Oral analgesics are unlikely to be effective, but if available, Entonox (nitrous oxide) may be beneficial.
1. Do not rub the sting area with sand or other substance. Do not cut, suck or incise the area. Do not use a compressive bandage or tourniquet.

## Medical Treatment

Stings may cause severe, even lethal envenoming after an initially minor sting. Admit all cases. Apply vinegar soaked combines to sting area. If systemic envenoming present, treat pain with analgesia (try Fentanyl), nausea/vomiting with promethazine. If pulmonary oedema develops, consider adrenaline & CPAP. Look for and treat hypertension, but avoid long acting drugs and beta blockers.

Irukandji stings are potentially severe, even lethal. They require urgent treatment. All definite or suspected cases should be observed for at least 6 hrs after cessation of symptoms, as late re-envenoming cannot be excluded.

All cases should have still active nematocysts left on the skin inactivated. This is best achieved by applying a vinegar soaked combine for at least 1 minute. To assist in identifying the jellyfish, where this is potentially possible, it may be adviseable to first apply a piece of sticky tape (Selloptape or similar) to the sting area to pick up nematocysts. In places with access to expert marine biologists, an early call to the expert may be worthwhile, as it may enable early identification of the culprit species (in Cairns, ring Dr. Jamie Seymour, Cook University).

If there are systemic symptoms present, early control of pain, nausea and hypertension is important. Fentanyl has been suggested as valuable in controlling irukandji pain (IV, 0.5 µg/kg, given every 10 mins until pain controlled). Promethazine has been suggested for control of nausea after irukandji stings. Control of hypertension may be achieved just by controlling pain. If not, further treatment may be required. Choice of medication is unclear, but beta blockers should be avoided.

If there is a good response to analgesia control, observe the patient for at least 6 hrs before considering discharge. In patients with severe pain, poorly controlled, there is a greater risk of cardiac dysfunction, failure or pulmonary oedema. Adrenaline has been used successfully in treating cardiac dysfunction and pulmonary oedema after irukandji stings. CPAP may be useful for pulmonary oedema in this setting, or intubation & ventilation may be needed.

Unlike box jellyfish stings, there is no likelihood of local tissue injury at the sting site.

In the two known fatal cases, early severe hypertension occurred, with fatal intracranial haemorrhage. It is therefore clear that early severe hypertension requires urgent treatment.

There is no antivenom for irukandji stings. CSL Box Jellyfish Antivenom has been used in a few cases of irukandji sting. It gave good short term relief of pain, but after 30 mins, the pain returned as severe as before. It is not clear from this very limited experience if it had any effect against cardiac dysfunction or pulmonary oedema. Experimental studies have shown that there are venom antigens in Carulia barnesi irukandji venom that are recognised by box jellyfish antivenom. It is not known if these antigens are of clinical significance in human envenoming. Therefore, at this point in time, there is no good supporting evidence for using box jellyfish antivenom in human cases of irukandji envenoming. It is possible future studies will change this view.
