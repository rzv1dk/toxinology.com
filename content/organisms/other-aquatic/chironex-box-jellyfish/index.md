## Antivenom

### Antivenom Therapy

Antivenom is only part of the treatment for systemic envenoming. Its role in managing severe cardiac and respiratory effects of box jellyfish envenoming is uncertain and controversial. Multiple doses may be required IV.

### Antivenom Dosage

Refer to dosage schedule for relevant antivenoms listed on this site

### Antivenom Reactions

Full range possible; see relevant section in antivenom pages

### Adverse Antivenom Reaction Management

Cease antivenom, resuscitate, adrenaline, IV fluids, then cautiously restart antivenom

### Known Antivenoms

Box jellyfish antivenom

#### Description

Salt precipitated. Liquid final product 1.63 to 3.74 ml. 20000 units / ampoule. 1 unit neutralizes 0.01 mg venom in vitro.

#### Comments

There is recent evidence that Box Jellyfish Antivenom does successfully neutralise the lethal jellyfish cardiotoxins, but that these toxins enter the circulation so quickly and exert their effect very quickly so that it may be difficult to give antivenom in time to prevent cardiotoxicity. This is not a reason to avoid antivenom use, but does indicate some limitations to effectiveness in rare very severe cases with cardiac dysrhythmia or arrest.

## Clinical Effects

The size of the patient, previous ill health (particularly cardiac disease) and total area of tentacle contact are the major factors determining likely severity of envenoming. If the area of contact is greater than half of a lower limb in extent, then potentially lethal envenoming is possible. This means small children are at greater relative risk. Any case with early collapse and cardiac and/or respiratory effects has sustained a severe sting and such cases are potentially lethal. These major systemic effects are early phenomena. They are not likely to develop for the first time hours after the sting. In general, therefore, patients who have not had significant cardiac or respiratory problems in the first hour probably have not had a potentially life threatening sting.

The Australian box jellyfish, Chironex fleckeri, is almost certainly the most lethal jellyfish in the world. It has killed numerous people in northern Australian waters and probably a number in coastal waters north of Australia, especially around Borneo. Most of the literature on this jellyfish suggests that most stings are severe, even life threatening, requiring urgent, heroic treatment. More recently, research has suggested this is an overly pessimistic view. Figures for antivenom useage in Australia support the relative rarity of severe stings. Work from Darwin indicates that most confirmed box jellyfish stings are not severe and only a minority will require major treatment, including antivenom. This epidemiologic information should not, however, cause clinical complacency. Stings by these jellyfish can be very severe and potentially lethal, particularly in small children, but also in adults.

The sting causes moderate to severe local pain and a typical "ladder" pattern of tentacle contact. It is likely that at least some discharging nematocysts will inject a portion of their venom into dermal capillaries, resulting in rapid systemic envenoming. It follows that the length of tentacle contact is important in estimating the likely severity of the sting. In general it is accepted that a sting involving an area greater than one half of one limb (usually the leg) is potentially lethal. For a given jellyfish size, it is more likely that such an extensive area of contact will occur in a small child than in an adult.

It appears that severity of pain may suggest potential severity of envenoming. In cases with fatal or near fatal stings, pain has generally been reported as very severe.

In addition to the local pain caused by the sting, the victim may develop systemic envenoming and in severe cases, may do so very rapidly, within minutes. In the most severe stings there is a cardiac arrhythmia or cardiac arrest. In slightly less severe cases, without such major cardiotoxic effects, there may be rapid development of respiratory failure. These effects are usually short lived, but in the acute stage, almost always initiating pre-hospital, can be rapidly lethal. Further, the sting contact areas may become necrotic and infected, resulting in long term scars.

It is these severe, sometimes lethal cases that predominate in the literature. A recent prospective review of confirmed stings in Darwin indicated that most cases do not develop these major systemic effects, but can develop skin erruptions locally along sting tracks, about 5 days post-sting. These lesions are painless, pruritic, consistent with a delayed hypersensitivity reaction (type IV) and occur in at least 50% of all cases of box jellyfish sting. Long term scars do not occur in these cases.

### Special Risk Groups
#### Children

Children are at potentially greater risk than adults, because of smaller size, therefore higher venom concentrations.

#### Pregnancy

There are still too few cases to determine if direct venom effects on the foetus are possible, but clearly the foetus is at risk in cases where severe envenoming has placed the mother at risk.

#### Elderly

The elderly or infirm are at greater risk of either primary envenoming or secondary effects such as myocardial ischaemia.

### Specific Effects Relating to Body Systems or Venom Types
#### Cardiotoxicity

Cardiotoxicity is a principal feature of severe envenoming. There may be cardiac arrhythmias or cardiac arrest.

#### Coagulopathy

Coagulopathy does not occur, except as a secondary complication of prolonged cardiopulmonary failure. Though the venom is haemolytic, haemolysis does not occur in human victims, at least to any clinically significant extent.

#### System Effects

The principal recorded systemic effects, other than those secondary to pain, are cardiorespiratory (see below).

#### Local Effects

Moderate to very severe local pain; local erythema and sometimes blistering along tentacle contact track, in "ladder" like formation.

#### Necrosis

Local necrosis along the tracks of tentacle contact can occur, but generally only in severe envenoming.

#### Myotoxicity

Myotoxicity is not a feature of box jellyfish envenoming.

#### Neurotoxic Paralysis

True neurotoxicity probably does not occur, but respiratory failure can occur in the acute phase in severe stings. The mechanism involved is not clear.

#### Renal Damage

Renal damage can occur secondary to cardiopulmonary failure.

#### Untreated Lethality

Uncertain, but clearly lethal if extensive stings.

## First Aid

Wash stung area with vinegar to inactivate unfired stinging cells, remove adherrent tentacles, apply a cold pack.

First aid for Australian box-jellyfish or sea wasp (Chironex fleckeri) envenoming

1. Remove the victim from the water, at all times being aware of the potential for further stings, both to the victim and rescuers. If possible wear protective clothing, such as stinger suits or pantyhose. Be careful not to touch undischarged tentacles on the victim with bare skin, as this may result in the rescuer also being stung.
1. Immediately douse the entire stung area with copious quantities of vinegar for at least 30 seconds.
1. Assess vital functions, especially airway, breathing and circulation. Apply respiratory and cardiac support, as appropriate. If there is cardiac arrest, commence full resuscitation in an optimistic fashion and continue until medical care is reached.
1. If there is major envenoming with cardiac complications or arrest or respiratory complications, and CSL Box Jellyfish Antivenom is available at the beach, give 3 ampoules intramuscularly (providing those administering are trained to do so). If suitably trained personnel are available to give antivenom IV, then this is preferable to IM.
1. If available, give oxygen. Entonox (nitrous oxide), if available, may be used for pain.
1. Urgently transport the victim to medical care.

## Medical Treatment

Box jellyfish stings can cause severe, even fatal envenoming. Massive envenoming can occur early. First aid using vinegar to deactivate tentacles and cold packs for pain relief are essential. If cardiopulmonary failure occurs, optimistic and prolonged cardiopulmonary resuscitation is required. The role of IM antivenom given pre-hospital is uncertain. IV antivenom is preferred and should be used in all cases with systemic envenoming. Antivenom is reserved for such cases; those with local envenoming only usually respond to analgesia and cold packs.

Most cases of box jellyfish sting will be minor and will require only local treatment and analgesia. Severe stings will generally be obviously major at an early stage and may require full resuscitation for survival.

Every case should have unfired nematocysts on the skin inactivated by dousing with copious amounts of vinegar. Do not apply a pressure immobilisation bandage. Try and preserve nematocysts for future identification, by applying sticky tape (Sellotape or similar) to the skin over the sting area. Apply a cold pack to relieve both pain and swelling.

For less severe cases, use oral or parenteral analgesia. In more severe cases, consider use of IV CSL Box Jellyfish Antivenom.

There is no clearly proven treatment for cardiorespiratory failure in severe box jellyfish envenoming. IV antivenom is of uncertain benefit but should be tried. Verapamil, promoted by some, but decried by others, following animal studies, is best avoided, in the absence of clear safety and efficacy data.

### Management in Detail
#### Immediate Effects Management

Establish IV line, IV fluids, secure airway, maintain respiration if imperilled, treat hypotension (if present), douse wound with vinegar

#### Approach to Management

All cases should be treated as urgent & potentially lethal. Rapid assessment & commencement of treatment including appropriate antivenom (if indicated & available) is mandatory. Admit all cases.

#### Other Issues in Treatment

The major risk with box jellyfish stings is the early development of cardiorespiratory failure. Cardiac arrhythmias or arrest may develop within minutes of the sting. Only early and effective first aid (cardiorespiratory support) may save the patients life. It is vital that such support is commenced immediately once required, and maintained until adequate antivenom and other treatments can be commenced and a response determined. Only after a prolonged period of support, without any response from the patient (ie no spontaneous cardiac function) should a decision be made to cease treatment. The role of antivenom in managing such cases is most unclear, but if available, it certainly should be used IV.

Cardiotoxins

Nematocyst toxin : Mol. Wt = 20,000 This toxin caused brachycardia followed by cell lysis in chick embryonic cardiocytes ( concentrations exceeding 1.7 ~mg protein / ml ) affects ion permeability in lipid bilayer membranes. The toxin appears to aggregate easily forming larger weight molecular proteins. Olson et al. (1984) ( Ref : R001163 ).

### Myotoxins

Toxin 1 : Mol. Wt. approx 600,000 ( subunits of 18 kDa ). Toxin 1 is a nematocyst venom ( released from isolated microbasic mastigophores ) myotoxin lethal to mice by iv injection ( 20 µl caused fatality within 3 minutes ) but showed no activity against barnacle musculature. Toxin 1 induced contractures in rat diaphragm preparation and guinea pig ileum, vas deferens and atria preparations. Toxin 1 was shown to be labile when released from nematocysts, losing all myotoxic activity within 3 days at 5°C. Endean (1987) ( Ref : R001160 ).

Toxin 2 : Mol. Wt. approx 150,000 ( subunits of 18 kDa ). Toxin 2 is a nematocyst venom ( released from isolated microbasic mastigophores ) myotoxin lethal to mice by iv injection ( 20 µl caused fatality within 3 minutes ) and showed marked activity against barnacle musculature ( 200 µl induced a marked contracture which was sustained for 24 minutes ). Toxin 2 induced contractures in rat diaphragm preparation and guinea pig ileum, vas deferens and atria preparations. Toxin 2 was shown to be labile when released from nematocysts, losing all myotoxic activity within 3 days at 5°C. Endean (1987) ( Ref : R001160 ).

### Neurotoxins Channel Toxins

Toxin 4 : Mol. Wt. approx 150,000. Toxin 4 is a non-nematocyst ( crude tentacle extract ) neurotoxin shown to be lethal to mice ( concentrations exceeding 0.8 µg protein / mouse ). Toxin 4 was also shown to block conductance in toad isolated sciatic nerve and cause a progressive loss of response to stimulation of rat diaphragm. Endean et al. ( 1993 ) ( Ref : R001161 ).

### Haematological Haemorrhagins

Toxin 3 : Mol. Wt. approx 70,000. Toxin 3 is a nematocyst venom haemolysin shown to be lethal to mice. Endean et al. ( 1993 ) ( Ref : R001161 ).
