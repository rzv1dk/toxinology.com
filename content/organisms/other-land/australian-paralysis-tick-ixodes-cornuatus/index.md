## Clinical Effects

Fatal outcome possible in untreated cases with progressive paralysis, especially in children. Beware worsening of paralysis despite removal of all ticks.

Australian paralysis ticks of the Genus Ixodes are hard bodied ticks, whose natural hosts are native animals, often bandicoots. However, feeding female ticks can attach to humans and commence feeding. Their saliva contains a potent presynaptic neurotoxin, holocyclotoxin, which can cause progressive flaccid paralysis.

These ticks are common in coastal eastern Australia, particularly Ixodes holocyclus, which is responsible for most cases of both human and animal envenoming, from North Queensland south as far as Victoria. In Victoria Ixodes cornuatus has also caused envenoming. The other Australian Ixodes species and those Ixodes species found elsewhere are not confirmed as a cause of envenoming and paralysis.

Tick paralysis in Eastern Australia is probably a common phenomenon in domestic animals, causing major problems with stock, but there are currently few reported cases of humans with significant envenoming. It is likely this reflects a low rate of envenoming rather than a low rate of tick exposure. Most episodes of human paralysis tick exposure result in only local irritation, often with an erythematous allergic response, sometimes with central minor tissue injury, but not systemic effects. Only a small proportion of episodes of tick exposure result in systemic envenoming. This is most common with larger female ticks, feeding for a prolonged period and children are at most risk.

Cases with systemic envenoming are usually not characterised with a significant local reaction to the tick, hence its presence most often goes unnoticed until paralysis occasions a serach. In this situation ticks are most likely to be found in less obvious places, particularly the scalp, in or around the ear and in body folds. In any case of tick envenoming it is essential that a thorough search is made, as more than one tick may be present.

Systemic envenoming is characterised by progressive flaccid paralysis, most often first noticed as an ataxic gait, but may progress to full respiratory paralysis. The latter is lethal unless respiratory support is provided. There are at least 20 recorded deaths in Australia from tick paralysis, which means these small, often ignored arthropods have killed more humans than the Australian funnel web spiders. Nearly all deaths have been in small children (15/20 under 5 years old), with only 2 deaths in adults.

The flaccid paralysis usually takes several days to become evident after the tick attaches. There is an associated hyporeflexia or areflexia. There is sometimes a fever. After removal of all ticks, paralysis may continue to worsen for up to 48 hours. There are occasionally other effects, including acute myocarditis and acute myolysis, both seeming to occur only after removal of the tick. Their causation remains uncertain.

### Special Risk Groups
#### Children

Risk to children is similar to risk for adults, but childs smaller body mass implies comparitive venom concentration will be higher, so more severe envenoming likely, the increased risk being inversely proportional to the child's size. Most fatal cases have involved young children under 5 years old.

#### Pregnancy

Potential risk to foetus from systemic envenoming, similar to risk to mother

#### Elderly

The higher incidence of pre-existing diseases in the elderly and associated frailty place them at higher risk. Increased risk will largely be determined by nature and extent of pre-existing disease and by any medications used to treat such disease.

## First Aid

First aid for Tick bites (paralysis ticks)

1. The primary purpose of first aid in envenoming is to prevent the systemic spread of venom from the site of inoculation, and limit any deleterious local effects of envenoming. In tick paralysis, neither of these two objectives is likely to be met by first aid, as by the time there is clinical evidence of envenoming the salivary venom has already attained widespread body distribution and the local effects are, in comparison, minor. Hence prevention of envenoming by avoidance of bites, and regular body searches while at risk, to expeditiously remove ticks, are of more value than first aid.
1. In the case of a person, usually a child, developing the early symptoms and signs of paralysis, first aid should be directed towards getting the child to medical care quickly, and maintenance of vital functions, if imperilled. Particularly watch for developing bulbar and respiratory paralysis, keep the patient fasted, and nurse on the side to avoid the chance of aspiration of vomitus.
1. If there is any impairment of vital functions, such as problems with respiration, airway, circulation, heart function, these must be supported as a priority. In particular, for bites causing flaccid paralysis, including respiratory paralysis, both airway and respiration may be impaired, requiring urgent and prolonged treatment, which may include the mouth to mask (mouth to mouth) technique of expired air transfer. Seek urgent medical attention.
1. Do not use Tourniquets, cut, suck or scarify the wound or apply chemicals or electric shock.
1. If there is any evidence of developing paralysis, avoid peroral intake, absolutely no alcohol. No sedatives outside hospital. If there will be considerable delay before reaching medical aid, measured in several hours to days, then give clear fluids by mouth to prevent dehydration.
1. If there is any evidence of significant envenoming, such as difficulty walking or signs of paralysis, the patient should be transported as quickly and as passively as possible to the nearest place where they can be seen by a medically-trained person (health station, dispensary, clinic or hospital).
1. If the tick is present, DO NOT ATTEMPT to kill it. Avoid pressing the tick's body as more venom might be discharged. If a fine pair of forceps (tweezers) are available, remove the tick by holding the proboscis (mouth-parts) and gently pulling. Usually the mouth-parts come away intact and there will be no inflammation, but it is sometimes necessary for a medical practitioner to cut the embedded mouth-parts out of a small portion of skin.

## Medical Treatment

Australian paralysis ticks can cause progressive flaccid paralysis requiring respiratory support for a period, if full respiratory paralysis. All ticks should be removed. Check cryptic areas especially, such as scalp and in ears. Paralysis may worsen for up to 48 hrs after all ticks removed. No antivenom is available.

Most Australian paralysis tick bites cause no more than local irritation, resulting in their early removal, before major systemic effects develop. Cases developing paralysis are usually associated with lack of detected local irritation, so that the tick has time to successfully commence feeding and inject toxic saliva. While paralytic effects can occur after attachment for only a few hours, this is rare; most cases developing paralysis have had a tick in situ for several days. Children are at most risk.

The most common presentation is a child with ataxic gait and general malaise. Careful examination will often discover other features of developing flaccid paralysis, such as ptosis and hyporeflexia. Without removal of the tick and treatment, the paralysis can progress to full respiratory paralysis, requiring mechanical ventilation for survival.

If respiration is already compromised, first treatment priority is to secure the airway and assist ventilation, before seeking to remove all ticks. In all other cases, the first priority is to search for ticks and remove them. Ticks are usually located in cryptic areas in cases with developing paralysis. Particularly search the scalp, in and around the ears and in all body fold areas and the groin. The tick may look like a local lesion, if embeded, not obviously like a tick, as the abdomen may be swollen and the legs and head not immediately visible, hidden under the body.

Consensus suggest that exposing the tick to chemicals, petroleum jelly, etc. is unlikely to facilitate tick removal and probably will cause the tick to release more paralytic toxins or bacteria (eg. Borrelia , Rickettsia). The tick must be prised off, using forceps placed either side of the mouth parts to pull it off. Do not hold the tick by its body to pull it off, as this may squeeze in more toxic saliva and result in mouth parts being left behind. Do not assume there is only one tick; always search all areas, even after a first tick has been located and removed.

After the tick is removed, saliva already injected continues to be toxic for up to 48 hours, therefore the paralysis may continue to worsen during this period, before starting to improve. If worsening continues after 48 hrs, then consider the possibility some ticks have been missed and conduct another thorough search.

The tick neurotoxin is presynaptic, and paralysis is the major toxic effect. It will reverse once all ticks are removed (allowing for the 48 hr period mentioned earlier), but the time for reversal is variable, sometimes up to several days. If there is major paralysis, then respiratory support in an ICU is required during this period. Full recovery of muscle power may take weeks or longer.

### Management in Detail
#### Immediate Effects Management

Establish IV line, IV fluids, secure airway, maintain respiration if imperilled, treat hypotension (if present), clean wound

#### Approach to Management

All cases should be treated as urgent & potentially lethal. Rapid assessment & commencement of supportive treatment is mandatory. Admit all cases.

#### Follow Up

Follow up cases with significant envenoming

#### Local Effects Management

Check for ticks, especially in cryptic areas such as scalp & ears, remove carefully, clean wounds. Tick removal requires forceps to lever tick out, grasping on either side of the mouth parts (hypostome); do not try and hold body of tick to pull off, as this may squeeze more toxic saliva in and may leave mouth parts behind.

#### Systemic Effects Management

Symptomatic care + IV fluids (as required)

#### Cardiotoxin Effects Management

No cardiotoxic effects likely

#### Haematologic Effects Management

Haematologic effects not likely

#### Haematologic Other Effects Management

Not applicable

#### Neurotoxic Excitatory Effects Management

#### Neurotoxic Paralytic Effects Management

Support respiration, protect airway, intubate & ventillate if required. Be aware paralysis may worsen for up to 48 hrs post tick removal. Worsening after that time may indicate not all ticks have been removed.
