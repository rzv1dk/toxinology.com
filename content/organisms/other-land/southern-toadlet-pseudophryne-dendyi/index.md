## Clinical Effects

On current evidence, it appears unlikely that this species would cause significant adverse effects in humans. However, it cannot presently be stated with certainty that this species will be harmless. There is clinical experience of frog/toad skin poisoning of humans covering only a very limited range of species, mostly Bufo toads, with few case reports and no clinical trial treatments. The following clinical description is therefore based on limited evidence from other species and should be used as a guide only, not as definitive information and its applicability to this species is uncertain.

For Bufo toad species, the major risk appears to be ingestion of poison, either by mouthing or licking the toad skin (general restricted to children), or consumption of products containing toad skin toxins (notably herbal products, especially Chinese aphrodisiacs; "chan su", "kyushin" and related products such as "Rock Hard" and "Love Stone"). The latter may not document their toad toxin content. Following exposure to the toxin the patient may rapidly commence repeated vomiting, often associated with abdominal pain, and sometimes diarrhoea and/or collapse. There is potentially significant fluid loss from the gut. In an hour or more, sometimes sooner, the cardiotoxic effects of the Bufotoxins will become apparent, with tachy- or bradycardia, hypotension, then cardiac arrhythmias, which can be associated with hyperkalaemia. Death may rapidly ensue. The bufotoxins act as cardiac glycosides, similar to digoxin, thus polyclonal digoxin immunoassay (but not monoclonal digoxin assay) can show significant plasma "digoxin" levels. Further, limited experience using anti-digoxin Fab (Digibind) suggests this therapy may be an effective antidote to the bufotoxins.

For poison arrow frogs, again the major risk appears to be ingestion of poison, either by mouthing or licking the frog skin (general restricted to children), although use as an injected poison (suicide or homicide) cannot be excluded. Clinical effects in humans are not documented for these toxins, but based on experimental evidence, both neurotoxicity (flaccid and/or spastic paralysis) and cardiotoxicity may be possible. Based on the use of these toxins on poison darts, it is possible that poisoning could be rapid, severe and potentially fatal.

### Coloration & Markings

Dorsal surface and flank colour is dark brown to blackish brown usually with a bright yellow interocular bar, a bright yellow anal blotch ( or blotches ), a bright yellow blotch at the base of teach arm and with or without small bright yellow spots or dots scattered on the dorsum, flanks, arms and legs. The remainder of the arms, legs, fingers and toes is similar to the dorsal surface ground colour. Ventral surface and throat is marbled or mottled black and white.

### Breeding

Hatching takes place after sufficient rains. Metamorphosis varies between about 12 to 30 weeks.

## Diagnosis

The diagnosis of poisoning by ingestion of frog/toad skin secretions may be obvious or obscure. If there is a history of placing either a whole animal or portions of it, especially skin, in the patient's mouth or licking a frog/toad, followed by symptoms or signs of poisoning, the diagnosis is obvious. Many frog/toad skin secretions are bitter and very unpleasant in taste. The pattern of symptoms will vary, depending on the toxins involved, determined by the species of frog/toad. Symptoms and signs can include one or more of; nausea, vomiting (which may be severe), diarrhoea, abdominal pain, secondary dehydration features (consequent on GIT losses), headache, collapse, convulsions (even status epilepticus), tachy- or bradycardia, hypotension, cardiac arrhythmias, cardiac arrest, flaccid (or possibly spastic) paralysis, respiratory paralysis, consequent hypoxic effects. At least for Bufo toad toxins, onset of GIT effects can be rapid, while major cardiac effects may take several hours to become evident. Time from ingestion to death is generally measured in hours.

The obvious and deliberate ingestion of frog/toad skin secretions is, however, the less common presentation; more commonly the patient will have ingested a substance, unaware of its contents or potentially lethal effects. Most case reports of human poisoning by these toxins involve consumption of herbal aphrodisiacs such as "chan su", "kyushin" and related products such as "Rock Hard" and "Love Stone" which contain Bufo toad toxins. Typically this will cause early onset of profuse vomiting, diarrhoea, abdominal pain, non-specific weakness and even collapse. The severity of GIT symptoms encourages early presentation, but perhaps because of the nature and purpose of the ingested substances, it appears victims frequently delay some hours before seeking aid. At presentation they are commonly hypotensive, tachycardic (though can be bradycardic), hyperkalaemic and may have high "digoxin" levels and cardiac arrhythmias. Cardiac abnormalities can rapidly progress to severe bradycardia, ventricular fibrillation or tachycardia, cardiac arrest and death. Measurement of digoxin levels using polyclonal digoxin immunoassays can assist in diagnosis, by demonstrating raised "digoxin" levels (in fact, Bufotoxins). Monoclonal digoxin immunoassays are not helpful as they will not detect "digoxin". It is important to also determine potassium levels serially and to perform continuous cardiac monitoring.

The presentation following ingestion of other frog skin secretions, such as poison arrow frog secretions, is much less clear, but is most likely to be a mixture of non-specific GIT symptoms, plus neurotoxic flaccid (or possibly spastic) paralysis, which may progress rapidly to respiratory paralysis. Initial paralytic effects may include ataxia and cranial nerve paresis. There may also be cardiac dysrhythmias. There is no commercial diagnostic test to identify these frog skin toxins, unlike the measurement of digoxin levels in Bufo toad skin poisoning.

## First Aid

First aid for poisonous frogs unlikely to cause significant poisoning if the toxins are innoculated or ingested

1. Skin contact:
1. Wash skin with water
1. Oral ingestion:
1. Providing there is no conscious state or other impairment that might render vomiting hazardous, through inhalation of vomitus, induction of vomiting may be useful in cases where several frogs have been ingested. Alternatively, consider absorption using activated charcoal.
1. Carefully observe for altered pulse rate or rhythm or signs of cardiac failure and support cardiac function, if possible. Specifically, if there is cardiac arrest, apply full external cardiorespiratory resuscitation. If there is flaccid or spastic paralysis, protect the airway. If there is respiratory paralysis, support respiration with expired air resuscitation.

## Medical Treatment

While poisoning following ingestion of some poisonous frogs can potentially result in severe systemic effects, in most cases focussed on the cardiovascular dysfunction or paralysis induced by the cardiotoxic and/or neurotoxic toxins in the frog skin, current evidence indicates significant poisoning is not likely following exposure to this species. The following information is therefore of doubtful applicability to this species, but is included for completeness.

For some other species of frogs/toads which produce significant amounts of potent toxins, death can occur, particularly after oral ingestion of Bufo toad toxins (as may be found in some herbal, especially Chinese aphrodisiacs or Chinese herbal teas). There are no antivenoms or direct antidotes to treat this type of poisoning. However, at least for some species, whose toxins have a digitalis type activity (eg Bufo toad toxins), it is possible to use anti-digitalis medications (digibind etc) to neutralise circulating toxins. It may also be possible to reduce circulating toxin load in severe cases by haemoperfusion. Apart from such specific measures, treatment is directed to support of cardiorespiratory function and in selected cases, use of medication to counteract cardiotoxic activity. The potential seriousness of cases should not be forgotten; fatalities can and do occur, at least with Bufo toxin ingestion. In addition to cardiorespiratory support, it may be necessary to counteract protracted vomiting and/or diarrhoea in some cases, both pharmacologically and through IV fluid replacement, where losses are high. There is no published experience with poison dart frog skin secretion poisoning. It is not likely anti-digitalis medications (digibind etc) would be useful.

For any form of frog/toad skin secretion poisoning, if presenting early or before extensive vomiting, decontamination using activated charcoal would seem a reasonable, if untested early management option. It is also important to decontaminate any area in contact with the frog/toad skin, such as hands or mouth. If there has been oral contact, irrigate the mouth with copious amounts of water, which must not be swallowed. For skin contact, thoroughly wash the area. Continuous cardiac monitoring should commence immediately, with baseline ECG.

In general, treatment for all frog/toad skin poisoning is supportive, replacing GIT fluid losses from intractable vomiting, supporting cardiac and respiratory function, correcting hyperkalaemia, if present, as no specific antidote is available. However, for Bufo toad skin poisoning, the best documented form of amphibian skin poisoning of humans, the similarity of the skin secretions to cardiac glycosides, specifically digoxin, has allowed use of anti-digoxin antibodies (eg Digibind) with some success. Indications for use of anti-digoxin Fab antibodies remain imprecise, but in cases of definite or likely poisoning by Bufo toad skin toxins, especially if high digoxin levels are known to be present, this treatment should be actively and urgently considered. In any such patient, even in the absence of measured digoxin levels, if there is either hyperkalaemia or cardiac arrhythmia present, it is appropriate to commence high-dose anti-digoxin Fab antibody treatment, starting with at least 10 vials (possibly more) of Digibind (or equivalent) and being prepared to give further doses if there is inadequate response or relapse, looking specifically for reversion of bradycardia and return to sinus rhythm. The role of haemoperfusion in severe cases, if any, has not been established. In the absence of anti-digoxin Fab antibody, lidocaine or phenytoin have been suggested as treatment of ventricular dysrhythmias, and atropine for brady-dysrhythmias. Suggested adult dose for lidocaine is 1.0 to 1.5mg/kg as an IV bolus, followed by maintenance at 1-4mg/minute. Suggested adult dose for phenytoin is 15mg/kg (up to 1g), with infusion rate not exceeding 0.5mg/kg/minute, with maintenance of 2mg/kg every 12 hours (every 8 hours in children), monitoring phenytoin levels throughout. Suggested adult dose for atropine is 0.5 to 1.0mg IV every 5 minutes, to a total dose of 0.04mg/kg (paediatric dose is 0.02mg/kg IV every 5 minutes).

For the more paralytic frog skin secretions, no clearly targeted treatment (such as Digibind) is currently available. It is therefore necessary in severe cases of poisoning to focus on respiratory support (intubation, ventilation, as indicated) and ionotrope support of blood pressure, when imperilled. In a laboratory situation, at least for batrachotoxin (principally in Phyllobates spp.), its voltage-gated sodium channel actions, which block inactivation of the channel, causing hyperstimulation of the affected cell, can be reversed by application of a different type of toxin (tetrodotoxin) targeting the same sodium channels. The applicability of this finding to treatment of human poisoning remains unknown. For the batrachotoxins at least, it is theoretically possible that a sodium channel blocker, such as Xylocaine (lignocaine) may counteract the frog skin toxin effect; this has never been tested clinically and neither dosage nor safety can be guaranteed.

#### Approach to Management

There is little published experience on treatment of poisoning by amphibian skin toxins. Most of the available literature focuses on Bufo toad toxin poisoning. In consequence, the described treatment is general for all frog/toad skin poisoning, and is based mostly on theoretical considerations, not on clear-cut practical clinical experience with such poisonings. This inevitable limitation should be borne in mind when considering applying the information.
