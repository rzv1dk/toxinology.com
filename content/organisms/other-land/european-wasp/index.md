## Clinical Effects

Most cases of wasp stings are minor only, with short lived local pain & minor swelling. Some cases may develop more extensive local swelling. This does not indicate the likely progression to anaphylaxis with subsequent stings. However, patients who have even mild systemic symptoms, such as mild bronchospasm, are potentially at risk of a more major reaction, including anaphylaxis, on subsequent exposure. These patients should be referred to a clinical immunologist/allergist for advice and possible desensitisation. They should also be given and instructed on the use of an emergency adrenaline (epinephrine) injection set, such as an Epipen.

Cases of massive multiple stings are at risk of major systemic envenoming and should be managed as an emergency. Small children are at highest risk of major or lethal envenoming.

The major stinging wasps, such as the European wasp, can sting multiple times, unlike the honey bee. The sting is similar in local effects to the honey bee, causing local pain and swelling. If large numbers of stings occur, generally a rare event, systemic toxicity can occur. This is characterised by haemolysis, DIC, renal failure and in severe cases, multiple organ failure. The number of stings required to trigger such a systemic reaction varies, being particularly dependent on the size of the patient. Small children are at greatest risk. In general, at least >50 stings would be required, though in an adult >300 stings might be needed to cause major systemic toxicity. Obviously far fewer stings are required to render the patient miserable.

It is not either the local effects or systemic toxicity from massive multiple stings which is most concerning, however, but the potential for allergic reactions in sensitized individuals. These people develop IgE in response to a sting. Subsequent stings may cause a major, even lethal type 1 allergic reaction (anaphylaxis). It appears that wasp stings are less likely to trigger this response than bee stings.

A further concern, given the habits of wasps, is the possibility of intraoral stings. Wasps may enter the mouth in food or drink (particularly drinking from soft drink cans). Stings to the pharynx may cause swelling severe enough to occlude the airway, potentially lethal.

### Special Risk Groups
#### Children

In cases of massive multiple stings, the risk to children from systemic envenoming is similar in nature to the risk for adults, but a childs smaller body mass implies that the comparitive venom concentration will be higher, so more severe envenoming is likely, the increased risk being inversely proportional to the size of the child.

#### Pregnancy

Mother and child may each be adversely affected by systemic envenoming in cases of massive multiple stings, but the risk isnot currently quantified.

#### Elderly

The higher incidence of pre-existing diseases in the elderly and associated frailty place them at higher risk should envenoming occur, as in massive multiple stings. The nature and extent of increased risk will largely be determined by the nature and extent of pre-existing disease and by any medications used to treat such disease. Pre-existing disease may also influence the severity and treatment success for sting allergy anaphylaxis. Pre-existing medications for hypertension (beta blockers, ACE inhibitors) may also adverserly affect treatment for anaphylaxis.

## First Aid

Apply a local cold pack to reduce swelling and pain.

First aid for Wasp Stings (includes hornets)

1. Apply a cold pack to the sting area.
1. If the patient develops generalised rash, difficulty breathing or becomes faint or collapses, urgent treatment is required, as this is an acute allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) to the venom. If there is any impairment of vital functions, such as problems with respiration, airway, circulation, heart function, these must be supported as a priority. In particular, both airway and respiration may be impaired, requiring urgent and prolonged treatment, which may include the mouth to mask (mouth to mouth) technique of expired air transfer. Seek urgent medical attention.
1. If the victim is already known to have an allergy to insect venom (bee or wasp), they are at risk of a major allergic reaction. At the first sign of such a reaction, if available, administer epinephrine (adrenaline, "epipen") as instructed on the medication. If the reaction includes airway constriction (asthma) and a "ventolin" puffer is available, use it. Seek urgent medical attention.
1. Stings inside the mouth may cause dangerous swelling, even in people who are not allergic to venom, and require urgent medical attention.

## Medical Treatment

Most stings will cause local pain & swelling only, not requiring medical treatment. Stings in the mouth can cause pharyngeal swelling and occlude the airway; all cases require urgent medical attention and securing of airway. Cases with major allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) require appropriate treatment, including adrenaline & resuscitation. All cases with systemic allergic reactions require assessment & advice from an immunologist allergist, with a view to prevention & training to handle future reactions, including provision of Epipen or similar.

The management of wasp stings is in most respects similar to stings by other hymenoptera, including the Honey bee. Unlike the honey bee, no sting will remain in the wound.

Most stings will be minor, requiring no more than minor local symptomatic treatment and reassurance. The application of a local cold pack releives pain and reduces swelling in most cases. In some cases the swelling is more extensive and persistent. Oral antihistamines may speed resolution of swelling in these cases. Sometimes the local reaction may be more severe, including minor ulceration and infection.

Stings in the mouth are more concerning, because pharyngeal swelling can occlude the airway, causing respiratory arrest and death. All cases with intraoral stings should receive antihistamines and should be observed in hospital until swelling is resolving. If there is already major swelling, threatening airway patency, consider urgent intubation. If airway patency is already lost, then only urgent establishment of a route of respiration will save the patients life. This may require cricothyroid needle insertion (large bore) to provide a route for air entry, while other measures (possibly tracheostomy) are arranged.

If the patient has had massive multiple stings (at least >50 stings, probably >300 stings in an adult, less in a small child) there is a risk of true systemic venom toxicity. Admit all such cases. Check carefully for evidence of systemic toxicity, looking particularly for haemolysis, DIC, renal failure and shock. Insert an IV line, maintain adequate IV fluid throughput, circulatory volume, consistent with renal function. Support renal function as required. Manage DIC and renal failure as for other causes.

The most common medical problem after a hymenopteran sting is severe allergy (anaphylaxis), requiring standard treatment, including adrenaline, IV fluid load, full resuscitation as appropriate. For cases with severe bronchospasm consider also adrenaline nebuliser or direct application to airways.

All cases with any degree of systemic reaction are at risk of a more severe reaction on subsequent exposure, therefore require review by an immunologist allergist to determine risk potential and to institute patient education and provision of emergency adrenaline (eg Epipen etc) including clear instruction on when and how to use.

### Management in Detail
#### Immediate Effects Management

Symptomatic care only for local reactions. Full support if major systemic allergic reaction, including adrenaline IM, airway & respiratory support, cardiac support, IV line & fluid load if hypotensive/shocked.

#### Approach to Management

Only cases with systemic allergic effects or cases with massive multiple stings or intraoral stings need medical input. In these case, admit & observe, treat as appropriate for the type of effect evident.

#### Follow Up

Follow up cases with significant local or systemic envenoming

#### Local Effects Management

Clean wound, local cold pack

#### Systemic Effects Management

Symptomatic care + IV fluids (as required)

#### Cardiotoxin Effects Management

No cardiotoxic effects likely

#### Haematologic Effects Management

Coagulopathy (DIC) only likely in cases with massive multiple stings causing true suystemic envenoming. Treat as for other causes of DIC secondary to intravascular haemolysis and/or renal failure.

#### Haematologic Other Effects Management

Haemolysis only likely in cases with massive multiple stings. May require transfusion if severe anaemia.

#### Important Laboratory Test

Only cases with anaphylaxis or massive multiple stings require laboratory tests for acute management; tests for haemolysis, coagulopathy, renal function. Cases with mild systemic reactions may be at risk of more severe reactions on subsequent exposure; they should be referred to an immunologist allergist for appropriate IgE testing and advice.
