## Case Studies

Tonkin M.A. and Negrine J. (1994) Wild Platypus attack in the Antipodes. A case report. J. Hand Surg., Vol. 19, No. 2, pp 162-164.

Case Report. (from Royal North Shore Hospital, Sydney, Australia)
29 yr. old female naturalist was spurred by an Ornithorhynchus anatinus whilst studying the mammal in the wild in western New South Wales. One spur entered dorsum subcutaneous tissue at (L) wrist; the other penetrated the thenar eminence. Intense pain and swelling of hand and forearm developed over some hours. Treated at local hospital with elevation, analgesics and intravenous penicillin and flucloxacillin for 24 hours followed by the same oral antibiotics plus Flagyl for a further 6 days, with gradual subsidence of pain.
Presented to hospital, 24 hours after cessation of antibiotics, with increasing pain; diffuse forearm swelling and lymphangitis. Finger and wrist motion remained normal but palpation adjacent to palmar penetration and of forearm flexor compartment caused pain. The recurrence of signs and symptoms had accompanied increased activity involving recurrent use of a pipette. Settled with re-institution of the above antibiotics, splintage and elevation. Antibiotics ceased after 24 hours as it was considered that there had never been any evidence of infection. All symptoms had abated within 2 further weeks.
Laboratory investigations undertaken whilst in hospital: liver function; creatinine kinase; red cell acetylcholinesterase; urinary catecholamines; serum magnesium; calcium; sodium; phosphate; urea; creatinine; ESR; WCC - these were all normal.

Fenner P.J., Williamson J.A. and Myers D. (1992) Platypus envenomation - a painful learning experience. Med. J. Aust., Vol. 157, pp 829-832.

Case Report.
57 yr. old war veteran, with previous experience of shrapnel wounds, presented to the surgery of one of authors (P.J.F.) with intractable pain and marked swelling of (R) hand. 4 hours previously he had been fishing at a mountain resort inland from Mackay, North Queensland when he had seen a small Ornithorhynchus anatinus resting on a log in the middle of a river. When approached it did not move,patient thought it may be "sick or injured". He picked it up by the back of the neck, in a similar way to holding a cat, and it moved very little. It was then supported underneath and placed back in the water. Suddenly, in a wheeling motion one of its rear legs came back and struck the patient on dorsum of (R) hand, causing the spur on the animal's hind leg to drive into the flesh, angling towards fingers. At the same time the spur on the other leg penetrated the radial side of the patient's (R) middle finger over middle phalanx.
There was "immediate, severe pain" The platypus was difficult to disengage from the hand and had to be pulled in the reverse direction to enable detachment. Neither wound bled to any extent.
Pain and swelling in (R) hand became worse, reaching peak intensity 90 minutes after envenomation, not easing thereafter. Patient described pain as " so bad I started to become incoherent". Even coughing caused severe increase in level of pain in hand. Patient and son tried heat treatment by application of hot towel to wound - of little benefit. Any movement of hand greatly aggravated pain. Envenomed area became hyperaesthetic; touching it or even the weight of towel caused increased pain. Pain was excruciating and aggravated during 100 km. trip over rough roads to doctor's surgery. Temporary arm sling provided little relief but prevented pain from worsening due to venous engorgement in the dependent position.
On arrival at surgery, 4 hrs. 30 min post envenoming: severe, unremitting pain; swollen (R) hand - most evident on (R) middle finger; hand colour appeared pale - no ischaemia; capillary refill normal. (R) radial pulse present - regular, at rate of 120 b.p.m; respirations - 26 / min; blood pressure - 140/ 100 mm.Hg. I/ V access obtained - 15 mg morphine given slowly. Over next 5 minutes - very little pain relief. Attempt made to place injured hand in hot water - patient unable to hang hand down below heart level without further increase in already unbearable pain. Attempt made to place hand in bag of ice while elevated, with out success - increased pain.
Further I/ V morphine 7.5 mg. given - eventually reduced pain to tolerable level. Patient remained alert; respirations - 24/ min; pulse - 100 b.p.m; blood pressure - 140/ 90 mm.Hg. ECG - no abnormalities.
Tetanus prophylaxis given prior to transfer to hospital where he arrived 5 hours 30 minutes post envenomation. Pulse - 74 b.p.m; blood pressure - 130/ 75 mm.Hg; temperature - 36.4¡C; severe pain. Commenced on morphine infusion of 1.5 mg / hour, followed by full (R) wrist block with 20 ml. plain 0.5% bupivicaine - patient obtained significant pain relief. Swabs taken from both spur wounds - patient given cephalothin sodium I/ V 1 g. every 6 hours. Hand elevated on 2 pillows.
Laboratory tests included full blood count; ESR; urea and electrolytes; renal function tests; cardiac enzymes and skeletal muscle fractions. All gave normal results except for ESR of 44 mm. in 1 hour (normal range: 2 -10 mm); total protein - 56 g/ dL (normal range: 60-80 g/ dL); serum albumin 26 g/ dL (normal range: 33-50 g/ dL). Respirations remained at 18-20/ min; pulse oximetry showed haemoglobin oxygen saturation (SpO2) of 87% breathing air. Given oxygen via nasal specs at 2L/ min. - resultant increase in SpO2 of 97%.
Vomiting and nausea started shortly after commencing morphine infusion - controlled by a single I/ V injection of 10 mg. metoclopramide. Hand remained numb and comfortable - morphine infusion reduced to 1 mg./ hour.(16 hours post envenomation); patient mildly pyrexic (37.5¡C) - no evidence of underlying infection.
Within 2 hours of reducing morphine infusion, patient complained that severe muscle and skin pain was moving "all over". Pain "started moving from (R) forearm into the upper arm, (R) side of chest and then it moved all over from the top of my head to the tips of my toes". Worsened by any skin pressure; touching any area of the body made the pain at the site "severe". Described as "feeling like a deep, painful bruise". Also started to complain of "swollen and sore throat", but apart from slight reddening of the fauces, nothing found. No (R) axillary or cervical lymphadenopathy.
Morphine infusion increased to 1.5 mg./ hour, but although this appeared to reduce (R) hand pain, it had little effect on "whole body" symptoms. Wrist block which was performed 6 hours post envenomation, wore off some 18 hours later. Reasonable analgesia of hand was maintained with morphine infusion alone - no further wrist block performed.
24 hours post envenomation: little change in appearance of (R) hand; hand and fingers remained swollen, with intact normal circulation. (R) forearm gave appearance of further swelling. Patient complained of chest pain - repeat ECG performed, showing no change. Peak flow readings normal; leg cramps responded to quinine sulphate (300 mg. by mouth twice daily). An oral dose of ibuprofen commenced to see if it would help muscle pains and hyperaesthesia - it did not and ibuprofen was withdrawn.(Patient taking ranitadine for previous duodenal ulcer).
48 hours post envenomation: hand and forearm still "tender to touch"; less hyperaesthetic. Ice and cold packs could now be placed on the area. Swelling just as marked but finger movement was possible. Dextropropoxyphene napsylate (oral) - 200 mg. every 4 hours, commenced; morphine infusion slowed to 1 mg/ hour and ceased later that night. A single 10 mg. I/ M dose required in early hours of morning when patient rolled over injured hand in bed.
3 days post envenomation: swelling decreased slightly; a little more movement in fingers; dorsum hand and fingers still very painful to touch; tenderness had moved down out of forearm in a reverse sequence to its appearance; "total body" pain had similarly subsided. Cold packs to hand now very effective as analgesics, but pain and tenderness returned as area permitted to warm. Pain was "tolerable".
6 days after admission, patient discharged with arm in sling. There was 25% normal range of movement in hand and fingers, except for middle finger which was still very swollen and had no movement.
12 days later, patient had barely 10% flexion in this finger; was still taking dextropropoxyphene and using cold packs every 4 hours for pain relief. There was sensitivity to touch of (R) lower forearm, dorsum of hand, and all fingers. There was little forearm swelling. Oral prednisone commenced.
At review, 2 weeks post envenomation, patient still complained of pain and tenderness in (R) hand. Unable to put any pressure on hand; had difficulty cutting food, dressing himself and signing cheques. Pain woke him at night if he compressed hand. He felt there was "great improvement" since starting prednisone and there was less "pressure pain" when using hand. For first time he was just able to hold weight of a light jacket in his fingers. Still unable to bear any weight (including bed sheet ) on back of hand as it irritated hairs on hand causing a strange painful sensation. At this time significant forearm wasting was observed; whole arm noticeably weaker. Circumference of dominant forearm over muscle compartment was 270 mm., compared with 285 mm. at comparable site on (L) forearm. Similarly circumference of (R) upper arm was 290 mm. compared with 310 mm. on (L) upper arm. Skin over dorsum of hand and middle finger now beginning to peel off. No localised hyperhydrosis, hypohydrosis or cyanosis observed.
1 month post envenomation: continued pain in (R) hand when any muscular activity attempted; unable make fist; approximation of thumb and fingertips now possible, but flexion of middle (envenomed finger) was less than 50%, and that of ring finger was 60%. Other fingers had 100% flexion, including previously deformed index finger. Forearm muscle mass had smaller volume than 2 weeks previously - circumference now 220 mm., despite his being (R) handed. Upper arm measurements remained unchanged. Prednisone therapy ceased after 4 weeks. Repeat blood tests showed a now normal ESR of 10 mm. in 1 hour but a leukocytosis of 14.6 X 10 exp.9/ L with neutrophilia. No clinical signs of infection, though it was difficult to exclude a tendonitis.
Serum albumin level remained slightly low at 30 g/ L.
3 months post envenomation: diminished forearm muscle wasting; some stiffness and swelling of middle finger and swelling of 4th finger.
Steadily improving hand usage was still not normal at 4 months post envenomation. (R) forearm measured 235 mm.; (R) middle and ring fingers and dorsum of hand still showed some swelling; making fist still not possible.
On examination 19 weeks post envenoming: 10% reduction in hand clenching position; normal strength of interossei; bilateral forearm strength and dimensions equal. (R) middle finger still swollen; normal strength, but ached the day after being used.

## Clinical Effects

Fatal outcome very unlikely

Stings from the male platypus hind leg spur are rare and there are few case reports. Envenoming causes local reactions, with intense pain, swelling, but not tissue injury/necrosis. Systemic effects are not likely, other than secondary effects of the intense pain. However, the pain can be resistant to analgesia, and can be long lasting, with pain on movement of the stung area persisting for days to weeks. In at least one case the ongoing pain was associated with some muscle wasting in the affected limb.

### Special Risk Groups
#### Children

While children are likely to be extremely distressed by a platypus sting, there is no evidence that they are at increased risk.

#### Pregnancy

There is no evidence that the local envenoming from platypus stings to a pregnant woman is likely to affect the foetus.

#### Elderly

There is no evidence that the elderley or infirm are at increased risk fromn platypus stings, but there is a potential for the severe pain and distress this causes to exacerbate an existing condition, such as myocardial ischaemia.

## Description

### Adult Length

0.4 m

No data

### Coloration & Markings

Dorsal surface of muzzle is dark bluish grey and dorsal surface of body, head and tail is dark brown. Laterally, there is usually a yellowish spot below each eye. Ventral surface is paler becoming greyish to yellowish grey or pale reddish brown.

### Breeding

Breeding period is during spring, July to August in northern regions and as late as October in southern regions. The period from mating to egg-laying is about 2 weeks with a further 2 weeks incubation. The young are suckled until they leave the burrow at about 4 months of age. Usually 2 ( range of 1 to 3 ) ovoid, leathery shelled eggs are laid ( 16 to 18 mm by 14 to 15 mm ). The female has no nipples. The milk is sucked from the skin or hair as it is excreted from the ducts by the sucking stimulus in the mammary region.

### Dentition

True calcified teeth are present during the embryonic and early juvenile stages of development but are replaced by horny pads by the time they reach independence. The mandible has horny serrations.

### Habits

The platypus is amphibious and secretive. It emerges from its underground burrow to feed usually during early morning or late afternoon. The front feet provide the thrust used for swimming, the hind feet are usually trailing and used mainly for balance or for decreasing speed. When submerged the ear openings and eyes are closed. The muzzle remains close to the bottom surface and is used in the mud or gravel bottoms to find food by sense of touch and move objects. Food is retained in the cheek pouches until it surfaces for feeding. The platypus needs to surface quite frequently for breathing. Often seen swimming along the surface with only the upper muzzle, and portions of the upper head and upper body exposed. The platypus digs winding burrows, typically semicircular in cross-section, 1 or 2 metres above the water's surface, 5 to 15 metres or more in length and up to about 0.5 metres in depth in banks.

### Prey

Food consists largely of bottom dwelling or surface dwelling animals and larvae including aquatic insects, crustaceans, worms, frog and insect larvae and many other small marine animals.

Sexual Dimorphism

Males are slightly larger than females. It is only the adult males which has venomous spurs on the inner side of the hind legs.

## First Aid

There is no proven first aid that is effective at reducing pain associated with platypus stings.

First aid for platypus Spur Stings

1. If in the water, leave the water.
1. Do not apply local bandages, tourniquet, cut or suck the wound or apply electric current. Neither application of heat or cold have proved effective.
1. Seek medical attention for pain relief and tetanus immunisation, if appropriate.

## Medical Treatment

Medical management of platypus stings is based on adequate pain relief, which may include regional block, analgesia sometimes being required for a prolonged period of days, less commonly several weeks.

The treatment of platypus stings is principally the management of severe local pain. In less severe cases it may be possible to control pain readilly, allowing outpatient management, but in most cases the pain will be severe and intractable, requiring IV narcotic analgesia as an inpatient. If this is ineffective, consider regional nerve block in the acute situation. However, even after initial intense pain has subsided partially, there may be ongoing local pain and associated swelling, persistent for days to several weeks, requiring ongoing analgesia. Prednisolone has been used and recommended to reduce the swelling associated with the sting, on the assumption this is a factor in ongoing pain.

### Management in Detail
#### Immediate Effects Management

Analgesia comensurate with the severity of the pain.

#### Approach to Management

The most important aspect of management is immediate and ongoing analgesia, which will usually require at least IV narcotic analgesia, but even regional block may be appropriate in severe intractable pain. Pain may persist for days, even several weeks, requiring ongoing analgesia. While unproven by clinical trial, oral steroids (prednisolone) has been used and advocated to shorten the period of ongoing pain and swelling.

## Venom

### Venom Components

- C-type Natriuretic Peptide. de Plater et al ( 1995 ) ( Ref : R000975 ), de Plater et al ( 1998 ) ( Ref : R000976 ).
- Three other major proteins have been isolated with Mol. Wts. approx 140,000 , 55,000 and 16,000. The 140 kDa protein exhibited hyaluronidase activity. The nature of the other two proteins is unknown. de Plater et al ( 1995 ) ( Ref : R000975 ).
- Defensin-like peptides ( DLP-1, DLP-2, DLP-3 and DLP-4 ). Torres et al (2000) ( Ref : R000977 ).
- Other Toxins

C-type Natriuretic Peptide : Oedematergic activity of crude venom was investigated using subcutaneous injection into rat paw and pharmacological activity was investigated using rat uterus in vitro. Venom was assayed for hyaluronidase, phospholipase and protease activities. Whole venom induced local oedema after subplantar injection and relaxed rat uterus. This activity was shown to be associated with a 4,200 Mol. Wt. natriuretic peptide ( ovCNP-39 ). This peptide has substantial homology with both human and porcine CNP's ( C-type natriuretic peptide ). ovCNP-39 produced oedema in rat paw and released histamine from rat peritoneal mast cells. de Plater et al ( 1995 ) ( Ref : R000975 ). Both N- and C-terminal synthetic peptides ovCNP-39 (1-17) and ovCNP-39 (18-39) were shown to be equipotent histamine releasers suggesting that ovCNP-39 and other natriuretic peptides do not act through conventional natriuretic peptide receptors on mast cells. de Plater et al ( 1998 ) ( Ref : R000976 ).

Defensin-like peptides ( DLP-1, DLP-2, DLP-3 and DLP-4 ). All 4 polypeptides have Mol. Wt. appox = 5,000. DLP-1 is similar to beta-defensin-12 and the sodium neurotoxin peptide ShI ( Stichodactyla helianthus neurotoxin I ). The DLP's displayed no antimicrobial, myotoxic or cell growth promoting activities. Torres et al (2000) ( Ref : R000977 ).

### Venom Activity

Ornithorhynchus anatinus venom has been fond to have potent action on putative nociceptors producing a non-specific cation current. This response was blocked by membrane-permeant Ca2+-ATPase inhibitor, thapsigargin and tyrosine and-serine-kinase inhibitor k252a. This suggests the response in dependent on calcium release from intracellular stores through activation of tyrosine or serine-threonine kinases. The venom component(s) that are responsible for this response have yet to be identified but are probably not C-type natriuretic peptide or defensin-like peptides de Plater (2001) (R001204).
