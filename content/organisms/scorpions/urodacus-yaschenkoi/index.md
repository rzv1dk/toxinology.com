## Clinical Effects

Fatal outcome very unlikely

These moderate to large Australian scorpions are infrequently reported as a cause of stings. In one prospective study (Isbister et al; 2003), none of 5 cases reported even significant local pain, let alone systemic effects, suggesting these scorpions are the least likely in Australia to cause problems for humans. However, both Southcott and Flecker report stings by Urodacus spp. causing moderate to severe local pain and in some cases associated local redness, swelling, prostration, and pyrexia, requiring 24 hours for recovery. White (pers. com.) has seen several Urodacus stings in sandy outback areas, all resulting in severe, but short lived local pain, without systemic effects.

### Coloration & Markings

Carapace is yellowish brown to orange brown sometimes with a greyish tint. Tergites are similar in colour to carapace but usually slightly darker. Metasomal segments and telson vesicle are orange brown sometimes with a greyish tint on segment V and telson vesicle. Pedipalps are yellowish brown to orange brown with darker orange brown to dark reddish brown fingers. Ventral surface and sternites are yellowish brown. legs are yellowish brown sometimes with a greyish tint on upper leg segments.

## First Aid

First aid for scorpion stings (in areas where no potentially dangerous species may be found)

1. Reassure the victim. Despite sometimes intense local pain, usually lasting less than one hour, these scorpions are not able to cause major of lethal envenoming and most stings will settle rapidly.
1. Do not apply a bandage or tourniquet, or cut, suck or incise the wound or apply electric shock. Neither the application of local heat or cold are proven as effective.
1. If the victim develops general symptoms or the pain remains severe after one hour, seek medical advice.

## Medical Treatment

Stings cause predominantly local effects of short duration, usually not requiring medical treatment. No antivenom is available or indicated.
