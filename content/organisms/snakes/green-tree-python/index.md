## Clinical Effects

Fatal outcome extremely unlikely

Boas and pythons are all non-venomous snakes, but this does not imply they are all harmless to humans. As a group, they rely on numerous sharp teeth to assist prey capture, followed by constriction to subdue prey. Depending on the size and nature of the prey, constriction can kill through suffocation or disruption, of vital internal organs, as a result of the immense pressure some of these snakes can apply in constriction. The risk to humans is therefore not from envenoming, which does not occur, but from mechanical and secondary effects of bites and constriction. While any sized boa or python is likely capable of inflicting a bite with multiple skin penetration from teeth, the probability of problems from constriction is dependent on the size of the human victim (smaller size, such as small children = higher likelihood of effectice constriction), the size of the snake (in general bigger snake = bigger risk), and the species of snake (some species are far more muscular and pose a greater risk at any given length of snake).

In practice, significant injuries from boas and pythons are almost certainly uncommon to rare, both in the wild, and probably in captivity. Fatalities caused by these snakes are exceptionally rare, and instances of people actually being eaten by large boas or pythons are very few indeed. Thus the most common problem likely to present relates to the local effects of the bite. With large numbers of long, thin, very sharp, pointed, recurved teeth at their disposal, a boa/python bite may result in numerous, sometimes deep tooth punctures, that cause both mechanical injury and innoculation of bacteria, from skin or the teeth. While local infection is a problem in only a minority of cases, it can be quite severe. Certainly local pain and swelling should be expected after any boa/python bite.

## Description

### Adult Length

0.90 m

Large in length, strongly compressed, medium bodied python ( forming conspicuous vertebral ridge ) with a strongly compressed, prehensile tail. Can grow to a maximum of about 1.91 metres. Head is relatively short, chunky and distinct from neck. Snout is broadly rounded when viewed from above and laterally. Eyes are large in size with vertically elliptical pupils. Dorsal scales are smooth without apical pits, smallest vertebrally and largest lower laterally. First dorsal scale row is enlarged and about half the width of ventrals. Cloacal spurs present in males and females. Scales without sensory pits. Dorsal scale count ( 45 to 55 ) - ( 55 to 67 ) - ( 30 to 43 ).

### Coloration & Markings

Dorsal surface body colour is bright green ( occasionally with a bluish tint ) with the lower lateral 1 to 6 scale rows yellowish. Most specimens have a few white to yellow or pale blue, irregularly scattered scales dorsally and / or a white to pale yellow or pale blue, narrow vertebral stripe or line. Head dorsum is green, sometimes with indistinct pale bluish markings ( some persistence of juvenile markings ). Head laterally is green with paler green or whitish to yellowish supralabials. Head venter and throat is white, cream or yellow. Ventral surface colour is white, cream or yellow. Subcaudal surface colour is white, cream or yellow usually with some suffusion of green that sometimes may form a median subcaudal stripe.

### Head Scales

Rostral with a pair of deep slit-like pits, rostral is high and projects posteriorly to separate internasals, single pair of well developed internasals separated by rostral and a few small granules, often 1 or 2 pairs of enlarged scales posterior to internasals, remainder of head dorsum covered with small scales and granules, nasal usually entire, numerous small scales in loreal region, about 11 to 19 circumocular scales ( excluding supralabials ), 12 to 16 supralabials ( first 2 or 3 supralabials with deep diagonal slit-like pits, usually 6th and 7th or 7th and 8th in contact with eye ), 14 to 18 infralabials ( with about 6 ( 5 to 7 ) with deep rectangular pits ( first pitted infralabial usually well anterior to eye level ) that form a ridge on the lip margin ) and mental groove bordered by and containing numerous small granular scales.

### Breeding

Oviparous with a clutch size of about 8 to 12 eggs.

### Dentition

Usually 1 ( 1 or 2 ) premaxillary teeth, about 15 to 19 maxillary teeth, 3 to 5 palatine teeth, 9 to 13 pterygoid teeth and 15 to 17 dentary teeth.

### Habits

Mainly a nocturnal and arboreal snake that tends to take refuge in trees, bush and plants at low heights ( usually up to about a metre ) during the day. Will descend to the ground in search of prey.

Juveniles

Juveniles are usually bright yellow ( occasionally orange to reddish brown or yellowish brown and rarely green ) with a series of brown or black edged, alternating, white, dorsolateral spots or vertical bars ( sometimes connected by a brown to blackish vertebral line ) with or without 3 alternating series of smaller, dark brown to blackish spots ( with or without white centres ). In reddish specimens the pale spots are usually reddish brown. Tail is black and white. Head dorsum and head laterally is similar to dorsal surface body colour with brown to black bordered, whitish spots and bars and a brown to black bordered ( above and below ), narrow, white lateral stripe extending from the nostril, through the eye to the angle of the jaw. Head venter, throat, ventral and subcaudal surface colour is white to yellow.

### Prey

Juveniles feed mainly on small lizards and adults feed mainly on small mammals and birds.

## First Aid

First aid for bites by pythons, boas and other strictly non-venomous snakes (excluding non-front-fanged colubroid snakes).

1. Beware, for a large snake, constriction of more than one person is possible; if possible get several people to assist in controlling the snake. There are no absolute methods for disengaging a constricting snake, but as a starting point, it may be useful to start by unwrapping from the tail end first, progressively unwinding the snake from the patient. Be sure to have someone control the head of the snake, grasping it firmly on the neck, immediately behind the head, but again beware, as the snake may try to throw constricting coils around the hand, arm, or even body of the person grasping the head and may attempt to lever the head out of the person's grasp, allowing the snake to bite.
1. It is possible to break teeth off during this process; these lost teeth may act as a focus for secondary infection if not removed promptly. Often, just gripping the snake behind the head, while removing any coils around the patient, may induce the snake to open its mouth and disengage from biting. This is the preferred outcome. Trying to manually open the snakes mouth to disengage it from biting is very difficult, not least because of the loose connection between the right and left sides of the lower jaw, such that each moves independently.
1. Disinfectant may be used. Any jewelry, such as rings or bangles, should be removed from the bitten limb, in case major swelling develops.
1. In this setting, early medical attention is required, to assess for such injuries.
1. It is therefore advisable, if possible, to seek early medical attention, for update of tetanus immunisation (if required) and consideration of antibiotic treatment.

## Medical Treatment

Bites by boas & pythons do not cause envenoming, but can cause local pain, swelling, secondary infection, or rarely, more extensive injury from constriction. The wound should be washed thoroughly. If infection develops, commence targeted antibiotic therapy orally, or in more severe cases, IV. No antivenom is available, nor is it needed, because these snakes are non-venomous.
