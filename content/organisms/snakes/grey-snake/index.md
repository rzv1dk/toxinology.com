## Case Studies

Sutherland SK, Tibballs J, (2001) Australian Animal Toxins, Oxford University Press.

No personal cases, but notes a fatal case in a dog, which had dark urine, pain (possibly muscle pain of myolysis) and diarrhoea, but not paralysis.

## Clinical Effects

Fatal outcome very unlikely

There are no confirmed bites by this species, but several cases of bites by the related species, H. signata, none causing major systemic envenoming, but in one case there was a mild coagulopathy, characterized by mild elevation of FDP/d-dimers, with a fibrinogen at the lower range of normal. This resolved spontaneously over several hours, without the need for antivenom and no bleeding problems occurred. The major significance of this may be its potential for confusion with envenoming by a dangerous species, particularly brown snakes (Pseudonaja spp.).

The bite site is painful, with mild to moderate swelling, sometimes lasting >24 hrs, but systemic symptoms are not reported.

### Special Risk Groups
#### Children

Risk to children from local or systemic envenoming is similar in nature to the risk for adults, but a childs smaller body mass implies that the comparative venom concentration will be higher, so more severe envenoming is likely, the increased risk being inversely proportional to the size of the child.

#### Pregnancy

Potential risk to foetus from systemic envenoming, similar to risk to mother

#### Elderly

The higher incidence of pre-existing diseases in the elderly and associated frailty place them at higher risk should envenoming occur. The nature and extent of increased risk will largely be determined by the nature and extent of pre-existing disease and by any medications used to treat such disease.

## Description

### Adult Length

0.40 m

Small in length, medium bodied snake with a moderately short tail. Can grow to a maximum of about 0.71 metres. Head is slightly distinct from body. Eyes are medium to moderately large in size with round pupils. Dorsal scales are smooth and shiny.

### Coloration & Markings

Dorsal surface body colour is olive grey or brownish grey to grey with lateral scales usually paler. Scales, particularly lateral scales, anteriorly tipped black in some specimens. Head dorsum is black in juveniles fading ( and receding ) in adulthood to a black nuchal band of 2 to 4 scale widths or disappearing completely. Ventral surface is white, off-white or cream with darker flecking. Iris is black with a thin brown rim around the pupil, widest in the arc above the pupil.

### Head Scales

The usual 9 supracephalic head shields, internasals present, loreal absent, single preocular, no suboculars, usually 2 postoculars, temporals 1 + 3 and 6 supralabials ( 3rd to 4th in contact with eye ).

### Body Scales
#### Mid Body Scales

17 to 17 (usually 17)

#### Subcaudal Scales

35 to 50

single

No additional information

#### Ventral Scales

140 to 170

#### Anal Scale (Single or Divided)

divided

### Breeding

Mating occurs in spring ( September to November ) and sometimes autumn ( March to May ). Viviparous, 4 to 16 ( usually about 10 ) young born each year during January to March.

### Dentition

Front fangs located at anterior end of maxillary bone ( proteroglyphous ), 3 to 5 maxillary teeth following the fang.

### Habits

Mainly a nocturnal and secretive terrestrial snake. Shelters under logs, in deep soil cracks and abandoned burrows during the day. Mild disposition, will only bite if handled or provoked.

Juveniles

Newborns have black heads, fading and receding with age to a brownish grey on the head with a black nuchal bar in juveniles and young adults.

### Prey

Feeds mainly on terrestrial frogs, but will eat small lizards

## First Aid

Keep victim still, apply pressure immobilisation bandage & splint, do not wash bite area, transport to medical care

First aid for bites by Elapid snakes which do not cause significant injury at the bite site (see Comments for partial listing), but which may have the potential to cause significant general (systemic) effects, such as paralysis, muscle damage, or bleeding.

1. After ensuring the patient and onlookers have moved out of range of further strikes by the snake, the bitten person should be reassured and persuaded to lie down and remain still. Many will be terrified, fearing sudden death and, in this mood, they may behave irrationally or even hysterically. The basis for reassurance is the fact that many venomous bites do not result in envenoming, the relatively slow progression to severe envenoming (hours following elapid bites, days following viper bites) and the effectiveness of modern medical treatment.
1. The bite wound should not be tampered with in any way. Wiping it once with a damp cloth to remove surface venom is unlikely to do much harm (or good) but the wound must not be massaged. For Australian snakes only, do not wash or clean the wound in any way, as this may interfere with later venom detection once in a hospital.
1. All rings or other jewellery on the bitten limb, especially on fingers, should be removed, as they may act as tourniquets if oedema develops.
1. If the bite is on a limb, a broad bandage (even torn strips of clothing or pantyhose) should be applied over the bitten area at moderate pressure (as for a sprain; not so tight circulation is impaired), then extended to cover as much of the bitten limb as possible, including fingers or toes, going over the top of clothing rather than risking excessive limb movement by removing clothing. The bitten limb should then be immobilised as effectively as possible using an extemporised splint or sling.
1. If there is any impairment of vital functions, such as problems with respiration, airway, circulation, heart function, these must be supported as a priority. In particular, for bites causing flaccid paralysis, including respiratory paralysis, both airway and respiration may be impaired, requiring urgent and prolonged treatment, which may include the mouth to mask (mouth to mouth) technique of expired air transfer. Seek urgent medical attention.
1. Do not use Tourniquets, cut, suck or scarify the wound or apply chemicals or electric shock.
1. Avoid peroral intake, absolutely no alcohol. No sedatives outside hospital. If there will be considerable delay before reaching medical aid, measured in several hours to days, then give clear fluids by mouth to prevent dehydration.
1. If the offending snake has been killed it should be brought with the patient for identification (only relevant in areas where there are more than one naturally occurring venomous snake species), but be careful to avoid touching the head, as even a dead snake can envenom. No attempt should be made to pursue the snake into the undergrowth as this will risk further bites.
1. The snakebite victim should be transported as quickly and as passively as possible to the nearest place where they can be seen by a medically-trained person (health station, dispensary, clinic or hospital). The bitten limb must not be exercised as muscular contraction will promote systemic absorption of venom. If no motor vehicle or boat is available, the patient can be carried on a stretcher or hurdle, on the pillion or crossbar of a bicycle or on someone's back.
1. Most traditional, and many of the more recently fashionable, first aid measures are useless and potentially dangerous. These include local cauterization, incision, excision, amputation, suction by mouth, vacuum pump or syringe, combined incision and suction ("venom-ex" apparatus), injection or instillation of compounds such as potassium permanganate, phenol (carbolic soap) and trypsin, application of electric shocks or ice (cryotherapy), use of traditional herbal, folk and other remedies including the ingestion of emetic plant products and parts of the snake, multiple incisions, tattooing and so on.

## Medical Treatment

These snakes generally cause only mild envenoming, but can cause mild coagulopathy, so observe all cases for at least 12hrs, assess fully, including coagulation. AV unlikely to be needed, but if required, use CSL Tiger Snake AV.

Bites by these snakes may cause minimal local effects, or local pain and swelling, rarely systemic symptoms. However, they can cause a mild coagulopathy, which may cause confusion with more dangerous species.

As a precaution, in case of unexpected severe envenoming, it is wise to admit all cases, establish an IV line, give an initial IV fluid load, monitor fluid input/output, take blood for extended coagulation studies, complete blood picture, renal function, electrolytes and CK.

It is quite unlikely that antivenom therapy will be required. There is no certainty that any antivenom will be effective, but it seems most likely that CSL Tiger Snake AV would be the closest match. However, antivenom should only be given if there is clear evidence of major envenoming, notably severe coagulopathy (defibrination) or active bleeding. The dose is uncertain, but an initial dose of 3 vials would be appropriate.

### Management in Detail
#### Immediate Effects Management

Establish IV line, IV fluids, secure airway, maintain respiration if imperiled, treat hypotension (if present), clean wound

#### Approach to Management

It is possible that most cases will be minor, but some cases may be more severe, requiring admission and treatment, so assess carefully before discharge.

#### Follow Up

Follow up all cases given antivenom therapy, checking particularly for serum sickness

#### Local Effects Management

Do not clean wound

#### Systemic Effects Management

Symptomatic care + IV fluids (as required)

#### Cardiotoxin Effects Management

No cardiotoxic effects likely

#### Haematologic Effects Management

It is doubtful antivenom is required for the mild degree of coagulopathy which may occur. Only if there is severe coagulopathy or active bleeding should antivenom be considered.
