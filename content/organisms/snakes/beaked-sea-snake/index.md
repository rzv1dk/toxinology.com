## Case Studies

Reid H.A. (1975) Epidemiology of sea-snake bites. J. Trop. Med. Hyg., Vol. 78, No. 5, pp 106-113.

Report of series of cases by diverse species of sea snakes. No detail on individual cases. Records 55 cases of Enhydrina schistosa bites, 39 with no effects, 4 with trivial effects, 5 with serious but non-fatal effects and 7 fatalities. Time to death varied from 7.5 to 59 hours, but 5 died in the first 19 hours. Of the 5 severe non-fatal cases, time to clinical recovery varied from 12-30 days (no antivenom) and 2-15 days (given antivenom).

Reid H.A. (1975) Anti-venom in sea-snake bite poisoning. Lancet, Vol. 1, pp 622-623.

Case Report.
On a mudshore of Penang, 10 yr. old Australian boy sustained bite to arm from an Enhydrina schistosa. Admitted to hospital where doctor ruled out sea-snake bite because he could not see fang marks. Boy was transferred to Penang General Hospital, 44 hours post bite, when he appeared moribund. On examination: severe myalgia; ptosis; total ophthalmoplegia; respiratory insufficiency; heavy myoglobinuria, and hyperkalaemic T waves on ECG. Total stock of sea-snake antivenom (2,000 units) infused, together with dextrose and insulin. Patient improved for a time, but after a few hours condition deteriorated. Despite intubation and artificial respiration, patient died.

Reid H.A. (1961) Myoglobinuria and sea-snake bite poisoning. BMJ, Vol. 1, pp 1284-1289.

This paper documents detail on 4 cases, 2 of which, one fatal, are listed in the article above as Enhydrina schistosa bites. The fatal case, in a 32 yr old man, is documented in considerable detail. He was bitten on his left calf whilst bathing near Penang, Malaysia in 1957. Within 30 minutes he experienced giddiness, then a stiff neck, a stiff and swollen tongue, stiff limbs and difficulty swallowing. By 2.5 hours he was hypertensive (BP 120/100; pre bite recorded at 125/68). He had limited lateral eye movement, could open and close his mouth, but had difficulty grinning. Swallowing was painful. Lateral head movement was difficult and painful. Muscles were tender. Active and passive flexion was extremely painful. Reflexes were brisk. By 3.5 hours after the bite muscle power was decreasing, pupils were dilated but still reactive and there was diplopia. Jaw opening and tongue extrusion were severely limited. He could no longer lift his head off the bed. By 6 hours after the bite he had ptosis, one pupil no longer reacted to light, any muscle movement was very painful and swallowing almost impossible. By 7 hours after the bite he vomited, was more hypertensive (160/120) and had respiratory distress. By 8 hours after the bite BP had risen (180/125), he was comatose and cyanosed. A tracheostomy was performed and IPPR commenced, after giving D-tubocurarine because he was "fighting the machine". Cyanosis rapidly disappeared and he seemed to improve. At 13 hours after the bite he became cyanosed again and pulseless, ECG showing hyperkalaemic T waves and widening of the S and ST segment. Despite continued efforts, he died shortly afterwards.

Although no laboratory test results are available, it appears this patient had severe myolysis, probably with nuerotoxic envenoming as well, but died as a result of hyperkalaemic cardiotoxicity secondary to myotoxic envenoming. No antivenom was available at this time.

Case Report.
17 yr. old Chinese male, sustained bite to )(L) lower leg whilst bathing. 45 minutes post bite: frontal headache; pains in throat, (R) shoulder and chest. 1 hr. post bite: thirst; increased sweating. 75 minutes post bite, at hospital: difficulty in raising limbs form stretcher; profuse sweating; blood pressure - 140/ 90 mm.Hg. 3 hrs. 30 min. post bite: unable to sit up unaided but could lift head against esistance; limb power objectively normal despite patient's claim he "could not move;" all limbs held flexed; passive movements avoided due to pain. On lower (L) leg, fang marks 8.5 mm. apart could be easily distinguished from 2 maxillary and 4 pterygoid teeth marks.
Day 2, ~ 15 hrs. post bite: muscle pains and tenderness more severe in neck, shoulders and thighs; thirst continued. 23 hrs. post bite: objective weakness of arm abduction; no other change in muscle power. Arm jerks absent; knee and ankle jerks present.

Day 4: shoulder, arm and thigh muscles weaker.

Week 6: lower limb power objectively normal. Arm abduction, forearm extension, and hand flexion weaker on (L) side than (R) side.
Week 10: could sit up spontaneously.
4 months post bite: press-ups normal with a control patient of his own age and build; hand grip still weaker on (L) than (R).
By 6 months - fully recovered.

## Clinical Effects

Rate of fatality unclear, due to inadequate reporting, but fatalities certainly occur. Possibly 11 fatal cases in literature. In at least one case death was the result of hyperkalaemic cardiotoxicity secondary to myotoxic envenoming. Time from bite to death in confirmed sea snake fatalities ranges from 2 hours to several days, but most deaths occurred in the first 4-18 hours.

For most species of sea snakes there are no clinical reports of envenoming, but this may only reflect the paucity of documented cases, not necessarily a paucity of actual cases. However, this species has a number of definite and probable cases reported, including 11 fatalities. It is clearly able to cause severe envenoming. In general, a significant number of sea snake bites will be "dry bites", but the rate of such trivial bites varies from species to species and for most is not documented. The larger the snake, the longer it has hung on, the more likely significant envenoming will occur. Multiple bites are also more likely to be severe. Sea snake bites are characterised by systemic rather than local envenoming. Bite marks are usually present and visible, sometimes quite extensive, involving not just fangs, but many non-fang teeth, however there is usually no major local reaction. Swelling, if present at all, is usually minor. Local necrosis, bruising and blistering do not occur. In those cases where sufficient venom has been injected to cause systemic envenoming, first symptoms and signs occur from one to 6+ hours post bite. In addition to non-specific symptoms such as headache, nausea, vomiting and abdominal pain, there may be one of two principal systemic envenoming effects. One or both may be present. The first effect is progressive post synaptic flaccid neurotoxic paralysis, usually first manifest as ptosis, then ophthalmoplegia (initially partial, with diplopia on lateral gaze), dysarthria, fixed dilated pupils, drooling, difficulty swallowing, then generalised weakness of voluntary muscles in limbs, trunk, finally affecting the diaphragm, with respiratory difficulty and eventual respiratory paralysis. Without ventilatory support this will prove lethal. The time course from bite to first signs of paralysis (usually ptosis) is variable, from 30 minutes to several hours. Similarly, progression of paralysis may occur over 2-3 hours or may take more than 24 hours. It is uncertain if this purely paralytic effect occurs with this species. The second effect is systemic myolysis, presenting first as either muscle pain or red or brown urine (myoglobinuria), first seen usually several hours after the bite, but when present, will usually be obvious within the first 8-12 hours. As muscle damage progresses there is an associated muscle weakness, which may mimic mild paralysis. CK levels are elevated, sometimes to very high levels (>100,000 IU/L) and there may be hyperkalaemia, especially if there is also secondary renal failure, a common accompaniment of severe myolysis. It is this myotoxic effect which is described for clinical cases of envenoming by this species. Coagulopathy is not a feature of sea snake envenoming.

### Special Risk Groups
#### Children

Risk to children from local or systemic envenoming is similar in nature to the risk for adults, but a childs smaller body mass implies that the comparative venom concentration will be higher, so more severe envenoming is likely, the increased risk being inversely proportional to the size of the child.

#### Pregnancy

Potential risk to foetus from systemic envenoming, similar to risk to mother

#### Elderly

The higher incidence of pre-existing diseases in the elderly and associated frailty place them at higher risk should envenoming occur. The nature and extent of increased risk will largely be determined by the nature and extent of pre-existing disease and by any medications used to treat such disease.

## Description

### Adult Length

0.80 m

Medium in length, moderately stout bodied seasnake with a moderately slender and elongate neck and forebody, with stronger built, but elongate, mid and posterior body. Tail is compressed laterally. Can grow to a maximum of about 1.58 metres. Head elongate and slightly distinct from neck with a distinctive curved snout resulting in a beaked appearance. Eyes are medium in size with round pupils. The lower jaw skin is very extensible. Nostril located on snout dorsum. Dorsal scales are imbricate and weakly keeled. Ventrals barely wider than adjacent dorsal scales. About 40 to 55 scale rows around the neck. Preanals feebly enlarged.

### Coloration & Markings

Dorsal surface body colour to mid lateral is uniform pale silvery grey to blue grey ( occasionally with an olive tint ), with 40 to 60 obscure darker bars or saddle markings which fade laterally and posteriorly toward the tail. Darker markings ( which fade with age ) are slightly wider than paler interspaces. Lower lateral dorsal surface and ventral surface is white to off-white. Head colour is mottled grey.

### Head Scales

Head shields enlarged, symmetrical and unfragmented with characteristic mental shield greatly elongated, much longer than broad, somewhat dagger-like, with posterior edge partly lying within the mental groove, rostral slightly higher than wide, undivided and extended into a beak-like appearance, frontal small, single preocular, usually single ( sometimes 2 ) postoculars, usually 1 or 2 anterior temporals, 7 to 9 supralabials ( 3rd and 4th or 4th only in contact with eye, posterior 2 or 3 are very small ), usually 11 ( 9 to 12 ) infralabials and chin shields separated from mental groove by 1st infralabial. Mental shield small and partly concealed in mental groove.

### Breeding

Viviparous. Gestation period is about 14 weeks, usually 4 to 11 ( 2 to 33 ) young measuring about 15 to 28 cm in length at birth.

### Dentition

Front fangs located at anterior end of maxillary bone ( proteroglyphous ). Average fang length about 3.7 mm. Maxillary bone not extending forward beyond palatine, with 3 to 5 maxillary teeth. 13 to 16 dentary teeth and 5 or 6 palatine teeth much larger than pterygoid teeth.

Easily angered and quite aggressive. Active by both day and night.

Juveniles

## First Aid

Keep victim still, apply pressure immobilisation bandage & splint, gently wash bite area, transport to medical care

First aid for bites by Elapid snakes which do not cause significant injury at the bite site (see Comments for partial listing), but which may have the potential to cause significant general (systemic) effects, such as paralysis, muscle damage, or bleeding.

1. After ensuring the patient and onlookers have moved out of range of further strikes by the snake, the bitten person should be reassured and persuaded to lie down and remain still. Many will be terrified, fearing sudden death and, in this mood, they may behave irrationally or even hysterically. The basis for reassurance is the fact that many venomous bites do not result in envenoming, the relatively slow progression to severe envenoming (hours following elapid bites, days following viper bites) and the effectiveness of modern medical treatment.
1. The bite wound should not be tampered with in any way. Wiping it once with a damp cloth to remove surface venom is unlikely to do much harm (or good) but the wound must not be massaged. For Australian snakes only, do not wash or clean the wound in any way, as this may interfere with later venom detection once in a hospital.
1. All rings or other jewellery on the bitten limb, especially on fingers, should be removed, as they may act as tourniquets if oedema develops.
1. If the bite is on a limb, a broad bandage (even torn strips of clothing or pantyhose) should be applied over the bitten area at moderate pressure (as for a sprain; not so tight circulation is impaired), then extended to cover as much of the bitten limb as possible, including fingers or toes, going over the top of clothing rather than risking excessive limb movement by removing clothing. The bitten limb should then be immobilised as effectively as possible using an extemporised splint or sling.
1. If there is any impairment of vital functions, such as problems with respiration, airway, circulation, heart function, these must be supported as a priority. In particular, for bites causing flaccid paralysis, including respiratory paralysis, both airway and respiration may be impaired, requiring urgent and prolonged treatment, which may include the mouth to mask (mouth to mouth) technique of expired air transfer. Seek urgent medical attention.
1. Do not use Tourniquets, cut, suck or scarify the wound or apply chemicals or electric shock.
1. Avoid peroral intake, absolutely no alcohol. No sedatives outside hospital. If there will be considerable delay before reaching medical aid, measured in several hours to days, then give clear fluids by mouth to prevent dehydration.
1. If the offending snake has been killed it should be brought with the patient for identification (only relevant in areas where there are more than one naturally occurring venomous snake species), but be careful to avoid touching the head, as even a dead snake can envenom. No attempt should be made to pursue the snake into the undergrowth as this will risk further bites.
1. The snakebite victim should be transported as quickly and as passively as possible to the nearest place where they can be seen by a medically-trained person (health station, dispensary, clinic or hospital). The bitten limb must not be exercised as muscular contraction will promote systemic absorption of venom. If no motor vehicle or boat is available, the patient can be carried on a stretcher or hurdle, on the pillion or crossbar of a bicycle or on someone's back.
1. Most traditional, and many of the more recently fashionable, first aid measures are useless and potentially dangerous. These include local cauterization, incision, excision, amputation, suction by mouth, vacuum pump or syringe, combined incision and suction ("venom-ex" apparatus), injection or instillation of compounds such as potassium permanganate, phenol (carbolic soap) and trypsin, application of electric shocks or ice (cryotherapy), use of traditional herbal, folk and other remedies including the ingestion of emetic plant products and parts of the snake, multiple incisions, tattooing and so on.

## Medical Treatment

Sea snake bites vary from trivial to lethal envenoming, so urgently assess all cases, looking especially for paralysis and/or myolysis. These usually develop in first 6 hrs. They require good hydration/renal flow, respiratory support (for respiratory paralysis), IV antivenom (CSL Sea Snake AV).

While most sea snake bites, particularly by smaller species, are likely to be minor, severe, even lethal envenoming can occur. All cases should be managed as potentially lethal, requiring urgent assessment & admission.

Insert an IV line and give an IV fluid load. Monitor fluid input/output, looking for evidence of renal failure and myolysis (red or black/brown urine, positive for haemoglobin). Ensure good renal flow. Alkalinisation of urine is advocated by some, if there is myolysis, to reduce the chance of secondary renal damage due to myoglobinuria, but this is an unproven treatment. Carefully monitor for evidence of developing paralysis or myolysis, if the patient is initially well. At the first sign of either paralysis or myolysis, consider giving IV antivenom. CSL Sea Snake AV is the preferred choice, with an initial dose of 1-3 vials, depending on degree of envenoming. In the absence of this AV, try either CSL Tiger Snake or Polyvalent AV, at a ratio of 3 vials of either for each 1 vial of Sea Snake AV that would have been used. If the patient already has established major paralysis, requiring intubation & ventilation to support respiration, higher doses of AV may be needed. Be prepared to give more than 3 vials of Sea Snake AV, if the paralysis fails to reverse with the initial dose of 3 vials. Also consider performing a tensilon test, to determine if the postsynaptic paralysis will respond to anticholinesterases. The latter may reduce, short term, the extent of paralysis, but should only be considered as an adjunct to antivenom, not as a replacement. AV remains the key treatment for envenoming.

In most cases, at least the first mild signs of envenoming will be present by 6 hrs post bite (providing no effective first aid has been used). However, because later development of envenoming is possible, it is advisable to observe all cases for a minimum of 18 hrs post bite. If initial CK levels are normal, ensure repeat testing is performed, to pick up late developing myolysis.

### Myotoxins

Myotoxin VI-5 : Isolated by Fohlmann and Eaker (1977) ( Ref : R000101 ). Myotoxin VI-5 has 119 AA residues cross-linked by 7 disulfide bridges and a Mol. Wt. 13,446. Lind and Eaker (1981) ( Ref : R000094 ). Modification of one of two His residues with p-bromophenacyl bromide results in loss of almost all enzymatic activity, lethal potency and the ability to induce myoglobinuria. Fohlmann and Eaker (1977) ( Ref : R000101 ). In guinea pigs 0.4 mg / kg caused respiratory distress in all animals and death in some. In diaphragm muscle breakdown of the myofilaments and mitochondrial cristae with invasion of phagocytic cells was observed. Geh and Toh (1978) ( Ref : R000354 ). When injected in mice the PLA2 depolarized the soleus muscle fibres and blocked both directly and indirectly evoked contractions. Transmitter release remained detectable and there was no decrease in sensitivity of the postsynaptic membrane. Lesions of the plasmalemma and disorganization of the internal structure of the muscle fibre was observed. Brooke et al (1987) ( Ref : R000355 ).
