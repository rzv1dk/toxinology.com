## Clinical Effects

Fatal outcome common in untreated severe cases

There are few records of confirmed copperhead bites in Australia, but this may give a false impression of frequency of bites. As copperhead bites may often give a positive venom detection test result as tiger snake venom, they may be frequently listed as tiger snake bites. Therefore, the following account of clinical effects should be taken as a rough guide only, as it is based on so little actual clinical information.

The rate of envenoming is unknown, but probably similar to or less than that seen for tiger snakes (50%) for the common or lowlands copperhead, The highlands copperhead, being smaller, probably has a lower rate of effective bites. So far, of more than a dozen confirmed bites by the pygmy copperhead, none have resulted in significant envenoming. This may reflect either the snakes small size or comparatively weak venom.

It is unclear if copperhead bites cause local pain and reaction, but if they are similar to tiger snakes, then local pain, mild swelling, erythema and bruising may all occur. However, the only effect in known pygmy copperhead bites was mild local pain, though no bite caused significant envenoming, so the effect of a major bite, if possible, is unknown.

Lowland copperheads are known to cause flaccid paralysis in humans, with at least one fatality. It is likely the highlands copperhead may have the same potential, but less certain for the pygmy copperhead. It is also unclear if this paralytic effect is mixed presynaptic and postsynaptic, in which case antivenom would not be expected to reverse established paralysis, or if it is pure postsynaptic paralysis, much more amenable to both antivenom and anticholinesterases. Because of this uncertainty, any copperhead bite with significant paralysis should be considered for a trial of anticholinesterase. Venom studies have shown only postsynaptic neurotoxins.

There are no reports confirming coagulopathy following any copperhead species bite and no procoagulant is present in those venoms assayed. Venom studies have suggested a possible anticoagulant effect, with prolongation of the aPTT and there are several platelet aggregation inhibitors present in lowland copperhead venom.

Myolysis has never been reported for human cases of copperhead bite, but myolysins are present in at least lowland copperhead venom.

There is no information on renal damage following copperhead bite, but it is reasonable to consider it as a potential complication, especially if there is myolysis, coagulopathy or hypotension.

#### Children

Risk to children from local or systemic envenoming is similar in nature to the risk for adults, but a childs smaller body mass implies that the comparative venom concentration will be higher, so more severe envenoming is likely, the increased risk being inversely proportional to the size of the child.

Potential risk to foetus from systemic envenoming, similar to risk to mother

#### Elderly

The higher incidence of pre-existing diseases in the elderly and associated frailty place them at higher risk should envenoming occur. The nature and extent of increased risk will largely be determined by the nature and extent of pre-existing disease and by any medications used to treat such disease.

## Description

### Adult Length

1.00 m

Large in length, moderately robust, strongly muscled, medium bodied snake with a medium length tail. Can grow to a maximum of about 1.78 metres. Head is moderately small and narrow and slightly distinct from neck. Eyes are moderately large in size with round pupils. Dorsal scales are smooth. Lower lateral scale rows ( especially the 1st dorsal scale row ) are distinctly enlarged.

### Coloration & Markings

Dorsal surface body colour is reddish brown, greyish brown, chocolate brown or blackish brown with the lower lateral surface usually a distinctive orange red fading to cream or yellow on lowermost scale rows. A darker vertebral stripe ( confined to the middle of the vertebral scale row ) and a darker or paler transverse band across the nape is distinct in some specimens. Head dorsum may be similar to dorsal surface body colour but often a distinctly different shade of reddish brown, dark brown, greyish brown or blackish brown is most common. Snout is generally paler. Head laterally is generally paler than above with weakly defined, narrow, whitish margins on labials. Eyes have a blackish brown pupil with a thick reddish brown or dark brown rim. Ventral surface colour is usually cream to grey.

### Head Scales

The usual 9 supracephalic head shields, frontal scale distinctly longer than it is broad, single nasal in contact with single nasal, no suboculars, 2 postoculars, temporals 2 + 2 with the lower usually the larger, in contact with lower postocular and wedged between the 5th and 6th supralabial, 6 supralabials ( 3rd and 4th in contact with eye ) and 8 infralabials.

### Breeding

Mating occurs in spring ( September to November ) and sometimes autumn if favourable conditions prevail. Ovoviviparous, usually 10 to 20 ( maximum of 45 ) young born in summer peak period of January to March.

### Dentition

Front fangs located at anterior end of maxillary bone ( proteroglyphous ). Fang length approximately 3.3 mm ( varies between 3.0 to 4.5 mm ). Fang is followed by 3 to 5 solid maxillary teeth.

### Habits

Terrestrial and mainly diurnal ( sometimes active at night ) and sometimes cannibalistic. Inoffensive disposition, secretive and shy. If disturbed it will escape to cover. If cornered, it will hiss loudly, flatten its body and often thrash about menacingly without attempting to bite. Takes shelter under logs, flat rocks, grass tussocks and in burrows

Juveniles

Juveniles are usually lighter in colour than adults, have a dark coloured ( blackish or blackish brown ) dorsal nape band and a dark vertebral stripe. Occasionally, one or more of these markings may persist into adulthood.

### Prey

Mainly forages for lizards ( mainly skinks ) and frogs, but will eat small mammals and birds and snakes ( including its own kind ).

Sexual Dimorphism

Males tend to be significantly larger than females.

## First Aid

Keep victim still, apply pressure immobilisation bandage & splint, do not wash bite area, transport to medical care

First aid for bites by Elapid snakes which do not cause significant injury at the bite site (see Comments for partial listing), but which may have the potential to cause significant general (systemic) effects, such as paralysis, muscle damage, or bleeding.

1. After ensuring the patient and onlookers have moved out of range of further strikes by the snake, the bitten person should be reassured and persuaded to lie down and remain still. Many will be terrified, fearing sudden death and, in this mood, they may behave irrationally or even hysterically. The basis for reassurance is the fact that many venomous bites do not result in envenoming, the relatively slow progression to severe envenoming (hours following elapid bites, days following viper bites) and the effectiveness of modern medical treatment.
1. The bite wound should not be tampered with in any way. Wiping it once with a damp cloth to remove surface venom is unlikely to do much harm (or good) but the wound must not be massaged. For Australian snakes only, do not wash or clean the wound in any way, as this may interfere with later venom detection once in a hospital.
1. All rings or other jewellery on the bitten limb, especially on fingers, should be removed, as they may act as tourniquets if oedema develops.
1. If the bite is on a limb, a broad bandage (even torn strips of clothing or pantyhose) should be applied over the bitten area at moderate pressure (as for a sprain; not so tight circulation is impaired), then extended to cover as much of the bitten limb as possible, including fingers or toes, going over the top of clothing rather than risking excessive limb movement by removing clothing. The bitten limb should then be immobilised as effectively as possible using an extemporised splint or sling.
1. If there is any impairment of vital functions, such as problems with respiration, airway, circulation, heart function, these must be supported as a priority. In particular, for bites causing flaccid paralysis, including respiratory paralysis, both airway and respiration may be impaired, requiring urgent and prolonged treatment, which may include the mouth to mask (mouth to mouth) technique of expired air transfer. Seek urgent medical attention.
1. Do not use Tourniquets, cut, suck or scarify the wound or apply chemicals or electric shock.
1. Avoid peroral intake, absolutely no alcohol. No sedatives outside hospital. If there will be considerable delay before reaching medical aid, measured in several hours to days, then give clear fluids by mouth to prevent dehydration.
1. If the offending snake has been killed it should be brought with the patient for identification (only relevant in areas where there are more than one naturally occurring venomous snake species), but be careful to avoid touching the head, as even a dead snake can envenom. No attempt should be made to pursue the snake into the undergrowth as this will risk further bites.
1. The snakebite victim should be transported as quickly and as passively as possible to the nearest place where they can be seen by a medically-trained person (health station, dispensary, clinic or hospital). The bitten limb must not be exercised as muscular contraction will promote systemic absorption of venom. If no motor vehicle or boat is available, the patient can be carried on a stretcher or hurdle, on the pillion or crossbar of a bicycle or on someone's back.
1. Most traditional, and many of the more recently fashionable, first aid measures are useless and potentially dangerous. These include local cauterization, incision, excision, amputation, suction by mouth, vacuum pump or syringe, combined incision and suction ("venom-ex" apparatus), injection or instillation of compounds such as potassium permanganate, phenol (carbolic soap) and trypsin, application of electric shocks or ice (cryotherapy), use of traditional herbal, folk and other remedies including the ingestion of emetic plant products and parts of the snake, multiple incisions, tattooing and so on.

## Medical Treatment

While some cases will prove minor, copperhead bites are potentially severe (paralysis particularly), so must be assessed urgently. Antivenom is required for systemic envenoming.

Bites by Australian copperheads are characterized by local pain, slight swelling and systemic flaccid paralysis (pre- & post-synaptic), possibly myolysis (no reports of severe myolysis), but probably not coagulopathy or secondary renal failure. Probably about half of all bites will develop at least some systemic envenoming, and most of these cases will require antivenom therapy. Admit all cases of definite or suspected bites.

On presentation, establish a good IV line, commence IV fluids, take bloods for initial tests ("extended coagulation tests" = PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen level***, FDP/XDP, platelet count; CK, urea, creatinine, WCC, K+), perform venom detection on the bite site (if uncertain of identity of snake). (*** fibrinogen level is only useful if it can be reliably measured to very low levels; many labs are unable to do this or may use indirect measurement and in this setting fibrinogen is not a useful test and could be omitted)Unless contraindicated by pre-existing medical conditions, give an IV fluid load (1L over 2-3 hrs in an adult; volume determined by weight in a child), then keep well hydrated thereafter (100-150 mL/hr in an adult), while carefully watching for fluid overload, monitoring fluid input and output. If a first aid bandage is in place over the bitten limb, leave on until the blood tests, venom detection and full examination are complete and results available. To perform venom detection cut away the bandage immediately over the bite area, to swab for venom. Keep the cut-away bandages, in case they are needed later for venom detection.

If the patient presents with envenoming clearly established, or blood tests show evidence of systemic envenoming (developing flaccid paralysis, even just ptosis; developing myolysis, elevated CK >1,000; secondary renal damage, elevated creatinine & urea), then commence antivenom therapy prior to removal of first aid. Initial dose should be at least 3 vials of CSL Tiger Snake AV IV, diluted up to 1:10 (less in children, to avoid volume overload), with adrenaline ready to treat anaphylaxis, should this occur.

Repeat blood tests 3 hrs after completion of initial dose of antivenom therapy, to determine if myolysis has been arrested (no further or minimal rise in CK). If the CK has continued to climb dramatically, give further antivenom (usually another 2-3 vials). If the 3hr tests suggest improvement then wait 2-3 hrs and retest. If there is continued improvement, it is likely no further antivenom will be needed. If, however, there has been no improvement or worsening, give further antivenom. The role of antivenom in reversing myolysis is controversial, but given the potential lethality of severe myolysis, further antivenom therapy should always be considered.

For cases where initial blood tests show no sign of either myolysis (CK) and there is no evidence of flaccid paralysis on examination, no antivenom is required at this stage, and first aid should then be removed, but repeat tests 2-3 hrs and 5-6 hrs later and if these show developing envenoming, treat with antivenom, as discussed earlier.

For cases with myolysis, CK might potentially rise to high levels, >25,000 IU/L. This could be accompanied by muscle pain, tenderness and weakness. There may also be gross myoglobinuria (red to black urine) and the risk of renal failure. Hyperkalaemia could develop secondary to myolysis and might be more severe if there is secondary renal failure. It could cause cardiotoxicity and be potentially lethal and be difficult to treat.

Flaccid neurotoxic paralysis is a major feature of Australian copperhead envenoming. It is unknown if there are potent presynaptic neurotoxins prominent in the venom, but if there are then antivenom therapy is very unlikely to reverse paralysis already established. In this situation there is no value in continually giving antivenom in the hope of reversing established paralysis, but if paralysis is incomplete, then it is certainly worth giving antivenom to try and prevent progression of paralysis. In this latter situation it is likely that some progression of paralysis will occur over 1-3+ hrs after antivenom, caused by venom already bound to the terminal axon of the neuromuscular junction. It is unclear if the neurotoxins are principally postsynaptic, or a mixture of pre- & postsynaptic. If the former, then antivenom might reverse established paralysis.

Little is known of the local effects of Australian copperhead bites, but both pain and mild swelling might be expected. Secondary infection is uncommon, but if there is a strong suspicion of secondary infection with cellulitis, then antibiotic therapy, initially IV, is required, but in most cases of tiger snake bites, antibiotics are not required. Tetanus immunisation status should always be checked and a booster given when indicated.

### Haematological Haemorrhagins

PLA2 ( Platelet aggregating inhibitor ). Mol. Wt. approx. 15,000. A minimum of 80 nM was required to inhibit platelet aggregation. It inhibits platelet aggregation through the formation of lysophospholipids by the hydrolysis of phospholipids due to its inherent catalytic activity. Yuan et al (1993) ( Ref : R000846 ) and Yuan et al (1995) ( Ref : R000847 ).

Superbin I and Superbin II. Superbin I is homogenous, Mol. Wt. approx. 13,252, whereas Superbin II contains two proteins ( Superbin IIa and IIb ) Mol. Wts. 13,236 and 13,213. Superbins I and II PLA2 activity was 346.0 and 258.9 µmoles / min / mg respectively ( egg phosphatidycholine ) and anticoagulant activity was present but weak. Superbin I and II also inhibited aggregation of human platelets induced by collagen. Subburaju and Kini (1997) ( Ref : R000848 ).
