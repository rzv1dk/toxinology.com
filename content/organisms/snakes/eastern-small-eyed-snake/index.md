## Case Studies

Ivermee P., Desjardines J. and MeEwan S. (2000) Tiger snake antivenom for suspected small-eyed snake envenomation. Emerg. Med., Vol. 12, pp 246-247.

Single case report. 39 yr old bitten by organism unseen on leg while in garden, near Coffs Harbour, NSW, Australia, 2000. Developed severe constant abdominal pain and limb pain 4 hrs after possible bite. Noted to be lying with joints flexed, abdomen contracted, able to move all limbs, but pain on passive and active movement, diaphoretic, palmer erythema, febrile 38.5 C, BP 90/70, pulse 114. 2 fresh punctures on leg, 6mm apart, no local reaction. Given IV fluids, morphine for pain. Venom detection (urine) negative. Only lab test abnormal was CK (44,800 IU/l). Tentative diagnosis of eastern small eyed snake bite (because of locality, pure but severe myolytic presentation). Given 1 ampoule CSL Tiger Snake Antivenom IV, no reaction, symptoms started to abate 30 mins after antivenom, had resolved by 4 hrs. CK level decreased progressively from then until discharge on day 5.

This case, though reported as small eyed snake bite, which it may well have been, cannot be taken as a proven bite by this species. The authors note it is the most likely culprit. Of the other snakes in this region, the only others known to cause major myolysis are the tiger snake and rough scaled snake, both of which also cause defibrination coagulopathy and paralysis as well as myolysis, the former two being mor prominent. They also usually cause bite site pain, swelling and bruising. Therefore this case is not suggestive of a bite by these snakes. Red bellied black snakes, also present, can cause myolysis, but not so severe, and also cause marked local effects, notably pain and swelling. The small eyed snake, based on very limited clinical experience, can cause isolated severe myolysis. It is therefore reasonable to assume that this case was most likely a bite by this species.

Furtado M.A. and Lester I.A. (1968) Myoglobinuria following snakebite. Med. J. Aust., Vol. 1, pp 674-676.

This is the only definite case report of a bite by this snake. 20 yr old male herpetologist bitten on finger by a snake identified as "Cryptophis nigrescens" by patient (who was interested in snakes and had been bitten by this species previously, without harm), near Cairns, Queensland, Australia, in 1965. 3 days after the bite he sought medical advice because of a stiff neck and inability to open his mouth. A tentative diagnosis of tetanus was made, but his symptoms were not confirmed on examination and as he improved, he was sent home after 2 days. 3 days later he returned, now unable to walk because of intense muscular pain in his legs. There was decreased power in the legs and his urine was dark brown. A new diagnosis of leptospirosis was made and treatment with penicillin commenced. However, his weakness and muscle pain increased, he became febrile, oliguric, had hyperkalaemia (6.7 mEq/l) and was considered to be in renal failure. By the next day he had no power in the legs, only 2/5 power in the arms, had respiratory distress and so a tracheostomy was performed and he was placed on a ventilator. Later that day, while being turned, he had a cardiac arrest (described as "became pulseless" in the report), from which he could not be revived. Autopsy showed only kidney changes consistent with renal failure and muscle histology was normal, though a muscle biopsy performed prior to death showed necrosis and myoglobinuria was confirmed.

This is an old case, with limited laboratory testing and no CK levels given, but the progressive muscle pain and weakness, with myoglobinuria and muscle biopsy showing necrosis is consistent with venom-induced myolysis. The normal muscle histology at autopsy is therefore puzzling.

## Clinical Effects

Fatal outcome uncommon, even untreated

There are only case reports of bites by one member of this genus, Cryptophis nigrescens, but other members are of a similar size. Similarly, there is venom data only for C. nigrescens, but it may be all species have similar venoms. Therefore, until new evidence suggests otherwise, it is prudent to assume all species may cause similar effects.

C. nigrescens has caused one known fatality, in rather unusual circumstances, with delayed envenoming, characterized by major myolysis and renal failure, without apparent coagulopathy or paralysis. Venom studies confirmed myotoxicity. The reason for delayed myotoxicity in this case is unclear, but a more recent probable case (not positive ID) suggests that myolysis is a real risk and may occur early, but is responsive to CSL Tiger Snake Antivenom.

The rate of envenoming for C. nigrescens is unknown, but as it is common in populated areas, it may well be low.

### Special Risk Groups
#### Children

Risk to children from local or systemic envenoming is similar in nature to the risk for adults, but a childs smaller body mass implies that the comparative venom concentration will be higher, so more severe envenoming is likely, the increased risk being inversely proportional to the size of the child.

#### Pregnancy

No information available

#### Elderly

The higher incidence of pre-existing diseases in the elderly and associated frailty place them at higher risk should envenoming occur. The nature and extent of increased risk will largely be determined by the nature and extent of pre-existing disease and by any medications used to treat such disease.

## Description

### Adult Length

0.45 m

Medium in length, cylindrical to slightly compressed, slender bodied snake with a moderately short tail. Can grow to a maximum of about 1.20 metres ( rarely exceeds 1.00 metres ) with most specimens less than 0.80 metres. Head is medium in size, somewhat rectangular, depressed and slightly distinct from neck. Snout is blunt when viewed from above. Eyes are moderately small in size with rounded ( vertically subelliptical ) pupils. Dorsal scales are smooth with vertebral scales on the anterior tail enlarged.

### Coloration & Markings

Dorsal surface body colour is uniform shiny bluish black. Head dorsum, head laterally and head venter is uniform black. Eyes are black without any noticeable colour on the iris or rim. Ventral surface is white, cream or pink sometimes with black flecking or blotching.

### Head Scales

The usual 9 supracephalic head shields, rostral about twice as wide as high, hexagonal frontal longer than wide and more than 1.5 times as wide as the supraocular, supranasal present, no loreal with nasal and single preocular scales in contact, 2 postoculars, no suboculars, temporals 2 + 2 ( lower anterior temporal is not in contact with postoculars resulting in a 1 + 3 arrangement ), 6 supralabials ( 3rd and 4th in contact with eye ) and 7 infralabials.

### Body Scales
#### Mid Body Scales

15 to 15 (usually 15)

#### Subcaudal Scales

30 to 45

single

No additional information

#### Ventral Scales

165 to 210

#### Anal Scale (Single or Divided)

single

### Breeding

Mating occurs in spring ( September to November ). Viviparous, about 4 ( range of 1 to 8 ) young born during February or March in southern portions of the range or October to February in the north where breeding may be unseasonal or a-seasonal. Young measure 10 to 20 cm in length at birth.

### Dentition

Front fangs located at anterior end of maxillary bone ( proteroglyphous ), 2 to 5 maxillary teeth following the fang.

### Habits

Nocturnal and secretive with a placid disposition. Shelters under logs, loose bark, under rocks or in rock crevices or deep soil cracks. Aggregation during hibernation has been recorded. If cornered or provoked it will thrash about aggressively, but is usually reluctant to bite.

### Prey

Feeds mainly on small lizards ( particularly skinks and geckos ), lizard eggs, and occasionally frogs and small snakes.

Sexual Dimorphism

Males grow to a larger size than females.

## First Aid

Keep victim still, apply pressure immobilisation bandage & splint, gently wash bite area, transport to medical care

First aid for bites by Elapid snakes which do not cause significant injury at the bite site (see Comments for partial listing), but which may have the potential to cause significant general (systemic) effects, such as paralysis, muscle damage, or bleeding.

1. After ensuring the patient and onlookers have moved out of range of further strikes by the snake, the bitten person should be reassured and persuaded to lie down and remain still. Many will be terrified, fearing sudden death and, in this mood, they may behave irrationally or even hysterically. The basis for reassurance is the fact that many venomous bites do not result in envenoming, the relatively slow progression to severe envenoming (hours following elapid bites, days following viper bites) and the effectiveness of modern medical treatment.
1. The bite wound should not be tampered with in any way. Wiping it once with a damp cloth to remove surface venom is unlikely to do much harm (or good) but the wound must not be massaged. For Australian snakes only, do not wash or clean the wound in any way, as this may interfere with later venom detection once in a hospital.
1. All rings or other jewellery on the bitten limb, especially on fingers, should be removed, as they may act as tourniquets if oedema develops.
1. If the bite is on a limb, a broad bandage (even torn strips of clothing or pantyhose) should be applied over the bitten area at moderate pressure (as for a sprain; not so tight circulation is impaired), then extended to cover as much of the bitten limb as possible, including fingers or toes, going over the top of clothing rather than risking excessive limb movement by removing clothing. The bitten limb should then be immobilised as effectively as possible using an extemporised splint or sling.
1. If there is any impairment of vital functions, such as problems with respiration, airway, circulation, heart function, these must be supported as a priority. In particular, for bites causing flaccid paralysis, including respiratory paralysis, both airway and respiration may be impaired, requiring urgent and prolonged treatment, which may include the mouth to mask (mouth to mouth) technique of expired air transfer. Seek urgent medical attention.
1. Do not use Tourniquets, cut, suck or scarify the wound or apply chemicals or electric shock.
1. Avoid peroral intake, absolutely no alcohol. No sedatives outside hospital. If there will be considerable delay before reaching medical aid, measured in several hours to days, then give clear fluids by mouth to prevent dehydration.
1. If the offending snake has been killed it should be brought with the patient for identification (only relevant in areas where there are more than one naturally occurring venomous snake species), but be careful to avoid touching the head, as even a dead snake can envenom. No attempt should be made to pursue the snake into the undergrowth as this will risk further bites.
1. The snakebite victim should be transported as quickly and as passively as possible to the nearest place where they can be seen by a medically-trained person (health station, dispensary, clinic or hospital). The bitten limb must not be exercised as muscular contraction will promote systemic absorption of venom. If no motor vehicle or boat is available, the patient can be carried on a stretcher or hurdle, on the pillion or crossbar of a bicycle or on someone's back.
1. Most traditional, and many of the more recently fashionable, first aid measures are useless and potentially dangerous. These include local cauterization, incision, excision, amputation, suction by mouth, vacuum pump or syringe, combined incision and suction ("venom-ex" apparatus), injection or instillation of compounds such as potassium permanganate, phenol (carbolic soap) and trypsin, application of electric shocks or ice (cryotherapy), use of traditional herbal, folk and other remedies including the ingestion of emetic plant products and parts of the snake, multiple incisions, tattooing and so on.

## Medical Treatment

Most bites will be minor, but can cause major myolysis, so admit, assess. If myolysis, give CSL Tiger Snake AV.

Bites by most species of Rhinoplocephalus are probably minor. This even applies to Rhinoplocephalus nigrescens, but this species has caused at least one fatality due to delayed severe myolysis and secondary renal failure. Because of this, bites by these snakes must be treated with great caution.

It is probably wisest to admit all cases at least overnight. Those that remain well the following day should be followed up with repeat tests for CK and renal function after discharge, at days 2 & 7, or earlier if they become symptomatic. On presentation, insert an IV line, give an initial IV fluid load, take blood for coagulation studies, CK, renal function, electrolytes & complete blood picture. Antivenom, preferably CSL Tiger Snake AV IV (initial dose 3 vials), should only be used if there is clear evidence of developing myolysis, such as a high CK (>1500 IU/l) and rising, with or without evidence of secondary renal failure or hyperkalaemia. Once the initial dose is given, it is unclear when and if a further dose is appropriate, but it could be considered if there was a further major rise in CK.

### Myotoxins

Sutherland (1981) ( Ref : R000025 and R000026 ) in monkey experiments demonstrated myolytic activity with a 16 fold ( relatively moderate ) increase in CK about 3 hours after inoculation. Pollitt (1980) ( Ref : R000075) isolated a fraction, Cryptoxin which had myotoxic, but no neurotoxic effect. He also concluded that the destruction of muscle tissue may continue for several days after the bite.
