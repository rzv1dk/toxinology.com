## Clinical Effects

Insufficient clinical reports to know

There are neither case reports or venom studies to indicate the potential hazard posed by Drysdalia spp.. However, their small size makes effective envenoming of humans unlikely, other than perhaps mild local pain and swelling. Nevertheless, experience with some other small Australian elapids suggest mild systemic envenoming cannot be excluded, so any patients bitten by these snakes should be tested for paralysis, myolysis, coagulopathy and renal failure. Should significant envenoming occur, the choice of antivenom is uncertain, but CSL Tiger Snake Antivenom would seem the first choice.

### Special Risk Groups
#### Children

Risk to children from local or systemic envenoming is similar in nature to the risk for adults, but a childs smaller body mass implies that the comparative venom concentration will be higher, so more severe envenoming is likely, the increased risk being inversely proportional to the size of the child.

#### Pregnancy

Potential risk to foetus from systemic envenoming, similar to risk to mother

#### Elderly

The higher incidence of pre-existing diseases in the elderly and associated frailty place them at higher risk should envenoming occur. The nature and extent of increased risk will largely be determined by the nature and extent of pre-existing disease and by any medications used to treat such disease.

## Description

### Adult Length

0.25 m

Very small in length, moderately slender bodied snake with a moderately short tail. Can grow to a maximum of about 0.39 metres. Head is slightly distinct from neck. Snout is rounded when viewed from above. Eyes are medium in size with round pupils. Dorsal scales are smooth ( matt-like ) with lower lateral scale rows not noticeably enlarged. Ventral and subcaudal scales are smooth.

### Coloration & Markings

Dorsal surface is pale grey to dark grey, greyish brown, yellowish brow or olive brown often with a reddish or brownish tinge on the scales producing longitudinal striations. A distinctive yellow, orange or or yellowish brown ( 2 or 3 mid-dorsal scales in length ), usually vertebrally incomplete, nuchal bar varies between prominence and obscurity between specimens. The incompleteness is usually no more than 1 or 2 scales wide on the vertebral line. Head dorsum is a darker grey, darker brown or blackish with or without some flecking or mottling. Snout and head laterally is generally paler with a distinctive narrow black streak extending from the nostril, beneath the eye to the side of the neck. Labials are white with dark grey to black spotting. Ventral and subcaudal surface colour is cream to yellow with dark brown to black spotting or flecking along the lateral edges of ventrals. The iris is dark brown with a reddish brown rim around the pupil.

### Head Scales

The usual 9 supracephalic head shields, nostril located in single nasal, no loreal, no suboculars, nasal and preocular scales in contact, frontal longer than broad but less than 1.5 times as broad as the supraocular, single preocular, 2 postoculars, temporals 2 + 2, 6 supralabials ( 3rd and 4th in contact with eye ) and usually 7 infralabials.

### Breeding

Viviparous, usually only 2 or 3 young born during summer.

### Dentition

Front fangs located at anterior end of maxillary bone ( proteroglyphous ), 3 to 5 maxillary teeth following the fang.

### Habits

Mainly nocturnal and terrestrial, sometimes forages during the day. Shelters under logs, leaf litter, under rocks ( particularly limestone ) or in rock crevices.

Juveniles

Juveniles have a red or reddish orange ventral surface which gradually fades to a yellow or yellowish orange in adults.

### Prey

Feed mainly on lizards ( particularly skinks ).

Sexual Dimorphism

Males and females are of similar size.

## First Aid

Keep victim still, apply pressure immobilisation bandage & splint, do not wash bite area, transport to medical care

First aid for bites by Elapid snakes which do not cause significant injury at the bite site (see Comments for partial listing), but which may have the potential to cause significant general (systemic) effects, such as paralysis, muscle damage, or bleeding.

1. After ensuring the patient and onlookers have moved out of range of further strikes by the snake, the bitten person should be reassured and persuaded to lie down and remain still. Many will be terrified, fearing sudden death and, in this mood, they may behave irrationally or even hysterically. The basis for reassurance is the fact that many venomous bites do not result in envenoming, the relatively slow progression to severe envenoming (hours following elapid bites, days following viper bites) and the effectiveness of modern medical treatment.
1. The bite wound should not be tampered with in any way. Wiping it once with a damp cloth to remove surface venom is unlikely to do much harm (or good) but the wound must not be massaged. For Australian snakes only, do not wash or clean the wound in any way, as this may interfere with later venom detection once in a hospital.
1. All rings or other jewellery on the bitten limb, especially on fingers, should be removed, as they may act as tourniquets if oedema develops.
1. If the bite is on a limb, a broad bandage (even torn strips of clothing or pantyhose) should be applied over the bitten area at moderate pressure (as for a sprain; not so tight circulation is impaired), then extended to cover as much of the bitten limb as possible, including fingers or toes, going over the top of clothing rather than risking excessive limb movement by removing clothing. The bitten limb should then be immobilised as effectively as possible using an extemporised splint or sling.
1. If there is any impairment of vital functions, such as problems with respiration, airway, circulation, heart function, these must be supported as a priority. In particular, for bites causing flaccid paralysis, including respiratory paralysis, both airway and respiration may be impaired, requiring urgent and prolonged treatment, which may include the mouth to mask (mouth to mouth) technique of expired air transfer. Seek urgent medical attention.
1. Do not use Tourniquets, cut, suck or scarify the wound or apply chemicals or electric shock.
1. Avoid peroral intake, absolutely no alcohol. No sedatives outside hospital. If there will be considerable delay before reaching medical aid, measured in several hours to days, then give clear fluids by mouth to prevent dehydration.
1. If the offending snake has been killed it should be brought with the patient for identification (only relevant in areas where there are more than one naturally occurring venomous snake species), but be careful to avoid touching the head, as even a dead snake can envenom. No attempt should be made to pursue the snake into the undergrowth as this will risk further bites.
1. The snakebite victim should be transported as quickly and as passively as possible to the nearest place where they can be seen by a medically-trained person (health station, dispensary, clinic or hospital). The bitten limb must not be exercised as muscular contraction will promote systemic absorption of venom. If no motor vehicle or boat is available, the patient can be carried on a stretcher or hurdle, on the pillion or crossbar of a bicycle or on someone's back.
1. Most traditional, and many of the more recently fashionable, first aid measures are useless and potentially dangerous. These include local cauterization, incision, excision, amputation, suction by mouth, vacuum pump or syringe, combined incision and suction ("venom-ex" apparatus), injection or instillation of compounds such as potassium permanganate, phenol (carbolic soap) and trypsin, application of electric shocks or ice (cryotherapy), use of traditional herbal, folk and other remedies including the ingestion of emetic plant products and parts of the snake, multiple incisions, tattooing and so on.

## Medical Treatment

Previously thought to be harmless, now known to cause at least minor coagulopathy. All cases should be fully assessed. Minor coagulopathy may not warrant AV (CSL Tiger Snake AV), but more severe coagulopathy may justify AV treatment.

There are almost no records of bites by Drysdalia spp., but a single report of major envenoming by Drysdalia coronoides suggests they cannot be considered harmless. The following is based on that report, but it is likely that most bites by these snakes will prove minor. In view of the potential seriousness of envenoming, all cases should be admitted.

Significant bites are associated with local pain, swelling, which can be extensive, non-specific general systemic symptoms, occasionally collapse, at least cranial nerve paralysis, myolysis, potentially moderate to severe, apparently not coagulopathy, but secondary renal damage is possible.

All cases should be observed for at least 12 hrs, preferably overnight. On presentation, establish a good IV line, commence IV fluids, take bloods for initial tests ("extended coagulation tests" = PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen level, FDP/XDP, platelet count; CK, urea, creatinine, WCC, K+), perform venom detection on the bite site (if uncertain of identity of snake). Unless contraindicated by pre-existing medical conditions, give an IV fluid load (1L over 2-3 hrs in an adult; volume determined by weight in a child), then keep well hydrated thereafter (100-150 mL/hr in an adult), while carefully watching for fluid overload, monitoring fluid input and output. If a first aid bandage is in place over the bitten limb, leave on until the blood tests, venom detection and full examination are complete and results available. To perform venom detection cut away the bandage immediately over the bite area, to swab for venom. Keep the cut-away bandages, in case they are needed later for venom detection. Venom detection results for these snakes are uncertain.

Major systemic envenoming, though probably rare, can occur. Symptoms of general systemic envenoming, such as headache, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, even collapse, can occur, as can localised (facial) paralysis, myolysis, and possibly renal damage, in which case antivenom therapy should be considered. If the patient presents with major envenoming clearly established, or blood tests show evidence of systemic envenoming (developing flaccid paralysis, even just ptosis; developing myolysis, elevated CK >1,000; secondary renal damage, elevated creatinine & urea), then consider giving antivenom therapy prior to removal of first aid. Initial dose should be at least 2-3 vials of CSL Tiger Snake AV IV, diluted up to 1:10 (less in children, to avoid volume overload), with adrenaline ready to treat anaphylaxis, should this occur. This antivenom is of unproven value for Drysdalia snake bites, but is the most likely antivenom to prove effective.

In the event that antivenom therapy has been required, repeat blood tests 3 hrs after completion of initial dose of antivenom therapy, to determine if myolysis has been arrested (no further or minimal rise in CK). If the CK has continued to climb dramatically, give further antivenom (usually another 1-2 vials). If the 3hr tests suggest improvement then wait 2-3 hrs and retest. If there is continued improvement, it is likely no further antivenom will be needed. If, however, there has been no improvement or worsening, give further antivenom. The role of antivenom in reversing myolysis is controversial, but given the potential lethality of severe myolysis, further antivenom therapy should always be considered.

For virtually all cases, where initial blood tests show no sign of myolysis (CK) and there is no evidence of flaccid paralysis on examination, no antivenom is required at this stage, and first aid should then be removed, but repeat tests 2-3 hrs and 5-6 hrs later and if these show developing envenoming, treat with antivenom, as discussed earlier.

For cases with myolysis, CK may rise to high levels, >50,000 IU/L. This may be accompanied by muscle pain, tenderness and weakness. There may also be gross myoglobinuria (red to black urine) and the risk of renal failure. Hyperkalaemia could possibly develop secondary to myolysis and is more likely to be severe if there is secondary renal failure. It could cause cardiotoxicity and is potentially lethal and may be difficult to treat.

Flaccid neurotoxic paralysis has been described after a single case of Drysdalia coronoides envenoming. The paralytic features were limited to facial weakness, including ptosis. No neurotoxins are known from this little studied venom. If they are present, then they could be either postsynaptic or a mixture of both pre- & postsynaptic neurotoxins. If presynaptic neurotoxins were in the venom, antivenom therapy is very unlikely to reverse paralysis already established. There is no value in continually giving antivenom in the hope of reversing established paralysis, but if paralysis is incomplete, then it is certainly worth giving antivenom to try and prevent progression of paralysis. In this latter situation it is likely that some progression of paralysis will occur over 1-3+ hrs after antivenom, caused by venom already bound to the terminal axon of the neuromuscular junction. If any neurotoxins were purely postsynaptic, then antivenom might reverse established paralysis.

Drysdalia spp. do cause local pain and swelling, but there are no reports of local tissue injury. Secondary infection has not been reported and should be considered uncommon, but if there is a strong suspicion of secondary infection with cellulitis, then antibiotic therapy, initially IV, is required, but in most cases of tiger snake bites, antibiotics are not required. Tetanus immunisation status should always be checked and a booster given when indicated, but not until any coagulopathy has been reversed.

### Management in Detail
#### Immediate Effects Management

Establish IV line, IV fluids, secure airway, maintain respiration if imperiled, treat hypotension (if present), do not clean wound until venom detection performed

#### Approach to Management

It is possible that most cases will be minor, but some cases may be more severe, requiring admission and treatment, so assess carefully before discharge.

#### Follow Up

Follow up cases with significant local or systemic envenoming

#### Local Effects Management

Do not clean wound

#### Systemic Effects Management

Symptomatic care + IV fluids (as required)

#### Cardiotoxin Effects Management

Monitor for secondary cardiotoxicity due to hyperkalaemia induced by myolysis

#### Haematologic Effects Management

Haematologic effects not likely

#### Haematologic Other Effects Management

Not applicable

#### Important Laboratory Test

Coagulation (WBCT or PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen, FDP/XDP); FBC/CBP (platelets, Hb, WCC, absolute lymphocyte count); renal function (creatinine, urea); CK; electrolytes (K, Na, Cl, Glucose).

#### Myotoxic Effects Management

Ensure good renal flow (adequate IV fluids), consider appropriate antivenom, consider alkalinising urine, watch for secondary renal damage & hyperkalaemia
