## Summary

### Dry Bite

75%

### Rate of Envenoming

20-40%

### Venomous

High Risk

Severe envenoming possible, potentially lethal

## Antivenom

### Antivenom Therapy

Antivenom is the key treatment for systemic envenoming. Multiple doses may be required.

### Antivenom Dosage

Refer to dosage schedule for relevant antivenoms listed on this site. However, please note that ongoing research is indicating a lower dose of antivenom, just 2 vials, may be sufficient in most cases of brown snake bite.

### Antivenom Reactions

Full range possible; see relevant section in antivenom pages

### Adverse Antivenom Reaction Management

See standard guidelines on antivenom pages of this site

Salt precipitated, pepsin digested. Liquid final product 48.3 to 51.5 ml. #### Comments

There is limited case evidence that Polyvalent Snake Antivenom may be effective in treating significant bites by the New Guinea small eyed snake, Micropechis ikaheka. Dose should be at least 1 vial.

1 vial. ## Case Studies

Not all cases listed here cover the species listed in this record; these cases are included because most brown snake species cause similar effects and these cases shed light on clinical envenoming. Cases listed vary from species record to record; further cases may be found listed for related species of Pseudonaja.

Foxton H.V. (1914) A case of fatal snake-bite ( with autopsy ). Med. J. Aust., August 1, p 108.

A fatal snakebite in a man, age 29 years, bitten on the left hand by a brown snake. First aid was scarification and local ligature. By one hour he had headache, by 3 hours nausea, vomiting (blood stained) and this continued overnight. He developed haemorrhage from the gums, and at 18 hours post bite he collapsed, had convulsions, and was comatose. He continued to have convulsions until his death, some 2 hours later. Autopsy showed mid-brain and brain stem haemorrhages, and small haemorrhagic areas in the stomach, intestines, kidneys, and bladder. Death was attributed to the haemorrhages in pons and medulla. Death was clearly due to the coagulopathy.

Fairley N.H. (1929) The present position of snake bite and the snake bitten in Australia. Med. J. Aust., Mar. 9, pp 12-13.

A fatal snakebite in a woman, age 29 years, bitten on the leg, through thick riding breeches, by a brown snake which had been milked earlier the same day. First aid was scarification and ligature. Initially well, she later developed vomiting, severe abdominal pain, then signs of shock with pallor, sweating, tachycardia and hypotension. She passed bright blood per rectum. She also developed some paralysis, with dysarthria, but died of "cardio-vascular failure" 12 hours post bite. No autopsy. The cause of death may well have been due to shock following blood loss associated with the coagulopathy. Fairley comments that significant bleeding problems are common after brown snake bite.

Schapel G.J., Utley D. and Wilson G.C. (1971) Envenomation by the Australian common brown snake - Pseudonaja ( Demansia ) textilis textilis. Med. J. Aust., Vol. 1, No. 3, pp 142-144.

A non-fatal case. A 19 year old man was bitten on the left wrist by a captive brown snake. At 15 minutes post bite he was given a test dose of brown snake antivenom I.V. (with adrenaline, antihistamine pre-medication), which resulted in anaphylaxis, successfully treated with adrenaline and steroids. He had received antivenoms on 2 previous occasions. Three hours post bite he developed extensive generalized submucosal and subcutaneous bruising, oozing of blood from skin puncture wounds, and haematemesis (500 ml). Investigations showed a marked coagulopathy, and thrombocytopenia. Further antivenom was clearly indicated and given under cover of adrenaline and steroids, and in addition he was heparinized. However, by 12 hours post bite "bleeding was controlled", although some evidence of coagulopathy was apparently present for several days. There was a short-lived rise in blood urea level, but urine output remained adequate. There was no evidence of paralysis at any stage. An eventual complete recovery was made.

Sutherland S.K., Coulter A.R. and Broad A.J. (1975) Human snake bite victims : the successful detection of circulating snake venom by radioimmunoassay. Med. J. Aust., No. 1, pp 27-29.

Three cases of snakebite where RIA venom detection was used to prove the diagnosis. Two were due to brown snakes.

Non-fatal bite to the leg of a 42 year old woman by P. textilis. The patient did not believe she had been bitten. At 20 minutes post bite she developed headache, then collapsed, possibly had a convulsion or cardiac arrest, and was given brown snake antivenom at 30 minutes post bite. She promptly improved clinically. She also developed persistent ooze of blood from the bite site, and at 5 hours post bite her blood was unclottable (after three ampoules of antivenom). Fibrinogen was given but the blood remained unclottable for at least several hours. She subsequently made a full recovery. Blood taken prior to the first dose of antivenom showed 20 ng/ul of brown snake venom.

Non-fatal bite to the ankle of a 66 year old woman by P. textilis. She subsequently felt dizzy but this resolved, and no further symptoms developed. She did not receive antivenom. A level of 35 ng/ml of brown snake venom was detected in her serum. No comment on tests for coagulopathy is made.

Pearn J., Morrison J., Charles N. and Muir V. (1981) First-aid for snake-bite. Efficacy of a constrictive bandage with limb immobilisation in the management of human envenomation. Med. J. Aust., Vol. 2, No. 6, pp 293-295.

A non-fatal bite to the hand of an adult male by a P. textilis. A compression type bandage was applied as first aid, without splint. During the two hours this first aid was in place, the patient was symptom free, but within five minutes of removal, headache and nausea developed, then pallor, sweatiness, an ache of the face and neck, and dyspnoea. Previously normal investigations now showed a blood level of brown snake venom of 1.5 ng/ul (peaking at 5 ng/ul at 45 minutes after release of first aid), and a defibrination type coagulopathy. The latter persisted for at least 24 hours, despite one ampoule of brown snake antivenom, which however relieved the symptomatology. There was no evidence of neurotoxicity.

This case report is probably the most detailed study of the effectiveness of the compression immobilization type first aid (SUTHERLAND et al 1979) in man.

Sutherland S.K., Duncan A.W. and Tibballs J. (1982) Death from a snake bite associated with the supine hypotensive syndrome of pregnancy. Med. J. Aust., Vol. 2, pp 238-239.

A fatal bite to the foot of a 39 year old 39 week pregnant woman, by a brown snake (positive venom detection). The snake was small (approx 18cm?), and the bite was initially thought to be trivial. By 30 minutes post bite she had developed a headache, then sudden collapse while supine on a hospital couch. A cardiac arrest quickly followed and resucitation failed, the patient dying 1 1/2 hours post bite. Brown snake venom was found at the bite site, but not in blood or urine, at autopsy, and no other abnormalities were found. The ensuing discussion concludes that death was due to the supine hypotensive syndrome of pregnancy rather than to envenomation.

Acott C.J. (1988) Acute renal failure after envenomation by the common brown snake. Med. J. Aust., Vol. 149, No. 11-12, pp 709-710.

An important case report, demonstrating isolated renal failure after brown snake bite, without evidence of significant coagulopathy or neurotoxicity.

A 51 year old male was bitten on the right thumb by a small P. textilis. About 2 hours post bite he developed abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and "felt terrible". Antivenom (one ampoule polyvalent) was given at six hours post bite. Investigations on samples taken at that time showed normal renal function, a platelet count of 110 X 10 9/l, and normal levels of fibrinogen and FDP (XDP), and brown snake venom in the blood. There was no evidence of neurotoxicity. He remained apparently stable and well, but at 24 hours post bite it was realized he was virtually anuric, and retesting showed elevated urea and creatinine, consistent with acute renal failure, a further drop in platelets to 41 X 10 9/l, but normal coagulation tests. While otherwise well his renal function deteriorated, requiring nine days of haemodialysis. Renal biopsy showed acute tubular necrosis. he subsequently had normal renal function.

(from MILTON, 1989)
A fatal case of brown snake bite in a 2 year 9 month old girl, found comatose and apnoeic by her parents. There was no history of snakebite. Resuscitation failed. Autopsy revealed possible bite marks on the left leg, from which tissue blocks were taken, and subsequently shown to contain a high concentration of brown snake venom. There was modest congestion and oedema of the lungs with petechial haemorrhages. Further details not provided.

Morling A.C., Marshall L.R., and Herrmann R.P. (1989) Thrombocytopenia after brown snake envenomation. Med. J. Aust., Vol. 151, pp 627-628.

A paper detailing apparent thrombocytopenia following brown snake bites in Western Australia, presumably due to either P. affinis or P. nuchalis. 10 cases of such bites noted, all with evidence of a coagulopathy. All those with sufficient clinical information had some evidence of renal involvement. Four cases had platelet counts below the normal range. It is suggested by the authors that this may be evidence of a direct platelet aggregating effect of brown snake venom, but some doubt has been cast on the validity of this conclusion (WHITE 1990).

Overall case analysis:
In Australia a significant number of cases of suspected snakebite do not result in identification of the species of snake responsible. However brown snakes are considered the leading cause of snakebite. In the authors experience with over 200 cases, of 118 where the snake species was identifiable, 60 were due to brown snakes.

Of the 60, 19 showed significant envenomation (32%), however this figure may be biased in favour of severe cases, as mild cases or "dry bites" are less likely to result in identification of the snake responsible.

Of the 19 cases with significant envenomation, 16 (+1) had evidence of coagulopathy, 2 (+1) had evidence of neurotoxicity, 5 had evidence of renal damage, and none showed evidence of myotoxicity.

Of the remaining 41 cases, 13 had evidence of possible mild envenomation, and 28 had no evidence of envenomation.

White J. (1980) Ophidian envenomation a South Australian perspective. Records of the Adelaide Children's Hospital, Vol. 2(3), pp 311-421.

A two year old boy bitten multiply on the left posterior upper thigh by a large brown snake. Within 30 minutes the child had collapsed, had a possible convulsion, and had developed a severe defibrination type coagulopathy, with unclottable blood, no detectable fibrinogen, gross elevation of FDP, and a normal platelet count. The first dose of antivenom did not change these findings after 2 1/2 and 4 hours, but 2 hours after a second dose of antivenom there was substantial improvement, which continued without need of further antivenom. This child did not show evidence of neurotoxicity, or renal damage. Evidence of recovery from the coagulopathy 2 hours after the second dose of antivenom correlated with a clinical improvement, the child ceasing to be irritable, and instead happily playing with parents.

White J. (1980) Ophidian envenomation a South Australian perspective. Records of the Adelaide Children's Hospital, Vol. 2(3), pp 311-421.

A seven year old boy bitten once on the thumb by a small (approx 0.6 m) brown snake, proven by venom detection. By 30 minutes post bite the child was irritable, rousable but drowsy, with non-clottable blood, and a severe defibrination type coagulopathy. A single dose of antivenom was given and resolution of the coagulopathy was less prompt than in the more aggressively treated case 1, despite case 1 being overall less severely envenomed on clinical grounds. There was no evidence of neurotoxicity or renal damage.

White J. and Williams V. (1989) Severe envenomation with convulsion following multiple bites by a common brown snake, Pseudonaja textilis. Aust. Paediatr. J., Vol. 25, pp 109-111.

A three 1/2 year old boy was bitten twice on the right ankle by an 86 cm P. textilis, and after an early (inappropriate) removal of first aid, collapsed with grand-mal convulsions. On arrival at hospital 30 minutes post bite the child was irritable, awake, with a rash on hands and leg, and normotensive (almost hypertensive, BP 140/90). Unfortunately the seriousness of the envenomation was not initially realized by the treating staff. Over the next two hours the child became drowsy, more irritable, vomited, complained of abdominal pain, and a severe defibrination type coagulopathy was demonstrated. Expert advice was sought, and antivenom therapy advised. Over the next few hours five ampoules of brown snake antivenom were given. By 2 1/2 hours after the last dose of antivenom, there was substantial improvment in the coagulopathy, which continued to resolve thereafter, associated with a dramatic clinical improvment within two hours, the child ceasing to be irritable, and instead happily playing with parents. There was no evidence of neurotoxicity or renal damage.

This case demonstrates clearly the method of titrating antivenom dose against progress of the coagulopathy.

White J. and Fassett R. (1983) Acute renal failure and coagulopathy after snake bite. Med. J. Aust., Vol. 2, No. 3, pp 142-143.

A 27 year old male was bitten three times by a juvenile P. textilis, while handling same, and at the time was significantly inebriated, with a blood alcohol of 0.28. He presented for treatment about 30 minutes post bite, following a collapse at home, from which he spontaneously recovered, and which was preceeded by headache, nausea, vomiting, and confusion. At hospital he was severely agitated, requiring physical restraint and relaxant medication, which delayed commencement of antivenom therapy. By 45 minutes post bite he had a severe defibrination type coagulopathy. Antivenom therapy was commenced 2 1/2 hours post bite. Substantial resolution of the coagulopathy was not apparent until five ampoules of brown snake antivenom had been infused, some 12 hours post bite, although a trend towards normal was established two hours after the fourth ampoule. In this case a mild thrombocytopenia was noted, and progressed despite resolution of the coagulopathy. However, the renal function also deteriorated reaching a peak creatinine level of 0.46 on day six, renal function remained impaired for several months, although not sufficient to require dialysis.

This case demonstrates the unfortunate combination of alchohol and snakebite, renal failure, and the association of renal failure with thrombocytopenia.

White J. (2000) Why do people still die from brown-snake bites. Emerg. Med., Vol. 12, pp 204-206.

A review of the causes of brown snake deaths in Australia, pointing out that some early deaths may be unavoidable, particularly if no first aid is used, because the powerful venom procoagulants may cause initial thrombosis before fibrinolysis is fully activated and these thrombi may occulde vital vessels, particularly coronary vessels. This is a credible explanation for the early cardiac collapse seen in some cases of brown snake bite. As the bite may not be felt, particularly in adults, it is possible the victim may not know they have been bitten, so fail to used first aid or seek treatment until it is too late. As most brown snake bites are minor (80%), routine use of antivenom in every case cannot be justified.

Sprivulis P., Jelinek G.A. and Marshall L. (1996) Efficacy and potency of antivenoms in neutralizing the procoagulant effects of Australian snake venoms in dog and human plasma. Anaesth. Intens. Care, Vol. 24, No. 3, pp 379-381.

2 cases of fatal brown snake bite associated with intracranial haemorrhage.

Case 1: 72 yr old woman bitten on foot by a brown snake (positive ID by venom detection; species of Pseudonaja uncertain, most likely P. affinis or P. nuchalis) in WA, Australia in 1993. 2 hrs after the bite she was asymptomatic, but had a gross defibrination coagulopathy. She was given 3 ampoules of CSL Brown Snake Antivenom IV after premed with IV hydrocortisone, IV promethazine and SC adrenaline and was also given 2 units of FFP. Subsequent coagulation testing showed some improvement. 13 hrs after the bite she developed a headache and lost consciousness. She was given more SC adrenaline and 2 ampoules of brown snake antivenom, plus 2 units of FFP and 8 units of cryoprecipitate. A CT head scan showed a 3 cm left frontal haematoma and a 1.2 cm left parietal subdural haematoma with midline shift. She was intubated but died 22 hrs after the bite. Autopsy was not performed (request denied).

Case 2: 31 yr old man bitten on finger by a brown snake (later positive ID of specimen as a gwardar, P. nuchalis) in the Kimberley region, WA, Australia in 1993. He collapsed shortly after, hitting his head, causing a laceration. When seen at a regional hospital 1 hr later he was alert, but bleeding from his scalp and gums and he had a haematemesis. Venom detection was negative. Coagulation tests showed gross defibrination coagulopathy. His friends identified the snake (incorrectly) as a king brown, Pseudechis australis, so he was given 1 ampoule of CSL Black Snake Antivenom, preceeded by premed with SC adrenaline and IV promethazine and hydrocortisone. 4 hrs after the bite he became confused, developed diplopia and partial ptosis and was given 2 ampoules of CSL Polyvalent Snake Antivenom. He worsened, required intubation and ventilation. He was evacuated to a major hospital, at which time the snake was correctly identified. He was therefore given 2 ampoules of CSL Brown Snake Antivenom, some 7 hrs after the bite. A CT head scan showed bilateral occipitotemporal haematomas and brain swelling. The haematomas were drained, but he died 23 hrs after the bite. Autopsy showed intracerebral and subarachnoid haemorrhages and features suggesting "brain tearing" consistent with a traumatic cause, most likely his blow to the head when he collapsed after the bite.

The authors note that the second case illustrates the danger of accepting a lay identification of a snake. They do not believe the adrenaline premed contributed to the intracranial haemorrhages.

Henderson A, Baldwin L.N. and May C. (1993) Fatal brown snake ( Pseudonaja textilis ) envenomation despite the use of antivenom. Med. J. Aust., Vol. 158, No. 10, pp 709-710.

Report of 2 cases of fatal brown snake bite.

Case 1: 42 yr old man bitten on the foot by a brown snake (positive ID by venom detection) near Emerald, Queensland, Australia. Within a few minutes of the bite, which he thought was a wasp sting, having not seen a snake, he developed dyspnoea and called an ambulance, which arrived 7 mins later, by which time he was unconscious but breathing. He was transported to the local hospital, but before arrival, about 1 hr after the bite, he had a cardiac arrest. On arrival he was apnoeic and asystolic. Cardiac rhythm was restored with difficulty, including intubation, ventilation, external cardiac massage, use of IV adrenaline. The working diagnosis was anaphylaxis. He remained hypotensive for 40 mins. 7 hrs after the bite he passed bright blood per rectum, and fresh blood appeared in his nasogastric aspirate, with oozing of blood from his venepuncture sites. Lab tests confirmed coagulopathy and brown snake venom was detected in his urine. He was then given 2 ampoules of CSL Brown Snake Antivenom. He continued to bleed and was given another ampoule of brown snake antivenom plus 1 ampoule of CSL Polyvalent Snake Antivenom and 6 units of FFP with 3 units of fresh blood. He was then transferred to a major hospital, arriving 16 hrs after the bite, at which time he had gross pulmonary oedema, was haemodynamically unstable, varying between hypotension and hypertension and he still had a coagulopathy, though his fibrinogen was 2.0 g/l. A CT head scan showed gross cerebral oedema. He developed brain stem death 2 days later and support was withdrawn. No autopsy is noted.

Case 2: 39 yr old man bitten several times on the foot by a brown snake (positive ID of specimen) in outer Brisbane, Queensland, Australia. Poor first aid was used and by the time he reached hospital, 15 mins after the bite, he was unconscious, hypoventilating, hypotensive. He was intubated, causing nasopharyngeal bleding. He was given 2 ampoules of CSL Brown Snake Antivenom and 1 ampoule of CSL Polyvalent Snake Antivenom IV, preceeded by premed with SC adrenaline and IV promethazine. Coagulation tests showed complete defibrination and thrombocytopenia. By 2 hrs after the bite he was still bleeding from his nose, was slightly hypotensive (100/50) and appeared oliguric, with gross haematuria. More brown snake antivenom was given (2 ampoules, then 2 more) and dopamine to raise his BP, but he remained oliguric, vasoconstricted and coagulopathic. He now had pulmonary oedema. He was given 14 units of FFP and 20 units of platelets over 2 hrs, with some improvement in coagulopathy, using plasma exchange to reduce volume overload. At 7 hrs after the bite he was anuric and had gross pulmonary oedema. At 12 hrs he developed large swings in BP. He was given a further 7 ampoules of antivenom, but developed refractory hypotension and died 17 hrs after the bite, by which time the coagulopathy had corrected. No autopsy is noted.

Though not discussed by the authors, it appears FFP was given in the second case before evidence of full neutralisation of procoagulant, which may have stimulated hyperfibrinolysis, would not have greatly helped the coagulopathy resolution and certainly contributed to fluid overload and consequent cardiac stress, despite otherwise heroic measures in a situation where primary cardiac damage early in the envenoming process seems likely. Similarly, FFP was probably not appropriate in the first case, contributing similar negative effects in a similar clinical setting.

Sutherland S.K. (1992) Deaths from snake bite in Australia, 1981-1991. Med. J. Aust., Vol. 157, No. 11-12, pp 740-746.

Report of snakebite fatalities in Australia, 1981-1991. Records 18 deaths with positive ID of snake, mostly by venom detection. Brown snakes caused 11/18 deaths. These 11 cases are listed below (case numbers are those of Sutherland).

Case 1: 16 yr old girl bitten near Walgett, NSW, 1981. Walked to aid after bite, died 60 mins after bite. Cause of death not stated.

Case 2: 27 yr old pregnant woman bitten by 0.18 M snake near Menindee, NSW, in 1982, died 90 mins after bite despite early antivenom. Cause of death uncertain, though attributed to supine hypotensive syndrome of pregnancy.

Case 3: 48 yr old man bitten near Kirra, Queensland, in 1983, thought snake was harmless, but 30 mins later collapsed unconscious, declared dead 75 mins after bite. Cause of death probably cardiac arrest, but not stated.

Case 4: 40 yr old man bitten near Cooktown, Queensland in 1984. Collapsed 10 mins after bite, unconscious, cyanosed, declared dead 3 hrs after bite. Probable coagulopathy, cause of death not stated, most likely cardiac arrest or failure.

Case 5: No information other than 71 yr old female from rural SA in 1985, brown snake venom detected. [Personal knowledge of Prof. White: This lady was actually found dead in her home, with a shovel and a dead brown snake next to her. She had killed the snake but had been bitten and probably died shortly after].

Case 6: 61 yr old woman near Cervantes, WA in 1986. Collapsed after arriving at health centre, died 1.5 hrs after bite. Probable coagulopathy at autopsy, cause of death not stated, most likely cardiac collapse.

Case 7: 53 yr old man bitten at least 8 times near Tugun, Queensland in 1986. Thought snake was harmless. Later felt dizzy, collapsed, hypotensive, but BP restored with IV adrenaline and recovered consciousness, seemed well, then coagulopathy noted, given antivenom with adrenaline premed. 7 hrs after bite sudden deterioration in mental state, given more antivenom and FFP, but CT head scan showed extensive intracerebral haemorrhage. Died 48 hrs after bite.

Case 12: 2 yr old child bittennear Carnarvon, WA in 1989. Child became tired, lay down (no history of bite at time), later found apnoeic, dead, so died <2hrs after likely bite (venom detected at PM). Autopsy showed mild lung oedema, cause of death not stated.

Case 16: 47 yr old man near Gatton, Queensland, in 1991. Used no first aid. Collapsed and had fit <60 mins later, became cyanotic, cardiac arrest. After 10 mins rhythm restored, given antivenom, but never regained consciousness. Cause of death was hypoxic brain damage.

Case 17: 60 yr old man bitten near Condoblin, NSW in 1991. Used tourniquet first aid. Oozing from bite later noted. Appeared generally well. Given antivenom 1 hr after bite, at which time tourniquet released. Within minutes collapsed with chest pain, was cyanotic, so given adrenaline for presumed anaphylaxis. Had ventricular fibrillation which failed to respond and died <2 hrs after bite. At autopsy little found, no IgE directed against the antivenom, so diagnosis of anaphylaxis uncertain. May have been cardiac collapse due to venom.

## Clinical Effects

Fatal outcome common in untreated severe cases. Cases with early cardiac collapse/arrest and inadequate early resuscitation are likely to suffer critical organ damage, perticularly hypoxic brain injury, from which survival is unlikely.

The following is an overview for all brown snakes, not just the species noted in this record. The nature of envenoming is similar for all species, except P. modesta, but some species produce more venom than others.

Brown snake bite is the most common cause of both snakebites and snakebite death in Australia at present. Brown snakes have small fangs and some specimens deliver only small quantities of venom, but that venom is highly toxic. Recent studies suggest that brown snakes can inject far more venom than originally thought possible. This may explain, in part, why cases of severe brown snake envenoming occasionally require such large quantities of antivenom. However, ongoing research tracking venom levels in response to antivenom therapy in prospective cases is providing a different view. This latter research indicates that even small amounts of antivenom, just 1-2 vials, are likely to effectively and rapidly neutralise all venom in virtually all cases.

Approximately 80 % of brown snake bites will be minor, showing trivial or no envenoming, thus not requiring antivenom therapy. Bites are often painless or cause minimal pain or local reaction and the small fang size results in bite marks that are sometimes invisible to the naked eye. Adults may be bitten without realizing it, which can cause tragic delays in treatment. Any clothing may reduce the chance of an effective bite. Brown snakes are excitable and may bite repeatedly if cornered, with a resultant much higher likelihood of major envenoming. As a general rule, a trivial looking bite may actually result in lethal envenoming, so every bite should be managed as a medical emergency, even though most will ultimately prove minor. To do otherwise risks avoidable fatalities or major complications.

Following an effective brown snake bite there may be rapid onset of systemic envenoming, sometimes within 5-15 minutes, symptoms of which may include headache, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, sudden collapse, convulsions (especially in children) and cardiac abnormalities (including cardiac arrest). Except where there has been a cardiac arrest, recovery from sudden loss of consciousness is often spontaneous, within 5-20 minutes of collapse. In recent years the majority of deaths following brown snake bite have been associated with early collapse and cardiac arrest, involving both adult and paediatric cases. These fatal cases are generally associated with inadequate early response to the cardiac arrest with either no or ineffective resuscitation, resulting in either pre-hospital death or catastrophic organ damage, particularly hypoxic brain damage, with consequent in-hospital death hours to days later. These deaths occur as a result of pre-hospital events, therefore before antivenom could reasonably be given. It is currently unclear if immediate expert cardiac resuscitation could result in a good outcome in such cases, but it is likely that at least some, maybe all could be saved.

Coagulopathy, defibrination type (labelled by some authors as "VICC" = venom induced consumptive coagulopathy), develops early and complete defibrination may be present within 15-30 minutes of a bite, especially if no first aid is used. However, coagulopathy does not always develop so early and may develop slowly over 2-6+ hrs. The coagulopathy is characterised by non-clotting blood (if complete defibrination), prolonged to grossly prolonged INR/PT, aPTT, low to absent fibrinogen and grossly elevated FDP/d-dimer. Platelet counts are often normal, at least in the first 12 hrs. They may fall later in association with a secondary "DIC", as seen with secondary renal damage, or possibly due to other factors. Increasing experience suggests this is most likely due to microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA); this is discussed below. The defibrination coagulopathy is reversible with CSL antivenom (Brown Snake or Polyvalent Snake Antivenom). Until recently expert opinion was that multiple ampoules were always required, usually 4-6+, though for some species (N. affinis, N. nuchalis/mengdeni/aspidorhyncha) even higher doses were routinely used. Current ongoing research measuring serial venom levels in prospective cases is indicating that far lower doses, just 1-2 vials, will be sufficient in nearly all cases. There is current controversy over the role of antivenom in reversing paralysis, as one research group contends that the venom procoagulants have a very short half life in circulation and so antivenom is unlikely to be given during this brief window of activity, thus will probably have no effect in most cases. This interpretation of research results and complex mathematical modelling of coagulation is not universally subscribed to.

Renal damage is the second common feature of brown snake envenoming. It is seen only in the minority of cases, is rare in children, but more common in adults if they have consumed alcohol around the time of the bite. The cause of the renal damage is uncertain, but may be secondary in most cases, though a primary nephrotoxin in the venom has been suspected, but not proven. Renal damage is almost always seen in cases with coagulopathy, but rarely it may occur in the absence of coagulopathy. The renal damage varies from a rise in creatinine over several days, settling to normal over a week or so, without oliguric, anuric or polyuric renal failure, through to complete anuric renal failure requiring a variable period of haemodialysis. Even this most severe manifestation is usually reversible over time. Microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) is emerging as an important result of brown snake envenoming and a potent cause of renal damage. Onset is usually many hours post-bite, generally after antivenom therapy has resulted in a reversal of the initial defibrination-type coagulopathy. There is development of intravascular haemolysis, with fragmented red cells, progressive thrombocytopenia, anaemia and deteriorating renal function. Oliguric or anuric renal failure can develop, but often the renal dysfunction is less severe. Platelet counts can be very low, but spontaneous bleeding is the exception. The syndrome reverses over the course of days to a week or more. It is unclear what effect, if any, primary venom toxicity plays in this process. It seems to develop after circulating venom has been destroyed by antivenom. It can occur in only mildly envenomed patients, not just those with severe envenoming.

While brown snake venoms contain potent presynaptic and postsynaptic neurotoxins, paralysis is rarely seen in human brown snake bite victims, though it is common in domestic animals bitten. In humans, paralytic features are most likely to be seen if there is a long delay in commencing antivenom therapy, but even then is uncommon. Because of the presence of presynaptic neurotoxins, antivenom may not reverse established paralysis, but as there are also postsynaptic neurotoxins present, in higher concentrations, antivenom should not be ignored as treatment for paralysis. In a patient with major paralysis after confirmed brown snake bite, consider an anticholinesterase trial (such as edrophonium) to determine if the paralysis is principally postsynaptic (may respond to antivenom) or presynaptic (no response to antivenom). In nearly all, possibly all cases of brown snake bite with paralysis, there will also be coagulopathy, requiring antivenom to reverse, likely sufficient to retard or reverse any postsynaptic paralysis.

Brown snake bite is not associated with venom-induced myolysis, although mild elevations of CK are sometimes seen.

In addition to secondary renal damage, which is usually an acute tubular necrosis and ultimately reversible, the major complication of brown snake envenoming is massive haemorrhage secondary to the coagulopathy. Of particular note is intracranial haemorrhage which is almost always lethal.

## Description

### Adult Length

1.20 m

Large in length, slender to moderately robust bodied snake with a medium length tail. Can grow to a maximum of about 1.82 metres. Head is indistinct from neck with a prominent canthus rostralis. Eyes are medium in size with round pupils. Dorsal scales are smooth.

### Coloration & Markings

Highly variable in colour but usually a monotone shade of brown. Dorsal surface body colour may be greyish yellow, yellowish brown, tan, ochre, greyish brown, olive, reddish brown, blackish brown, pale brown, dark brown or black sometimes becoming paler on the lower lateral 1 or 2 dorsal scale rows. Head dorsum is similar to dorsal surface body colour without markings. Head laterally is similar to dorsum, sometimes becoming paler on the labials. The iris is black with a thin yellow, orange to pale brown rim around the pupil. Rim is widest above and behind the pupil and usually an incomplete arc. Ventral surface colour is also quite variable but most often is off-white, cream, pale yellow or pale orange brown with darker orange brown blotching or spotting.

### Breeding

Mating occurs in spring extending into early summer ( September to December ). Oviparous, about 15 ( range of 8 to 35 ) eggs laid during October to December. A second clutch may be laid if ideal conditions prevail. Hatchlings emerge about 7 to 9 weeks later.

Front fangs located at anterior end of maxillary bone ( proteroglyphous ). At the genus level, there are 7 to 12 maxillary teeth following the fang. Fang length approximately 2.8 mm ( varies between 2.0 to 4.0 mm ).

### Habits

Diurnal and terrestrial snake with nocturnal tendencies in hotter weather. A swift moving snake with a more aggressive disposition than other species of the genus. If approached it will attempt to escape. If cornered it will hiss loudly, flatten the neck for a short period, then raise the forebody into an upright double S position and face the threat. Provocation or any approach too close will result in a rapid and snap like bite. Takes shelter in or under logs, abandoned animal burrows, deep soil cracks, under building materials and often found in and around farm sheds ( particularly hay sheds and grain silos which tend to attract mice ).

Juveniles

Juveniles have similar but prominent head markings which fade with age. Dorsal surface body colour and pattern vary greatly by locality. They usually have a black head ( except for the snout ) and black nuchal band but the entire upper body may be a uniform brown, or may have up to about 50 distinct black crossbands or may have alternating narrow and wide dark grey or black crossbands or may have dark tipped scales forming a reticulated pattern.

### Prey

Feeds mainly on small lizards ( particularly skinks, agamids and geckos ), frogs when available, mice and small birds.

## First Aid

Keep victim still, apply pressure immobilisation bandage & splint, do not wash bite area, transport to medical care

First aid for bites by Elapid snakes which do not cause significant injury at the bite site (see Comments for partial listing), but which may have the potential to cause significant general (systemic) effects, such as paralysis, muscle damage, or bleeding.

1. After ensuring the patient and onlookers have moved out of range of further strikes by the snake, the bitten person should be reassured and persuaded to lie down and remain still. Many will be terrified, fearing sudden death and, in this mood, they may behave irrationally or even hysterically. The basis for reassurance is the fact that many venomous bites do not result in envenoming, the relatively slow progression to severe envenoming (hours following elapid bites, days following viper bites) and the effectiveness of modern medical treatment.
1. The bite wound should not be tampered with in any way. Wiping it once with a damp cloth to remove surface venom is unlikely to do much harm (or good) but the wound must not be massaged. For Australian snakes only, do not wash or clean the wound in any way, as this may interfere with later venom detection once in a hospital.
1. All rings or other jewellery on the bitten limb, especially on fingers, should be removed, as they may act as tourniquets if oedema develops.
1. If the bite is on a limb, a broad bandage (even torn strips of clothing or pantyhose) should be applied over the bitten area at moderate pressure (as for a sprain; not so tight circulation is impaired), then extended to cover as much of the bitten limb as possible, including fingers or toes, going over the top of clothing rather than risking excessive limb movement by removing clothing. The bitten limb should then be immobilised as effectively as possible using an extemporised splint or sling.
1. If there is any impairment of vital functions, such as problems with respiration, airway, circulation, heart function, these must be supported as a priority. In particular, for bites causing flaccid paralysis, including respiratory paralysis, both airway and respiration may be impaired, requiring urgent and prolonged treatment, which may include the mouth to mask (mouth to mouth) technique of expired air transfer. Seek urgent medical attention.
1. Do not use Tourniquets, cut, suck or scarify the wound or apply chemicals or electric shock.
1. Avoid peroral intake, absolutely no alcohol. No sedatives outside hospital. If there will be considerable delay before reaching medical aid, measured in several hours to days, then give clear fluids by mouth to prevent dehydration.
1. If the offending snake has been killed it should be brought with the patient for identification (only relevant in areas where there are more than one naturally occurring venomous snake species), but be careful to avoid touching the head, as even a dead snake can envenom. No attempt should be made to pursue the snake into the undergrowth as this will risk further bites.
1. The snakebite victim should be transported as quickly and as passively as possible to the nearest place where they can be seen by a medically-trained person (health station, dispensary, clinic or hospital). The bitten limb must not be exercised as muscular contraction will promote systemic absorption of venom. If no motor vehicle or boat is available, the patient can be carried on a stretcher or hurdle, on the pillion or crossbar of a bicycle or on someone's back.
1. Most traditional, and many of the more recently fashionable, first aid measures are useless and potentially dangerous. These include local cauterization, incision, excision, amputation, suction by mouth, vacuum pump or syringe, combined incision and suction ("venom-ex" apparatus), injection or instillation of compounds such as potassium permanganate, phenol (carbolic soap) and trypsin, application of electric shocks or ice (cryotherapy), use of traditional herbal, folk and other remedies including the ingestion of emetic plant products and parts of the snake, multiple incisions, tattooing and so on.

## Medical Treatment

While most brown snake bites will prove minor, rapid lethal envenoming can occur, thus all cases must be assessed urgently. If there is systemic envenoming (coagulopathy or renal damage, rarely paralysis) give antivenom (number of vials required is reducing following ongoing clinical research).

Bites by Australian brown snakes are characterized by a high rate of "dry bites", but particularly in adults, severe envenoming can occur even in the absence of symptoms. The classic problems of brown snake systemic envenoming are defibrination coagulopathy ± renal failure. A few cases will also develop microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) which often develops even as the defibfination coagulopathy is resolving following antivenom therapy. In rare cases there may be flaccid paralysis, up to and including respiratory paralysis requiring respiratory support, potentially for prolonged periods. Paralysis is more likely to be seen in cases with a long delay between the bite and treatment. Therefore, all cases of definite or suspected brown snake bite should be admitted at least overnight for repeated assessment of coagulation, renal function, haemolysis and early signs of paralysis.

As part of the initial assessment, an IV line should be inserted, blood taken for standard tests ("extended coagulation studies" = PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen level***, FDP/XDP, platelet count; WCC, urea, creatinine, K+) and an IV fluid load given (*** fibrinogen level is only useful if it can be reliably measured to very low levels; many labs are unable to do this or may use indirect measurement and in this setting fibrinogen is not a useful test and could be omitted). In a healthy adult, give at least 1L IV fluids over 2-3 hrs, then at least 100-150 mL an hour for the next 6-12+ hrs, while carefully watching for fluid overload (in children, adjust rate of IV fluids based on weight). Leave any first aid bandage in place, while all this is done and until blood test results are available. If possible, perform venom detection on a bite site swab (cut away any first aid bandage directly over the bite site, but keep this bandage in case it is needed for later venom detection). Carefully assess the patient for evidence of active bleeding and for early signs of paralysis (ptosis, partial ophthalmoplegia etc), though flaccid paralysis is rare following brown snake bites.

If the first blood tests show significant coagulopathy (INR>1.5, usually >4, often >12; elevated FDP/XDP; low or "absent" fibrinogen) then commence antivenom therapy prior to removing first aid. Starting dose should be 2 vials of CSL Brown Snake AV IV, diluted up to 1:10 (depends on size of patient; lower dilutions in smaller children to avoid fluid overload). As dugites can deliver larger amounts of venom than eastern brown snakes, there was a previous trend in Western Australia to commence with higher doses of antivenom, usually at least 6 vials, but some hospitals commenced with 10 vials. This increased starting dose was not proven by clinical trial. Past WA experience seems to suggest most cases with defibrination ended up requiring at least 10 vials of antivenom. However, more recent research across Australia using a prospective study involving venom levels has indicated that far lower doses of antivenom may be sufficient. While these studies are ongoing, enough evidence has accumulated to recommend a lower starting dose of CSL Brown Snake Antivenom. This should be just 2 vials. Some authors suggest even 1 vial may be enough. In nearly all cases 1-2 vials appears likely to be sufficient. After this initial dose enough time must be allowed for recovery of fibrinogen, at least 6 hours, before considering if further antivenom is required. Always have adrenaline ready in case of anaphylaxis. Once antivenom is commenced, remove first aid.

Following completion of the first round of antivenom therapy, repeat all blood tests 3 and 6 hrs later. If the fibrinogen remains undetectable at 6+ hours, or has fallen further (in less severe cases), consider repeating the antivenom therapy and again reassess after 3 and 6 hrs. If, after the first round of antivenom, the 3 and/or 6 hr tests show a rise in fibrinogen, then do not give more antivenom, but instead repeat full blood tests after another 3-6 hrs. If these show continued improvement, then further antivenom is usually not required. If, however, these tests show a fall in fibrinogen, then further antivenom should be considered. Because of problems measuring low levels of fibrinogen it may be more appropriate to rely on the INR as an indicator of recovering fibrinogen levels. If the INR shows a trend towards normal (it doesn't need to have become normal) then this indicates recovering fibrinogen. This implies sufficient antivenom has been given.

If the first blood tests are normal, showing no sign of coagulopathy, then antivenom is not required, except if there is clear evidence of venom-induced renal failure or paralysis developing. Repeat blood tests after a further 2-3 hrs and 5-6 hrs in patients who have no initial evidence of coagulopathy, renal failure or paralysis. Should these show developing coagulopathy, then proceed with antivenom therapy, as discussed earlier. However, for those patients who appear well and in whom all 3 sets of blood tests are normal, observe them overnight and repeat tests the following morning, to ensure late developing envenoming is not missed. Never discharge patients in the first 12 hrs, just because they appear well and initial blood tests are normal. Though uncommon, late envenoming can occur.

All cases should have tetanus immunisation status confirmed and boosted, if required, but only once any coagulopathy has been fully reversed.

While defibrination coagulopathy is the major risk from brown snake envenoming, major haemorrhages are rare, except in cases with either recent trauma or surgery, or who sustain trauma after the bite, such as a blow to the head while collapsing from the bite. In the latter situation beware intracranial haemorrhage. Renal damage or failure is less common than defibrination, but is more likely if alcohol has been consumed and rarely can occur in the absence of major coagulopathy. If the only feature is a high or rising creatinine/urea, but the patient is not oliguric/anuric, then maintain IV fluids and check function twice daily. In many cases the creatinine may peak on about day 2-5, thereafter slowly return towards normal. If, however, it continues to rise or there is oliguric/anuric renal failure, then this should be managed as for other causes of renal failure. Sometimes such cases develop an apparent secondary "DIC", with thrombocytopenia. This is quite distinct from venom-induced defibrination seen in the acute phase of envenoming. In most of these cases there is a microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) and it is important to confirm this by looking for evidence of red cell fragmentation. Management of this condition, which has similarities to TTP, is evolving and controversies remain. Platelet transfusion is generally only appropriate if the level is extremely low and if there is active bleeding associated. The role of plasma exchange is unproven and uncertain. Conservative management may be the most appropriate in the majority of cases. In this setting MAHA may take days to a week or more to resolve. While there is usually renal failure it is often incomplete and haemodialysis can often be avoided. Paralysis is rare after brown snake bite and largely restricted to those cases where antivenom therapy has been delayed by many hours. Because these venoms contain both pre- and post-synaptic neurotoxins, once respiratory paralysis is established it is unlikely to be reversed with antivenom therapy. A prolonged period of respiratory support may be required.

Brown snake bites rarely cause significant reactions at the bite site, so antibiotic therapy should only be given if there is clear evidence of secondary local infection.

### Neurotoxins Channel Toxins

Textilotoxin : A potent presynaptic neurotoxin, Mol. Wt. 88,000, representing about 4 to 5 % of the crude venom. Southcott and Coulter (1979) ( Ref : R000072 ) report that it acts on the prejunctional terminal by selectively blocking the release of acetycholine after the appearance of the action potential. This blockage had no effect on the resting membrane potential of the muscle cells, nor was the nerve conduction altered. Sutherland (1983) ( Ref : R000007 ) reports that Textilotoxin had direct presynaptic actions and no appreciable effect on muscle or acetylcholine receptors. The presynaptic blockade was due to the PLA component acting on the axolemma. Tyler et al (1987) ( Ref : R000086 ) isolated 4 subunits : GuHCl-2, CM-1, CM-2 and CM-3 ( also referred to as A, B, C, and the two identical subunits D ) and showed that the N-terminal AA sequence of GuHCl-2 is homologous to that of the gamma subunit of taipoxin. Separation into subunits is reversible. Only one of the subunits has PLA2 activity. Coulter et al (1983 ) ( Ref : R000087 ) reports that the PLA2 activity of textilotoxin is quite low and that the CM-3 subunit has twice the specific activity of notexin. Specific activity was 56 ± 14 µmoles free fatty acids liberated / min / mg protein. Su et al (1983) ( Ref : R000422 ).

Pseudonajatoxin A : Barnett et al (1980) ( Ref : R000071 ) isolated this postsynaptic neurotoxin. It has 117 AA residues and a Mol. Wt. 12,280. It causes irreversible blockade by firm binding to the acetylcholine receptors.

### Haematological Haemorrhagins

Doery and Pearson (1961) ( Ref : R000023 ) showed the venom was low in direct haemolytic properties and PLA. Kellaway (1933) ( Ref : R000073 ) stated the venom possessed a strong, highly diffusable coagulation factor. Denson (1969) ( Ref : R000018 ) concluded that the coagulation factor was a complete prothrombin activator. Masci et al (1988) ( Ref : R000074 ) found that the prothrombin activator was a major component of the venom with a Mol. Wt. exceeding 200,000. They found it was related antigenically to the prothrombin activator of Oxyuranus scutellatus venom, able to activate citrated plasma, wartrin plasma, Factor V and Factor X deficient plasmas. Doery and Pearson (1961) ( Ref : R000023 ) showed the venom was low in direct haemolytic properties and PLA.

Procoagulant : Mol. Wt. about 80,000, specific activity 11,000 units / mg protein, > 80% inhibition by Dansyl-GGACK ( histadine active site label ) and leupeptin, > 50% inhibition by benzamidine, NANA ( N-acetyl neuraminic acid ) and DFP ( diisopropyl fluoro phosphate ) with aprotinin, neuraminidase, endoglycosidase, BAEE ( N-benzoyl arginine ethyl ester ), TAME ( N-tosyl arginine methyl ester ) and PMSF ( phenyl methyl sulfonyl fluoride ) without effect ( < 50 % ). Williams et al (1994) ( Ref : R000749 ).

Textarin : Mol. Wt. 50,000 to 53,000, isoelectric point pI = 5.5. P. textilis venom contains 2 different enzymes which convert human prothrombin into thrombin. Textarin is a serine protease containing a calcium binding molecule site and is strongly stimulated by phospholipid and calcium ions. A second activator, found in the supernatant of barium citrate adsorbed venom solution, activated prothrombin in the absence of any co-factor. Stocker et al (1994) ( Ref : R000759 ).
