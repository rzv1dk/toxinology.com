## Clinical Effects

Fatal outcome extremely unlikely

Boas and pythons are all non-venomous snakes, but this does not imply they are all harmless to humans. As a group, they rely on numerous sharp teeth to assist prey capture, followed by constriction to subdue prey. Depending on the size and nature of the prey, constriction can kill through suffocation or disruption, of vital internal organs, as a result of the immense pressure some of these snakes can apply in constriction. The risk to humans is therefore not from envenoming, which does not occur, but from mechanical and secondary effects of bites and constriction. While any sized boa or python is likely capable of inflicting a bite with multiple skin penetration from teeth, the probability of problems from constriction is dependent on the size of the human victim (smaller size, such as small children = higher likelihood of effectice constriction), the size of the snake (in general bigger snake = bigger risk), and the species of snake (some species are far more muscular and pose a greater risk at any given length of snake).

In practice, significant injuries from boas and pythons are almost certainly uncommon to rare, both in the wild, and probably in captivity. Fatalities caused by these snakes are exceptionally rare, and instances of people actually being eaten by large boas or pythons are very few indeed. Thus the most common problem likely to present relates to the local effects of the bite. With large numbers of long, thin, very sharp, pointed, recurved teeth at their disposal, a boa/python bite may result in numerous, sometimes deep tooth punctures, that cause both mechanical injury and innoculation of bacteria, from skin or the teeth. While local infection is a problem in only a minority of cases, it can be quite severe. Certainly local pain and swelling should be expected after any boa/python bite.

## Description

### Adult Length

1.50 m

Very large in length, heavy bodied python with a medium length prehensile tail. Can grow to a maximum of about 4.00 metres. Head is moderately short, chunky and distinct from neck. Snout is broadly rounded when viewed from above. Eyes are moderately large with vertically elliptical pupils. Dorsal scales are smooth without apical pits ( sometimes a few scattered scales with pits ) . First dorsal scale row is enlarged and about half the width of ventrals. Cloacal spurs present in males and females. Dorsal scale count ( 35 to 45 ) - ( 41 to 51 ) - ( 22 to 30 ).

### Coloration & Markings

Colour and pattern is highly variable throughout its range with several subspecies described. Some of these would appear to be only colour variants.

M. s. spilota : Dorsal surface body colour is usually blackish olive with cream to yellow spotted scales often grouped to form diamond shaped markings. Head dorsum and head laterally is similar to dorsal surface body colour with white, cream or yellowish labials barred with blackish olive. Ventral and subcaudal surface colour is white to yellowish with or without dark brown to black blotches.

M . s. variegata : Dorsal surface body colour is usually pale yellowish brown, pale brown, olive or olive brown with black edged, irregular, darker brown blotches and saddles that most often form complete crossbands on the body and tail. Head dorsum and head laterally is similar to dorsal surface body colour with dark brown to blackish markings that enclose pale ground colour to form pale spots. Head markings usually include a transverse interocular bar, transverse parietal bar and transverse occipital bar that are connected laterally, a longitudinal median bar extending from the snout and connected to the interocular bar and a lateral streak extending from the loreal region, passing through the eye and extending posteriorly to connect with ( or approach ) the occipital bar. Labials are distinctly paler, usually white to creamish often with blackish vertical sutures on supralabials beneath the eye and a blackish line on infralabials along the lip margin. Ventral and subcaudal surface colour is white to yellowish or yellowish brown with or without darker brown edging of many ventral shields and with or without a series of dark brown blotches on the subcaudal surface.

M. s. imbricata : Colour and pattern is quite variable. Dorsal surface body colour is usually greenish, olive or brown with numerous, irregular, black edged ( 1 or 2 rows of scales ) paler yellowish or yellowish brown blotches ( dorsolateral and lateral series ) sometimes fused vertebrally to form larger vertebral blotches or crossbars. The lateral series usually comprises longitudinally elongate blotches that often coalesce to form ventrolateral stripes. Pale scales within each blotch are usually immaculate whereas scales on interspaces outside of blotches are often black tipped. The extent of black tipping of interspace scales is highly variable and in some specimens the entire scales are black ( especially on the anterior body ) resulting in a black dorsal surface body colour and paler yellow or yellowish brown blotches. Head dorsum and head laterally is similar to dorsal surface body colour with symmetrical black markings that usually include a transverse interocular bar, transverse parietal bar and transverse occipital bar that may be connected laterally, a longitudinal median bar extending from the snout and a lateral streak extending from the loreal region, passing through the eye and extending posteriorly to connect with ( or approach ) the occipital bar. Labials are generally paler and cream to yellowish. Head venter, throat, ventral and subcaudal surface colour is cream to yellow often with a median and lateral series of black spots that may form 3 black stripes on the ventral surface.

### Head Scales

Rostral with a pair of deep groove-like sensory pits and as wide or wider than high, single pair of well developed internasals and in contact medially and followed by 1 or 2 pairs of shields that may be separated or in contact at midline ( no distinct frontal, at most a small centrally located frontal shield, supraoculars fragmented into small scales and posterior head covered with small and irregular scales, nasal usually semi-divided, numerous small scales in loreal region, about 9 to 12 circumocular scales ( excluding supralabials ), usually 12 to 14 ( 11 to 15 ) supralabials ( first 2 supralabials with deep slit-like pits, third supralabial similarly pitted or indented and usually 6th and 7th in contact with eye ), usually 17 to 19 ( 16 to 20 ) infralabials ( with about 6 to 8 with deep rectangular pits in a distinct groove and usually followed and / or preceded by an indented infralabial ( first pitted infralabial usually the 5th or 6th ) ) and mental groove bordered by about 5 to 8 gulars either side ( sometimes with small scales in groove ).

### Breeding

Oviparous with a clutch size of about 8 to 34 eggs. Hatchlings emerge when about 41 to 58 cm in length.

### Dentition

Usually 2 ( 1 or 2 ) premaxillary teeth, about 15 to 21 maxillary teeth, 5 or 6 palatine teeth, 9 to 15 pterygoid teeth and 17 to 23 dentary teeth.

### Habits

Nocturnal and mainly a terrestrial forager but often found in trees and bush.

### Prey

Feeds mainly on rodents and small mammals, but will feed on nestling birds and lizards.

## First Aid

First aid for bites by pythons, boas and other strictly non-venomous snakes (excluding non-front-fanged colubroid snakes).

1. Beware, for a large snake, constriction of more than one person is possible; if possible get several people to assist in controlling the snake. There are no absolute methods for disengaging a constricting snake, but as a starting point, it may be useful to start by unwrapping from the tail end first, progressively unwinding the snake from the patient. Be sure to have someone control the head of the snake, grasping it firmly on the neck, immediately behind the head, but again beware, as the snake may try to throw constricting coils around the hand, arm, or even body of the person grasping the head and may attempt to lever the head out of the person's grasp, allowing the snake to bite.
1. It is possible to break teeth off during this process; these lost teeth may act as a focus for secondary infection if not removed promptly. Often, just gripping the snake behind the head, while removing any coils around the patient, may induce the snake to open its mouth and disengage from biting. This is the preferred outcome. Trying to manually open the snakes mouth to disengage it from biting is very difficult, not least because of the loose connection between the right and left sides of the lower jaw, such that each moves independently.
1. Disinfectant may be used. Any jewelry, such as rings or bangles, should be removed from the bitten limb, in case major swelling develops.
1. In this setting, early medical attention is required, to assess for such injuries.
1. It is therefore advisable, if possible, to seek early medical attention, for update of tetanus immunisation (if required) and consideration of antibiotic treatment.

## Medical Treatment

Bites by boas & pythons do not cause envenoming, but can cause local pain, swelling, secondary infection, or rarely, more extensive injury from constriction. The wound should be washed thoroughly. If infection develops, commence targeted antibiotic therapy orally, or in more severe cases, IV. No antivenom is available, nor is it needed, because these snakes are non-venomous.
