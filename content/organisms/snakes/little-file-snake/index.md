## Clinical Effects

Good

File snakes are generally docile and reluctant to bite. They do have sharp teeth, so if a bite does occur, it could cause local mechanical injury, with teeth marks, pain and swelling. Secondary infection is possible. These snakes are non-venomous, so no envenoming, local or systemic, will occur.

## Description

### Adult Length

0.65 m

Medium in length, moderately to strongly stout bodied snake with a short, slightly compressed prehensile tail. Can grow to a maximum of about 1.22 metres. Head is small, short, barely distinct from neck and largely covered with small granular scales similar to the body. Snout is short and obtusely rounded when viewed from above. Eyes are located dorsolaterally, very small in size with round pupils. Nostrils are rounded, close together and face dorsally. Skin is loose or baggy ( but tighter than A. arafurae ), rough. Dorsal scales are keeled, small, granular, juxtaposed and tuberculate. Ventrals, subcaudals and anals are not enlarged or distinguishable. A distinct fold along the median ventral surface.

### Coloration & Markings

Dorsal surface body colour is pale grey, yellowish brown or orange brown with dull grey, brown or black crossbands that usually form rings on the ventral surface. Dark crossbands are broad vertebrally and become narrow laterally. Transverse markings on the ventral surface tend to become faded or less distinct. Head dorsum and head laterally is similar to dorsal surface crossband colour with some often indistinct, white, grey or yellowish spotting. Head venter, throat and ventral surface colour is paler grey or brown with invasion of crossbands. Subcaudal surface is usually darker brown to black with the pale dorsal bands not reaching underneath.

### Head Scales

Head is covered by many small, granular scales, about 8 to 15 interoculars ( counted in a line ) about 5 to 7 scales from nasal to eye ( counted in a line ), 13 to 22 supralabials separated from contact with eye by about 5 to 7 scales ( counted in a line ) and about 13 to 22 infralabials.

### Breeding

Viviparous with about 6 to 12 young per litter. Gestation period is about 6 to 8 months.
Young measure about 23 to 37 cm in length at birth. Data suggests that females reproduce only once every several years.

### Dentition

About 12 to 15 subequal maxillary teeth. About 5 to 7 pterygoid teeth.

### Habits

Nocturnal and aquatic snake. Excellent swimmer but helpless on land.

### Prey

Feeds on fish and crustaceans.

Sexual Dimorphism

Adult females attain slightly larger sizes than males.

## First Aid

First aid for bites by pythons, boas and other strictly non-venomous snakes (excluding non-front-fanged colubroid snakes).

1. Beware, for a large snake, constriction of more than one person is possible; if possible get several people to assist in controlling the snake. There are no absolute methods for disengaging a constricting snake, but as a starting point, it may be useful to start by unwrapping from the tail end first, progressively unwinding the snake from the patient. Be sure to have someone control the head of the snake, grasping it firmly on the neck, immediately behind the head, but again beware, as the snake may try to throw constricting coils around the hand, arm, or even body of the person grasping the head and may attempt to lever the head out of the person's grasp, allowing the snake to bite.
1. It is possible to break teeth off during this process; these lost teeth may act as a focus for secondary infection if not removed promptly. Often, just gripping the snake behind the head, while removing any coils around the patient, may induce the snake to open its mouth and disengage from biting. This is the preferred outcome. Trying to manually open the snakes mouth to disengage it from biting is very difficult, not least because of the loose connection between the right and left sides of the lower jaw, such that each moves independently.
1. Disinfectant may be used. Any jewelry, such as rings or bangles, should be removed from the bitten limb, in case major swelling develops.
1. In this setting, early medical attention is required, to assess for such injuries.
1. It is therefore advisable, if possible, to seek early medical attention, for update of tetanus immunisation (if required) and consideration of antibiotic treatment.

## Medical Treatment

Bites by these non-venomous snakes are uncommon, but can cause local trauma from sharp teeth, resulting in local pain and swelling, possibly secondary infection. The bite site should be cleaned, analgesia given if required, advise re secondary infection. If infection develops over next few days, give targeted antibiotic therapy.
