## Case Studies

Warrell DA (1995) Clinical Toxicology of Snakebite in Asia. In eds. Meier J. and White J., Handbook of Clinical Toxicology of Animal Venoms and Poisons. CRC Press : Boca Raton, pp 493-594.

At least eight other species of colubrid, other than those discussed above, have proved capable of causing mild envenoming, and it is likely that bites by many more species would produce similar symptoms. Most colubrids will have the opportunity to bite humans only when being handled and, under these circumstances, may be provoked to bite repeatedly or to sustain the bite long enough (and to "chew") so that venom can be injected by the back fangs or enter wounds made by enlarged posterior maxillary teeth. Herpetologists, snake catchers, those who handle snakes for culinary and medicinal purposes and people, often children, who keep snakes as pets, are therefore most likely to be bitten. Local pain ("stinging") and swelling with or without numbness, redness, lymphangitis and tender enlargement of local lymph nodes have been reported after bites by Balanophis ceylonensis, Boiga ceylonensis, B. dendrophila, Ahaetulla (Dryophis) nasuta, Coluber ravergieri, C. rhodorachis, Cerberus rhynchops, Enhydris enhydris, and Malpolon moilensis. Mild systemic symptoms such as headache and giddiness were reported after bites by Balanophis ceylonensis and Boiga forsteni.

Fritts T.H., McCoid M.J. and Haddock R.L. (1990) Risks to infants on Guam from bites of the brown tree snake ( Boiga irregularis ). Am. J. Trop. Med. Hyg., Vol. 42, No. 6, pp 607-611.

Case 1.
2 month old male(weight unknown) had been discovered in his bed in early hours of morning with a Boiga irregularis chewing on his arm and wrapped around his neck and (L) arm. Snake weighed 165g and was 1,488 mm. long. Presented at hospital 4hrs. 40min. later suffering respiratory difficulties. Treated with norepinephrine and placed in intensive care unit for further observations. Information on the symptoms experienced by the child is based on an oral report by the emergency room personnel who attended the child. The boy responded to the treatment and recovered sufficiently to be released from the hospital 2 days later.

Case 2.
2 month old female (weight 6.6 kg), sustained bite to palm of hand and dorsal and ventral surfaces of wrist, from a Boiga irregularis whilst in her crib. The girl's mother had noticed several tooth marks on the dorsal and ventral surfaces of the wrist and on the palm of the child's hand after responding to the child's cries at 2100 hrs. Hand was noticeably swollen. Search of the room revealed a snake, reported to be 5 ft. long, entering a crib where the girl's twin brother was sleeping. Snake was killed and taken along with the injured infant to the Naval Hospital on Guam. (The child had been put to bed at 1800 hrs. in apparent good health.)
On arrival: normal behaviour; pulse - 140 b.p.m; respirations - 43.
2 hours post bite: respiratory rate increased; pulse - 240 b.p.m. - shortly after, had a respiratory arrest with pulse rate of 70 b.p.m. Intubated and ventilated with oxygen - pulse rate returned to 240 b.p.m. There were copious upper and lower airway secretions which were removed by suction. Neurological examination revealed disorientation, spastic movements of the extremities, lack of response to vocalisations, and sluggish pupil response to bright light. Swelling extended from the hand to mid forearm. 3 days post bite, there were numerous blebs over the fingers and dorsum of the hand. Local signs resolved without evidence of necrosis or infection. The respiratory arrest and marked cardiac oscillations were judged to be critically serious with a guarded prognosis. The child spent 4 nights at the hospital and was discharged on day 6 post bite. The attending physician recorded the episode as respiratory failure with near cardiac arrest, possibly anaphylactoid reaction.

Case 3.
10 month old boy (weight 10 kg), sustained bite from a Boiga irregularis. Boy had awakened his parents at 0600 hrs. and was standing in his crib grasping a snake at mid-body with the tail wrapped around the child's neck and the anterior body wrapped around his leg. Snake was an adult male weighing 168g. and was 1,305 mm. in length. The child's (L) hand had tooth marks on the dorsal and palmar surfaces of the hand and on 4th and 5th fingers. The presence of saliva on hand and the large number of tooth marks suggest that the snake had attempted to swallow the hand. At the time of discovery, the hand was swollen in the region of the bite. The child lost use of the hand when the hand and arm became markedly swollen.
On arrival at Guam Memorial Hospital, 2 hrs post bite: (L) hand swollen.
3 hours post bite: swelling extended up the lower arm.
4 hours post bite: noted to be cranky; drooping eyelids; inability to stand upright or walk.
6 hours post bite: swelling reached elbow.
7 hours post bite: awake but limp - admitted to intensive care unit.
Parents noted improvement in child's disposition and attentiveness nearly 20 hours post bite. He was released from hospital the following day (Day 3 post bite). Arm remained swollen until Day 4 post bite.

Case 4.
A 4 week old male infant (weight 5.4 kg) experienced bleeding, localized swelling, and bruising on one hand after being bitten by a Boiga irregularis. The parents had been awakened by the cries of the infant sleeping between them and, upon noting the bite on the child's hand, searched for and found a snake (~ 1.370 mm. long) in the room. No medical attention was sought. By next morning, ~ 8 hours post bite, the child was lethargic and had droopy eyelids. Within a few days, no symptoms were evident.

"Many people on Guam believe Boiga irregularis is harmless and therefore do not usually seek medical attention for bites. Bites on children warrant careful interpretation. Parents may be more likely to seek medical help when a chid is bitten than when an adult experiences a similar bite. However, the large number of bites involving infants relative to older children and adults suggests a tendency for the snake to enter houses and attack sleeping children. The severity of the bite (i.e., the likelihood of enevenomation) is increased for small children by the child's small body size, the inability to escape or avoid the snake if it chooses to envenomate by chewing, and the inability to communicate the distress to adults or older children nearby. A bite suffered by an adult or active child handling the snake is likely to be a quick strike followed by the snake releasing, whereas the intentional bite associated with feeding involves holding on and definite masticatory movements facilitating injection of the venom into the prey. If such bites are occurring in the infants being bitten, the probability of serious envenomation is increased inversely proportional to the size and activity of the child. In several cases, parents have found snakes coiled around the neck and body, and chewing on the limbs and heads of children. Whether such attempts are stimulated by extreme hunger or by undetermined olfactory cues stimulating a feeding response is unknown.
Attention should be given to early detection of respiratory problems and associated neurological signs and symptoms by careful monitoring of patients with bites by Boiga irregularis. No fatalities are known to have been caused by this species, but the high numbers of snakes in close proximity to human residential areas on Guam contribute to the possibility of snakes biting infants."

## Clinical Effects

These snakes can inflict bites with mild to moderate local effects.

Brown tree snakes, currently considered non-front-fanged colubroids, lack defined hollow fangs (though may have enlarged mid- or posterior enlarged teeth that may or may not have grooves), but possess low pressure secretory glands that are variably termed, "Duvernoy's glands" (distinct from the venom glands in front fanged venomous snakes), or as "venom glands". Based on limited case experience this species is known to cause harmful effects in bitten humans (but note caveat below). These snakes can inflict bites with mild local effects such as local pain and swelling in adults and older children. Larger specimens may inflict bites with moderate local effects. However, experience with bites to infants from accidentally imported snakes on Guam suggests that at least in the very young child, envenoming can cause significant systemic envenoming, with collapse. True flaccid paralysis, coagulopathy, myolysis or renal failure does not occur.

Like any bite, including bites by humans, bites by these snakes may result in local oedema, pain, trauma from teeth punctures and potentially secondary infection and the clinical effects associated with this complication. Further, exposure to a series of bites over time, or to snake body fluids, faeces or shed skins in captive specimens, could at least theoretically, result in sensitisation of the immune system, such that subsequent bites might cause more marked local or possibly even some systemic effects, of an immune aetiology. These non-specific and non-toxin effects may appear similar to known effects of venoms following a bite by a known venomous snake, but their presence in a bite by this species should not be taken as significant evidence of the presence of "venom" in the oral/Duvernoys secretions produced by this species.

At this point in time we do not know the full content and potential effects in humans of contents of oral/Duvernoys secretions in this species of snake, therefore we cannot state, with certainty, the range of effects of secretions harmful or possibly harmful to humans might cause. Note that a prolonged bite, where significantly more oral secretions may be inoculated, might possibly result in more significant effects, on a simple dose basis.

Therefore bites by this species should be managed as for any standard animal bite, but with an additional expectation that local toxin effects might also occur for which no specific treatment may be available. In general treatment will be secondary and symptomatic. Though unlikely, systemic effects cannot be excluded. More vigilance in assessment is required if the bite was prolonged.

## Description

### Adult Length

1.40 m

Large in length, slender ( except the very large almost python like physique of Guam specimens ), laterally compressed body with elongate tail. Can grow to a maximum of about 2.0 metres in Australia, 2.3 metres in New Guinea and over 3.0 metres in Guam. Head is subovate, broad and very distinct from neck. Eyes are very large in size and prominent with vertical elliptical pupils. Dorsal scales are smooth. Vertebral scale row is enlarged. Ventral scales have a sharp ridge either side resulting in an arch-like cross-section which enhances its climbing ability.

### Coloration & Markings

Dorsal surface body colour is yellowish brown to bright reddish brown with numerous, irregular, narrow, dark crossbands in eastern Australia and cream to light reddish brown with numerous, irregular, broad, bright red or reddish brown crossbands in northern Australia. New Guinea specimens tend to be light brown to yellowish or red brown with darker, irregular, crossbands or spotting present or absent. Crossbands are at most weakly defined in east Australian and New Guinea specimens. In some specimens many of the scales are weakly black edged and / or patches of black interstitial skin. Head dorsum is usually similar in colour to dorsal surface body colour and becoming paler laterally. Supralabials are yellow usually with dark sutures. Some specimens, particularly the northern Australian specimens, have a distinct dark reddish or brownish bar extending from the snout, through the eye, to beyond the angle of the jaw. Ventral surface colour is usually cream to pale brown or salmon coloured in Australian specimens and pale yellow in New Guinea specimens.

### Head Scales

The usual 9 supracephalic head shields, rostral wider than high, single loreal, single preocular usually in contact ( or narrowly separated from ) frontal, 2 postoculars, usually 2 or 3 anterior temporals, usually 3 or 4 posterior temporals, usually 9 ( 8 to 10 ) supralabials ( usually 4th to 6th, occasionally 3rd to 5th or 5th to 7th in contact with eye ) and usually about 14 ( 12 to 16 ) infralabials with 4 or 5 infralabials in contact with anterior chin shields.

### Breeding

Oviparous with a clutch size of about 3 to 12 eggs. Incubation period is about 13 to 18 weeks. Hatchlings emerge when about 32 to 40 cm in length.

### Dentition

Opisthoglyphous dentition, possesses 3 pair of enlarged grooved fangs on the maxillary bone.

### Habits

Nocturnal ( or crepuscular ) and arboreal, but frequently forages for food on the ground. Tends to take shelter in tree hollows or fallen logs, caves or crevices and very occasionally under stones. A very aggressive snake if disturbed or provoked. It throws its forebody into a series of S-shaped loops and strikes swiftly, accurately and savagely often with the mouth agape.

Juveniles

New Guinea juveniles may be almost pink in colour with a translucent ventral surface.

### Prey

Feeds mainly on small mammals, birds, their chicks and eggs, small lizards ( and their eggs ) and occasionally small snakes. On Guam, this species has had a devastating effect on the native ground nesting and flightless bird populations.

### Habitat

Found in a wide range of habitats varying from rainforest and mangroves to both wet and dry sclerophyll forests and coastal heaths in both Australia and New Guinea, found also in montane forest ( up to about 1400 meters ) and cultivated gardens and near human village settlements in New Guinea. It has thrived on the small island habitat of Guam.

## First Aid

Clean wound

First aid for bites by non-front-fanged colubroid snakes likely to cause either no effects or only mild local effects.

1. After ensuring the patient and onlookers have moved out of range of further strikes by the snake, the bitten person should be reassured and persuaded to lie down and remain still. Many will be terrified, fearing sudden death and, in this mood, they may behave irrationally or even hysterically. The basis for reassurance is the fact that many venomous bites do not result in envenoming, the relatively slow progression to severe envenoming (hours following elapid bites, days following viper bites) and the effectiveness of modern medical treatment.
1. The bite wound should not be tampered with in any way. Wiping it once with a damp cloth to remove surface venom is unlikely to do much harm (or good) but the wound must not be massaged.
1. All rings or other jewellery on the bitten limb, especially on fingers, should be removed, as they may act as tourniquets if oedema develops.
1. The bitten limb should be immobilised as effectively as possible using an extemporised splint or sling.
1. If there is any impairment of vital functions, such as problems with respiration, airway, circulation, heart function, these must be supported as a priority. In particular, for bites causing flaccid paralysis, including respiratory paralysis, both airway and respiration may be impaired, requiring urgent and prolonged treatment, which may include the mouth to mask (mouth to mouth) technique of expired air transfer. Seek urgent medical attention.
1. Do not use Tourniquets, cut, suck or scarify the wound or apply chemicals or electric shock.
1. Avoid peroral intake, absolutely no alcohol. No sedatives outside hospital. If there will be considerable delay before reaching medical aid, measured in several hours to days, then give clear fluids by mouth to prevent dehydration.
1. If the offending snake has been killed it should be brought with the patient for identification (only relevant in areas where there are more than one naturally occurring venomous snake species), but be careful to avoid touching the head, as even a dead snake can envenom. No attempt should be made to pursue the snake into the undergrowth as this will risk further bites.
1. The snakebite victim should be transported as quickly and as passively as possible to the nearest place where they can be seen by a medically-trained person (health station, dispensary, clinic or hospital). The bitten limb must not be exercised as muscular contraction will promote systemic absorption of venom. If no motor vehicle or boat is available, the patient can be carried on a stretcher or hurdle, on the pillion or crossbar of a bicycle or on someone's back.
1. Most traditional, and many of the more recently fashionable, first aid measures are useless and potentially dangerous. These include local cauterization, incision, excision, amputation, suction by mouth, vacuum pump or syringe, combined incision and suction ("venom-ex" apparatus), injection or instillation of compounds such as potassium permanganate, phenol (carbolic soap) and trypsin, application of electric shocks or ice (cryotherapy), use of traditional herbal, folk and other remedies including the ingestion of emetic plant products and parts of the snake, multiple incisions, tattooing and so on.

## Medical Treatment

Bites by this species are not expected to cause medically significant effects and the only risk, probably small, is local secondary infection. Patients presenting with bites by these snakes do not require medical attention, other than to check for infection and ensure tetanus immune status. Patients should be advised to return if local symptoms develop, suggesting secondary infection.

Bites by this species are not expected to cause medically significant effects and no specific treatment is available, in particular no antivenom. Treatment is based on symptomatic and secondary treatment. Antibiotics should only be used if clinically indicated in cases where local infection is apparent, or non-sterile interference with the wound is evident, and preferably after swabs for culture/sensitivity have been taken, to ensure targeting of the organism is appropriate. In all cases tetanus immunisation status should be assured.

If the patient develops any evidence of systemic effects, not explained by secondary problems such as infection or allergic reactions, then further targeted investigations may be warranted, together with reconsideration of the diagnosis and the identity of the snake (was a potentially dangerous known venomous species misidentified as this harmless species?).

Like any bite, including bites by humans, bites by these snakes may result in local oedema, pain, trauma from teeth punctures and potentially secondary infection and the clinical effects associated with this complication. Further, exposure to a series of bites over time could, at least theoretically, result in sensitisation of the immune system, such that subsequent bites might cause more marked local or possibly even some systemic effects, of an immune aetiology. These non-specific and non-toxin effects may appear similar to known effects of venoms following a bite by a known venomous snake, but their presence in a bite by this species should not be taken as significant evidence of the presence of 'venom' in the oral/Duvernoy's secretions produced by this species and should not redirect management away from appropriate symptomatic and secondary treatments.

### Crude Venom

Crude venom : Contained only 15% protein and had low proteolytic activity and was not haemorrhagic in mice ( doses up to 200 µg ). The major lethal fraction, a single peak, ( LD50 = 7.3 mg / kg ip ( mice ) ) represented 12.5 % of secretion protein and contained 3 proteins, Mol. Wts. 12,500, 18,000 and 52,000. Mice injected with lethal doses of this fraction showed a slow onset of lethargy followed by prostration and respiratory arrest and necropsy was unremarkable. The two other peaks showing lethal activity were pooled ( scarcity of material ) and represented 9.4 % of secretion protein, two bands, Mol. Wts. about 14,500 and 17,000. The pooled LD50 was 3.7 mg / kg ip ( mice ). Mice injected with lethal doses of this pooled fraction showed tachypnea and "in-pouching" of the flank region, hypothermia and spastic tremors, prostration and excretion of dark urine. Large amounts of serosanguinous fluid in the peritoneal cavity, dark urine in the bladder and myofibre degeneration and necrosis was reported from necropsy. Weinstein et al (1991) ( Ref : R000660 ).
