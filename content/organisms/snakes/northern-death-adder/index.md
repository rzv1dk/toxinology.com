## Case Studies

Isbister G.K., Halkidis L., OLeary M.A. Whitaker R., Cullen P., Mulcahy R., Bonnin R. and Brown S.G.A. (2010) Human anti-snake venom IgG antibodies in a previously bitten snake-handler, but no protection against local envenoming. Toxicon, Vol. 55, pp 646-649.

A 60 year old male snake-handler who breeds a variety of death adders (Acanthophis spp.) was admitted to hospital on two occasions three months apart with death adder (Acanthophis spp.) bites. The patient had Type II Diabetes for which he took metformin. He had been bitten only by non-venomous snakes in the past.

On his first admission he was bitten to his left ring finger at 4.00pm by a common death adder (Acanthophis antarcticus) and immediately applied a pressure immobilisation bandage (PIB). Symptoms on arrival to the local hospital included: nausea, vomiting, diaphoresis and abdominal pain. He was managed symptomatically with metoclopra-mide, ondansetron and morphine, and given intravenous normal saline. No neurological signs or symptoms deveoped. He was transferred to a regional hospital and was assessed at 2.25 h post-bite and only had mild abdominal pain. He had a raised white cell count (17.3 x10exp.9/ L; neutrophils, 15.24 x10exp.9/ L, lymphocytes, 1.12 x10exp.9/ L), 3 h post-bite, but no other abnormalities. He was reviewed again 6 h post-bite and was asymptomatic and his PIB was removed. He was observed overnight and remained well so discharged home the next morning. His CK was 480 U/L 16 h post-bite.

Three months later he had a second bite, this time to the thenar eminence of his right hand by a Northern Death Adder (Acanthophis praelongus). A PIB was applied 15 min post-bite. He was given intravenous promethazine (25mg) for pruritus at a peripheral hospital prior to transfer and admission. On arrival to the emergency department about 2 h post-bite he complained of nausea, ''burning pain in his right hand and forearm. Fang marks were noted at the bite site, associated with local pain, erythema and oedema limited to the right hand. He had also developed a generalised fine erythematous rash. His examination was otherwise normal. He had a mild leukocytosis (white cell count, 13.6 x10exp.9/ L), normal biochemistry including a creatine kinase (CK) of 156 U/L [Normal range 46171 U/L]. Initial treatment included analgesia and antihistamines, and regular limb observations. The pressure bandage was removed 4 h post-bite with no change in his condition.
The patient was observed overnight and complained of increasing pain and swelling in the forearm and elbow 14 h post-bite. He was reviewed the following morning (22 h post-bite) and noted to have marked swelling and pain of the hand, forearm and elbow, but no neurological features of envenoming. Repeat laboratory investigations showed a significant rise in CK to 4770 U/L. His arm was elevated, analgesia provided (oral and intravenous) and close observation was continued because of the risk of myonecrosis. His CK remained elevated at 4690 U/L 6 h later.
The next morning, 46h post-bite, there was little change and the CK remained high on two further occasions (4450 and 4670 U/L). The use of antivenom was discussed with the patient after consultation with a clinical toxicologist and the patient consented to treatment. He was given 6000 units of CSL Ltd death adder antivenom, 55 h post- bite. He developed a mild reaction characterised by rash and itchiness, which was treated with a single oral dose of 25 mg promethazine. On the third morning (70 h post- bite), the patient stated he felt improved and the oedema, erythema and pain had decreased. The CK also decreased gradually to 4050 U/L and 3140 U/L at 63 and 72 h post-bite respectively. He was discharged with follow up. His condition continued to improve over the following days.

## Clinical Effects

Fatal outcome common in untreated severe cases

Some past literature has suggested that A. praelongus was the dominant death adder species in New Guinea, but this now appears unlikely, with A. laevis and A. rugosus being the extant New Guinea species. Therefore clinical experience with death adder bites in New Guinea probably does not apply specifically to A. praelongus. The following is based on mainland Australian experience with death adder bites. Death adder bites in humans cause predominantly post-synaptic neurotoxic envenoming, with progressive development of general flaccid paralysis, starting with ptosis, then ophthalmoplegia (initially partial, with diplopia on lateral gaze), dysarthria, fixed dilated pupils, drooling, difficulty swallowing, then generalised weakness of voluntary muscles in limbs, trunk, finally affecting the diaphragm, with respiratory difficulty and eventual respiratory paralysis. Without ventilatory support this will prove lethal. The time course from bite to first signs of paralysis (usually ptosis) is variable, from 30 minutes to several hours. Similarly, progression of paralysis may occur over 2-3 hours or may take more than 24 hours. Not all cases will be envenomed, but in the past 50% of cases developed lethal paralysis. Similarly, development of mild paralysis is not always followed by progression to full respiratory paralysis. Death adder bites are usually painful, with tight but visually unimpressive local swelling, generally without associated more extensive swelling or bruising, bleeding or blistering. The moderate sized fangs usually leave visible marks. Myolysis does not usually occur with death adder bites. While the venom contains an anticoagulant, coagulopathy is not a feature in envenomed humans. Renal failure is not seen, except if there has been a period of hypotension causing secondary renal damage. In common with most other snakebites, systemic envenoming may be accompanied by non-specific general symptoms such as headache, nausea, vomiting and abdominal pain. Neither local necrosis nor infection are a feature of death adder bites.

### Special Risk Groups
#### Children

Risk to children from local or systemic envenoming is similar in nature to the risk for adults, but a childs smaller body mass implies that the comparative venom concentration will be higher, so more severe envenoming is likely, the increased risk being inversely proportional to the size of the child.

#### Pregnancy

Potential risk to foetus from systemic envenoming, similar to risk to mother

#### Elderly

The higher incidence of pre-existing diseases in the elderly and associated frailty place them at higher risk should envenoming occur. The nature and extent of increased risk will largely be determined by the nature and extent of pre-existing disease and by any medications used to treat such disease.

## Description

### Adult Length

0.45 m

Small in length, short, moderately stout, stumpy bodied snake (but more elongate than A. antarcticus), with a medium length, slender rat-like tail terminating in a sharp, soft and curved spine. Can grow to a maximum of about 0.89 metres. Viper-like in appearance with a broad, triangular head, pronounced brow ridge and head is distinct from the very narrow neck. Eyes are medium in size with vertically elliptical pupils. Body and head scales are moderately keeled. Generally intermediate in rugosity of head shields (moderately to strongly rugose), keeling of dorsal scales, ventral and subcaudal scale counts when compared with A. antarcticus and A. pyrrhus. Dorsal scale count (19 or 21) - 23 (21) - 17 (19).

### Coloration & Markings

Variable in colour and generally darker than A. antarcticus with more prominent banding. Dorsal surface body colour is pale grey to dark grey, greyish brown, pale brown to dark brown or deep reddish brown, either uniform or with pale and dark, irregular, transverse bands, spotted or edged with brown to black. Tail tip is white, cream, yellow or black. Head dorsum is usually similar to dorsal surface body colour but darker. Supralabials are mainly brownish, sometimes with paler or darker sutures towards the lip margin. Eyes usually have a yellowish grey iris. Head venter is mainly white with dark brown to black pigment on mental and anterior infralabials. Ventral surface colour is white, grey or cream with some dark brown, dark grey or black flecking and often dark brown or black spotting on lateral edges of ventrals and outer edges of subcaudals.

### Head Scales

The usual 9 supracephalic head shields, rostral distinctly wider than high, free edge of supraocular is usually raised, loreal absent, single preocular in contact with nasal, usually 2 (sometimes 3) suboculars, usually 2 (sometimes 3) postoculars, temporals usually 3 + 3 (3 to 5 secondary temporals), 6 supralabials (3rd often in contact with eye, 5th and 6th the largest) and usually 8 infralabials. Head shields are rugose.

### Breeding

Mating occurs in spring (September to November), sometimes in autumn (March to May). Ovoviviparous, usually 12 to 24 young born in late summer peak period of February to March. Females usually breed every second year but may breed annually if food is plentiful and the is weather favourable.

### Dentition

Front fangs located at anterior end of maxillary bone (proteroglyphous). Average fang length is about 6 mm. Fang is followed by 2 or 3 solid maxillary teeth.

### Habits

Nocturnal, terrestrial and secretive. If disturbed they tend to rely on their camouflage and remain still. If provoked it will flatten its body in a coiled position and strike rapidly. If this fails it will resort to escape. It twitches its tail in leaf litter, close to its head, to attract potential prey to within striking distance. The rustling sound, insect like movement and pale coloured tail tip is an effective lure. Shelters in deep fixed leaf litter, usually near or under low bush or large boulders offering shade.

### Prey

Juveniles tend to feed mainly on small lizards, particularly skinks. Adults feed mainly on lizards, small mammals and birds and occasionally frogs.

## First Aid

Keep victim still, apply pressure immobilisation bandage & splint, do not wash bite area, transport to medical care

First aid for bites by Elapid snakes which do not cause significant injury at the bite site (see Comments for partial listing), but which may have the potential to cause significant general (systemic) effects, such as paralysis, muscle damage, or bleeding.

1. After ensuring the patient and onlookers have moved out of range of further strikes by the snake, the bitten person should be reassured and persuaded to lie down and remain still. Many will be terrified, fearing sudden death and, in this mood, they may behave irrationally or even hysterically. The basis for reassurance is the fact that many venomous bites do not result in envenoming, the relatively slow progression to severe envenoming (hours following elapid bites, days following viper bites) and the effectiveness of modern medical treatment.
1. The bite wound should not be tampered with in any way. Wiping it once with a damp cloth to remove surface venom is unlikely to do much harm (or good) but the wound must not be massaged. For Australian snakes only, do not wash or clean the wound in any way, as this may interfere with later venom detection once in a hospital.
1. All rings or other jewellery on the bitten limb, especially on fingers, should be removed, as they may act as tourniquets if oedema develops.
1. If the bite is on a limb, a broad bandage (even torn strips of clothing or pantyhose) should be applied over the bitten area at moderate pressure (as for a sprain; not so tight circulation is impaired), then extended to cover as much of the bitten limb as possible, including fingers or toes, going over the top of clothing rather than risking excessive limb movement by removing clothing. The bitten limb should then be immobilised as effectively as possible using an extemporised splint or sling.
1. If there is any impairment of vital functions, such as problems with respiration, airway, circulation, heart function, these must be supported as a priority. In particular, for bites causing flaccid paralysis, including respiratory paralysis, both airway and respiration may be impaired, requiring urgent and prolonged treatment, which may include the mouth to mask (mouth to mouth) technique of expired air transfer. Seek urgent medical attention.
1. Do not use Tourniquets, cut, suck or scarify the wound or apply chemicals or electric shock.
1. Avoid peroral intake, absolutely no alcohol. No sedatives outside hospital. If there will be considerable delay before reaching medical aid, measured in several hours to days, then give clear fluids by mouth to prevent dehydration.
1. If the offending snake has been killed it should be brought with the patient for identification (only relevant in areas where there are more than one naturally occurring venomous snake species), but be careful to avoid touching the head, as even a dead snake can envenom. No attempt should be made to pursue the snake into the undergrowth as this will risk further bites.
1. The snakebite victim should be transported as quickly and as passively as possible to the nearest place where they can be seen by a medically-trained person (health station, dispensary, clinic or hospital). The bitten limb must not be exercised as muscular contraction will promote systemic absorption of venom. If no motor vehicle or boat is available, the patient can be carried on a stretcher or hurdle, on the pillion or crossbar of a bicycle or on someone's back.
1. Most traditional, and many of the more recently fashionable, first aid measures are useless and potentially dangerous. These include local cauterization, incision, excision, amputation, suction by mouth, vacuum pump or syringe, combined incision and suction ("venom-ex" apparatus), injection or instillation of compounds such as potassium permanganate, phenol (carbolic soap) and trypsin, application of electric shocks or ice (cryotherapy), use of traditional herbal, folk and other remedies including the ingestion of emetic plant products and parts of the snake, multiple incisions, tattooing and so on.

## Medical Treatment

Death adder bites have a high potential for major envenoming (paralysis), so all cases require urgent assessment. At first sign of developing paralysis, give antivenom.

Death adder bite is characterised by local pain, minimal swelling and systemic flaccid paralysis (principally postsynaptic, but at least some species also have presynaptic neurotoxins which can cause failure of paralysis reversal with antivenom therapy; because this is variable, antivenom should always be used in an effort to reverse paralysis, but if no effect consider a Tensilon test (anticholinesterase) to determine if paralysis is reversible). Possibly as many as 70% of bites will develop envenoming, except for bites from captive specimens, where experience suggests a much higher rate of "dry bites". Except in this latter group, many cases will require antivenom and every case must be admitted.

On presentation, establish a good IV line, commence IV fluids, take bloods for initial tests ("extended coagulation tests" = PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen level***, FDP/XDP, platelet count; CK, urea, creatinine, WCC, K+), perform venom detection on the bite site (if uncertain of identity of snake). (*** fibrinogen level is only useful if it can be reliably measured to very low levels; many labs are unable to do this or may use indirect measurement and in this setting fibrinogen is not a useful test and could be omitted)Unless contraindicated by pre-existing medical conditions, give an IV fluid load (1L over 2-3 hrs in an adult; volume determined by weight in a child), then keep well hydrated thereafter (100-150 mL/hr in an adult), while carefully watching for fluid overload, monitoring fluid input and output. If a first aid bandage is in place over the bitten limb, leave on until the blood tests, venom detection and full examination are complete and results available. To perform venom detection cut away the bandage immediately over the bite area, to swab for venom. Keep the cut-away bandages, in case they are needed later for venom detection.

If the patient presents with envenoming clearly established, with evidence of systemic envenoming (developing flaccid paralysis, even just ptosis), then commence antivenom therapy prior to removal of first aid. Initial dose should be at least 1 vial* of CSL Death Adder AV IV, diluted up to 1:10 (less in children, to avoid volume overload), with adrenaline ready to treat anaphylaxis, should this occur. *In New Guinea, where antivenom is generally in short supply, high doses are not practical and in all but the most severe situations, only a single vial of antivenom is given.

It is probably advisable to repeat blood tests 3 hrs after completion of initial dose of antivenom therapy, but it is unlikely this will assist in a decision about further antivenom therapy. The key decider is the extent of paralytic signs. If these have progressed, then more antivenom should be considered. If the initial dose was only 1 vial, try giving 2+ vials as a second dose. If the initial dose was 2-3 vials (rare), the second dose should be 2+ vials. If there has been no progression of paralysis, but no improvement either, the decision is less clear cut. If the extent of paralysis is mot major (ie no respiratory paralysis, no significant glottic or tongue paralysis, no drooling), then waiting another 2-3 hrs may be reasonable. If, however, the extent of paralysis is major, then further antivenom should be given. Even though the neurotoxins are mostly postsynaptic, therefore theoretically reversible, in reality there is not always a good reversal response to antivenom, likely due to presence of presynaptic neurotoxins and occasionally high doses are needed (but see earlier note). As an adjunct to antivenom, or an alternative if antivenom is unavailable or supplies exhausted, consider using neostigmine and atropine, which have been shown to reduce paralytic signs (short-term only) in some (not all) patients with death adder envenoming. The mechanism is the anticholinesterase activity of neostigmine, which makes more neurotransmitter available to overcome the venom blocking the acetylcholine receptor on the muscle end plate.

For cases where there is no evidence of flaccid paralysis on examination, no antivenom is required at this stage, and first aid should then be removed, but continually reassess the patient, looking for development of ptosis etc and if this shows developing envenoming, treat with antivenom, as discussed earlier. Occasionally development of paralysis is very delayed, with first signs (ptosis) not evident until nearly 24 hrs post-bite (noted from Darwin area).

Death adder bites commonly develop both local pain and tight, but minimal swelling. Secondary infection is uncommon to rare, but if there is a strong suspicion of secondary infection with cellulitis, then antibiotic therapy, initially IV, is required, but in most cases of death adder bites, antibiotics are not required. Tetanus immunisation status should always be checked and a booster given when indicated.

## Venom

### Venom Components

- Acanthoxin B ( PLA2 enzyme ). PLA2 activity = 4.0 ± 0.2 µmoles / min / mg. Van Der Weyden et al (2000) ( Ref : R000903 ).
- P-EPTX-Ap1a ( Presynaptic neurotoxin ). PLA2 activity. Chaisakul et al. (2010) ( Ref : R009508 )
- Praelongins 2bIII, 2cII and 2cIV (PLA2 enzymes with antiplatelet activity). Sim (1998) ( Ref: R009514 )
### Crude Venom

Crude venom : Anticoagulant activity ( prolongation of clot formation ( prothrombin time ) for 221 ± 10.0 s ( A. antarcticus = 107 ± 0.1 s , A. pyrrhus = 195 ± 2.1 s ). Van Der Weyden et al (2000) ( Ref : R000903 ).

Crude venom : Using twitches in chick biventer cervicis muscle ( 3 -10 µg / ml ) and mouse phrenic nerve diaphragm 10 µg / ml ) A. praelongus venom was shown to be significantly less neurotoxic than A. antarcticus. In biventer muscle the venom abolished responses to exogenous ACh and carbachol, but not KCl indicating activity at postsynaptic nicotinic receptors. Venom failed to produce significant inhibition of direct twitches in chick biventer cervicis muscle. However, venom did initiate a significant direct contracture of muscle, indicating some myotoxic activity. Wickramaratna and Hodgson (2001) ( Ref : R000905 ).

### Average Venom Quantity

49.5 mg ( dry weight of milked venom ), Gopalakrishnakone and Chou (1990) ( Ref : R000004 ).
