## Antivenom

### Antivenom Therapy

Antivenom is the key treatment for systemic envenoming. Multiple doses may be required.

### Antivenom Dosage

Refer to dosage schedule for relevant antivenoms listed on this site

### Antivenom Reactions

Full range possible; see relevant section in antivenom pages

### Adverse Antivenom Reaction Management

Cease antivenom, resuscitate, adrenaline, IV fluids, then cautiously restart antivenom

## Case Studies

Trinca J.C., and Graydon J.J. (1971) The rough-scaled snake ( Tropidechis carinatus ). A dangerously venomous Australian snake. Med. J. Aust., Vol. 2, pp 801-809.

Case 1.
In 1954 , an experienced naturalist and snake collector was bitten on the back of the (R) hand by a specimen about 10 in. (25.4 cm.) long , when his attention was diverted while transferring the snake from one cage to another. In spite of on-the-spot incision, symptoms over the ensuing 24 hours included a stiff neck and headache, accelerated heart action, sweating and depressed breathing. No tourniquet was applied.

Case 2.
On May 15, 1956, a botany collector aged 52 years, weighing 145 lb, was bitten on the (L) thumb by a 3 ft. 6 in. (107 cm.) rough-scaled snake whilst on a field expedition with students. Despite the prompt application of a ligature and incision of the wound, unconsciousness occurred within four minutes. In the victim's own words, "after about one minute everything appeared grey, I walked with a staggering gait and then just collapsed". He was observed to have very shallow breathing and was frequently gasping for breath. His pulse was weak and he was cold and sweating freely. Difficulty in breathing persisted for about nine hours and giddiness for about 24 hours. He was admitted to hospital some hours after the bite in a semi-comatose condition. He was given 1,300 units of tiger- snake antivenine, and venesection of two pints of blood was performed. His condition improve, but later he collapsed again and was unconscious for half an hour. Subsequently he became nauseated and vomited. For several hours micturition was frequent and involuntary. His condition gradually improved and he was discharged perfectly well after two days in hospital. It should be noted that the dose of antivenine give in this case was less than half that recommended for a rough-scaled snake bite.

Case 3.
22 yr. old zoologist, sustained bite to (R) hand from a 20 inch long Tropidechis carinatus, whilst handling snake preparatory to preserving it. Snake had been caught the previous evening and identified by flashlight, as a non-venomous snake (Natrix mairii). Believing the snake to be non-venomous, he ignored the bite and took no first aid action. In 5 -10 min. he had developed diplopia and was nauseated. These symptoms made him realise his error and, after informing his companions, he suddenly lost consciousness. On admission to hospital he had a weakness and paralysis of the limbs, and dysphonia.
An intravenous injection of of 3,000 units of tiger snake anti-venine and a further 3,000 units distributed locally and intramuscularly , were given one half to one hour after the injury , and the symptoms cleared very rapidly. No other treatment was given except the local excision of an area of skin at the site of the bite.

Case 4.
51 yr. old male produced a 25 inch (63.5cm.) Tropidechis carinatus at a social club meeting in Sydney. He was noted for his practical jokes and believed the snake to be a baby carpet snake
(Morelia spilotes variegata) and, therefore, quite harmless. After being removed from a bag the snake struck him three times on the hand. The wound was scarified, but no tourniquet was applied. Death occurred within 5 minutes following a spasm of coughing. The snake was subsequently identified at the Australian Museum. At the inquest, death was attributed to the effects of snake poison while the victim was under the influence of alcohol.

Case 5.
23 yr. old snake attendant, sustained a bite to thumb while transferring a 2 foot (61 cm) Tropidechis carinatus from one cage to another. A tourniquet was tied around the thumb immediately and a 5% solution of potassium permanganate was injected directly into the punctures. The tourniquet was released after 20 minutes and no ill effects were experienced.

Case 6.
52 yr. old timber worker, was felling timber when he stepped around a log and was struck by a snake. The snake was killed and at the time was believed to be a tiger snake (Notechis scutatus). The victim was wearing sand shoes, and it was found that the snake had inflicted a bite on the foot, below the ankle. The snake was subsequently identified as a rough-scaled snake (Tropidechis carinatus) measuring 3 ft. 3 in. (99 cm.) in length and three and three quarter inches in girth. (On dissection a recently ingested, half grown rat was found in the stomach of the snake.)
The foot was washed promptly in a nearby creek; blood was pressed out of the wound, and a tourniquet was applied above the knee. Within a few minutes he had developed severe stomach cramps. On admission to hospital approximately one hour post bite, one puncture mark was visible on the outer side of the heel, and a slight abrasion nearby was attributed to the other fang, which had penetrated his sand shoe. A dry dressing was applied to the wound and a dose of 3,000 units of tiger-snake antivenine was given intravenously. At 11.15 a.m. he vomited 6 ounces of orange coloured fluid, after which he was given 50 mg of dimenhydrinate ("Andramine") by intramuscular injection. Later in the day he complained of stabbing pains in the region of the bite, not relieved by codeine given orally. His temperature was 99.2°F at 6 p.m., and he slept well with the aid of two "Nembudeine" tablets. The next day he was reported to be comfortable, although disinclined to eat. Urinalysis showed that the specific gravity was 1,020 with an albumin content of 1:2. He was discharged 2 days post bite, at which stage his urine showed a slight cloud of albumin and a specific gravity of 1,008. Apart from some difficulty in swallowing, which persisted for several months, all toxic effects had disappeared within 24 hours.

Case 7.
A snake collector was bitten on the (L) elbow , through a thin cotton garment, by a recently captured Tropidechis carinatus measuring 22 in. (56 cm). One fang was well embedded. Despite the application of a tourniquet and incision of the puncture wound, headache and giddiness occurred within two minutes, nausea in ten minutes, and blurring of vision in 20 minutes. Soon after the intravenous administration of 3,000 units of tiger-snake antivenine, 35 minutes post bite, these symptoms subsided. An injection of promethazine was given simultaneously with the antivenine.

Case 8.
A Forestry Department worker was bitten on the leg, at Binna Burra, Queensland, by a 3 foot Tropidechis carinatus. The snake was killed and subsequently identified by Mr David Fleahy. (On dissection snake's swollen mid-section contained a partly digested bush rat and a freshly swallowed adult doormouse possum.) The snake struck the victim first on the boot and then struck again managing to bite the leg through the trousers. A tourniquet was applied below the knee, and the wound was cut. He was admitted to hospital approximately 8 hours post bite. On admission he was asymptomatic. During his stay in hospital he showed no clinical signs of ill effects, and was discharged on the following day without requiring treatment.

Case 9.
A snake collector was bitten on (L) index finger by a 30 in. (76 cm) Tropidechis carinatus. Only one fang penetrated the skin. After receiving the bite, he dropped the snake and spent several minutes retrieving it, before applying a tourniquet and incising the bitten area. After incision the wound did not bleed, although there was a watery discharge. Ten minutes after the bite, he began to sweat. As the tourniquet was causing pain, it was removed after 20 minutes. Thirty minutes post bite, he was suffering severe headache and hypogastric pain. He had an urge to defaecate and thought he would lose control of his bowels. Forty-five minutes post bite, severe headache was still present, and he had difficulty in breathing, some blurring of vision and a generalised weakness with difficulty in standing and moving. He felt very cold and ill, but did not lose consciousness. His face was observed to be very pale, "drawn in and haggard". About an hour and a half post bite he started to feel a little better. His condition continued to improve during the next few hours, and he was able to sleep. The headache persisted for a few days, but he eventually made a complete recovery.

Case 10.
A snake colletor sustained a bite to (L) thumb from a 2 ft. 6 in. (76 cm.) long Tropidechis carinatus, whilst returning snake to a bag. One fang broke the skin, resulting in a scratch half an inch long. A tourniquet was applied immediately and the wound was sucked and incised. As no ill effects were felt after 25 minutes, the tourniquet was removed. Shortly afterwards . he noticed a tender swelling in the (L) axilla, and approximately one and a half hours later he developed a headache and felt nauseated, developed abdominal cramps and vomited. The vomitus contained blood, at first spots and then in clots. On admission to hospital, he could hardly walk, his speech was slurred and his vision blurred, and he was still vomiting bile and blood. In the patient's own words: "I could not make myself understood, I could hear well and knew what I wanted to say, but the words were slurred about my lips and no one could understand what I was trying to tell them." Two doses, each of 3,000 units, of tiger-snake antivenine were administered, the first on admission and the second about two hours later. His condition steadily improved and he was discharged the next day. There were no after effects except for a severe serum reaction 10 days later.

Case 11.
A collector sustained a bite to (L) hand from a 2 ft. 8 in. (82 cm.) long Tropidechis carinatus, whilst arranging specimen for photography. It had struck at the photographer's hand, but the victim did not realise he had been actually bitten until ten minutes later, when he felt a slight burning and itching in the region of the distal joint of the (L) little finger, which on examination revealed a small red spot resembling a scratched mosquito bite. 20 minutes after bite: lightheadedness and faintness; profuse generalised sweating; nausea; pallor; slurring of speech; severe headache; local burning and itching. Within an hour of injury, the whole finger had become swollen, and subsequently the swelling involved the ulnar half of the hand. The swelling and itchiness took two weeks to subside, but full sensation did not return to the distal part of the finger for several months. The victim was an experienced snake handler who had been bitten several times previously by snakes, but not by an Australian species. On this occasion, he was greatly concerned because of the rapidity of onset and the severity of the effects of an apparently trivial bite. 3 hours after injury his speech was still slurred but was improving, headache was still present and there was difficulty in concentration. Subsequently he vomited twice. He received medical aid in the form of an intravenous saline infusion, but no antivenine. Eight hours after the bite he was able to drive home and eat a normal meal, although the headache was still present and persisted until he went to bed. The next day he had completely recovered except for the local effects.

Case 12.
A 9 yr. old schoolboy, felt a sting on the (R) little finger when taking a corn cob out of a sack. On looking down he saw the head of a snake. His father subsequently emptied the sack and killed the snake, which was identified at the Queensland Museum as a 2 ft. 3 in. (69 cm.) Tropidechis carinatus. 10 minutes after being bitten, the boy said ,"I am going blind". he was observed to have a fixed stare, he was very pale and said he wanted to vomit. The parents promptly took the boy to hospital. No tourniquet or other first aid treatment was applied. On admission, 2 small puncture wounds were observed on (R) little finger; his pulse rate was 80 per min; respiration rate 30 per min.; he was drowsy and pale and he was complaining of pain in the abdomen. An intramuscular injection of 25 mg. of promethazine was given, and the area between the puncture mark was excised. 1 hour later: still complaining of abdominal pain and pain in both legs. Given 500 units of brown-snake antivenine.
2 hours later; observed to be sleeping; pulse - 108 b.p.m.; shallow respirations at 40 / min.
1 hour later: woke up and vomited.
1 hr. 30 min. later (6 hrs. post bite): passed very dark brown urine, which on testing with reagent strips, gave a positive result for blood and protein (100 mcg/ 100 ml.). Boiling revealed a protein content of 1:6 mg. Microscopy revealed two white cells, one epithelial cell, an excessive quantity of amorphous urates, and four granular casts per high power field. ## Clinical Effects

Fatal outcome common in untreated severe cases

Rough scaled snake bites are essentially very similar to tiger snake bites. Though the rate of envenoming is unknown, it may well be similar, at about 50%. These snakes are potentially lethal, even if fatalities have not been reported.

The bite site is likely to be painful, possibly with local mild swelling, erythema, bruising, maybe limited local necrosis around the bite sites, though this is not confirmed with present case reports. Systemic envenoming may develop quickly, possibly in 15-60 minutes after the bite, though may be delayed much longer. General symptoms may include headache, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, collapse, possibly convulsions (more likely in young children).

The hallmark features of rough scaled snake bite are flaccid paralysis, coagulopathy and myolysis.

The coagulopathy is of the defibrination type, which may develop very quickly, causing complete defibrination in 15-30 mins after the bite, if no first aid has been used. Classically there will be low or undetectable fibrinogen, grossly elevated FDP/d-dimers, prolonged to grossly prolonged INR/PT and aPTT, but usually a normal platelet count. Spontaneous bleeding is rare, bleeding gums are not reported, but there may be persistent oozing from bite sites and venepunctures. There is a potential for lethal haemorrhages. It responds well to antivenom, though large amounts may be needed. It is likely, though unproven, that untreated the coagulopathy will spontaneously resolve after 12-15+ hrs, leaving only elevated FDP/d-dimers as a marker.

The paralysis is classic flaccid paralysis of skeletal muscle, both presynaptic and postsynaptic, the former resistant to antivenom therapy. Fully established paralysis may take days, weeks or even months to resolve. The Australian record is held by this species, with 13 weeks on a ventilator. There is no likelihood that anticholinesterases will reverse this presynaptic paralysis.

Myolysis, as with tiger snakes, can be severe, with muscle pain, tenderness and weakness and CK levels > 100,000 IU/L in severe cases. Myoglobinuria is expected in such cases, with a potential for secondary renal damage or failure, secondary hyperkalaemia and the consequent risk of cardiotoxicity, potentially lethal.

## Description

### Adult Length

0.50 m

Medium in length, moderately slender to medium bodied snake with a medium length tail. Can grow to a maximum of about 1.07 metres. Head is broad and distinct from neck. Eyes are moderately large in size with round pupils. Brow ridge is moderately prominent. Dorsal scales longish and strongly keeled with a matt appearance.

### Coloration & Markings

Dorsal surface body colour is olive green, olive brown, brown or blackish brown with dark brown blotches forming transverse crossbands more pronounced anteriorly fading or disappearing posteriorly. Head colour tends to be similar but darker. Iris is blackish brown with a reddish brown rim. Ventral surface is cream, pale yellow or olive green usually with darker blotches.

### Head Scales

The usual 9 supracephalic head shields, frontal shield noticeably longer than it is broad, internasals present, loreal absent, preocular in contact with nasal, suboculars absent, usually 3 postoculars, temporals 1 + 2 and 7 supralabials ( 3rd and 4th in contact with eye, 5th and 6th the largest ).

#### Anal Scale (Single or Divided)

### Breeding

Mating occurs in spring and summer. Viviparous, usually about 10 ( range of 5 to 18) young born in late summer about every 2 years.

### Dentition

Front fangs located at anterior end of maxillary bone ( proteroglyphous ), 3 to 5 maxillary teeth following the fang. Fang length approximately 2.5 mm.

### Habits

Mainly nocturnal and terrestrial tending to diurnal on cooler sunny days. Shy and nervous disposition preferring to escape if disturbed. If cornered or provoked it raises the neck and forebody in an upright S position with the head focused on the intruder and may hiss in short bursts. It will make several short, rapid strikes before trying to escape. It is quite aggressive once disturbed, difficult to handle safely in captivity and will defend itself vigorously if threatened. Shelters in dense shrub vegetation, tree hollows and rotting tree crevices.

Juveniles

Adept climbers from birth.

### Prey

Feeds mainly on frogs, but will eat small lizards, mice and small birds.

Sexual Dimorphism

Males and females are of similar size.

## First Aid

Keep victim still, apply pressure immobilisation bandage & splint, do not wash bite area, transport to medical care

First aid for bites by Elapid snakes which do not cause significant injury at the bite site (see Comments for partial listing), but which may have the potential to cause significant general (systemic) effects, such as paralysis, muscle damage, or bleeding.

1. After ensuring the patient and onlookers have moved out of range of further strikes by the snake, the bitten person should be reassured and persuaded to lie down and remain still. Many will be terrified, fearing sudden death and, in this mood, they may behave irrationally or even hysterically. The basis for reassurance is the fact that many venomous bites do not result in envenoming, the relatively slow progression to severe envenoming (hours following elapid bites, days following viper bites) and the effectiveness of modern medical treatment.
1. The bite wound should not be tampered with in any way. Wiping it once with a damp cloth to remove surface venom is unlikely to do much harm (or good) but the wound must not be massaged. For Australian snakes only, do not wash or clean the wound in any way, as this may interfere with later venom detection once in a hospital.
1. All rings or other jewellery on the bitten limb, especially on fingers, should be removed, as they may act as tourniquets if oedema develops.
1. If the bite is on a limb, a broad bandage (even torn strips of clothing or pantyhose) should be applied over the bitten area at moderate pressure (as for a sprain; not so tight circulation is impaired), then extended to cover as much of the bitten limb as possible, including fingers or toes, going over the top of clothing rather than risking excessive limb movement by removing clothing. The bitten limb should then be immobilised as effectively as possible using an extemporised splint or sling.
1. If there is any impairment of vital functions, such as problems with respiration, airway, circulation, heart function, these must be supported as a priority. In particular, for bites causing flaccid paralysis, including respiratory paralysis, both airway and respiration may be impaired, requiring urgent and prolonged treatment, which may include the mouth to mask (mouth to mouth) technique of expired air transfer. Seek urgent medical attention.
1. Do not use Tourniquets, cut, suck or scarify the wound or apply chemicals or electric shock.
1. Avoid peroral intake, absolutely no alcohol. No sedatives outside hospital. If there will be considerable delay before reaching medical aid, measured in several hours to days, then give clear fluids by mouth to prevent dehydration.
1. If the offending snake has been killed it should be brought with the patient for identification (only relevant in areas where there are more than one naturally occurring venomous snake species), but be careful to avoid touching the head, as even a dead snake can envenom. No attempt should be made to pursue the snake into the undergrowth as this will risk further bites.
1. The snakebite victim should be transported as quickly and as passively as possible to the nearest place where they can be seen by a medically-trained person (health station, dispensary, clinic or hospital). The bitten limb must not be exercised as muscular contraction will promote systemic absorption of venom. If no motor vehicle or boat is available, the patient can be carried on a stretcher or hurdle, on the pillion or crossbar of a bicycle or on someone's back.
1. Most traditional, and many of the more recently fashionable, first aid measures are useless and potentially dangerous. These include local cauterization, incision, excision, amputation, suction by mouth, vacuum pump or syringe, combined incision and suction ("venom-ex" apparatus), injection or instillation of compounds such as potassium permanganate, phenol (carbolic soap) and trypsin, application of electric shocks or ice (cryotherapy), use of traditional herbal, folk and other remedies including the ingestion of emetic plant products and parts of the snake, multiple incisions, tattooing and so on.

## Medical Treatment

Rough scaled snake bites are similar to tiger snake bites, with a high potential for major envenoming. Systemic envenoming (paralysis, myolysis, coagulopathy) requires urgent antivenom therapy.

Bites by rough scaled snakes are characterized by local pain, slight swelling, local bruising, rarely superficial local necrosis around the bite site, and systemic flaccid paralysis (pre- & post-synaptic), myolysis (potentially very severe), defibrination coagulopathy (even without treatment this may resolve over 12-18 hrs after the bite), and secondary renal failure. Probably most bites will develop at least some systemic envenoming, so many cases will require antivenom therapy. Admit all cases of definite or suspected bites.

On presentation, establish a good IV line, commence IV fluids, take bloods for initial tests ("extended coagulation tests" = PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen level***, FDP/XDP, platelet count; CK, urea, creatinine, WCC, K+), perform venom detection on the bite site (if uncertain of identity of snake). (*** fibrinogen level is only useful if it can be reliably measured to very low levels; many labs are unable to do this or may use indirect measurement and in this setting fibrinogen is not a useful test and could be omitted)Unless contraindicated by pre-existing medical conditions, give an IV fluid load (1L over 2-3 hrs in an adult; volume determined by weight in a child), then keep well hydrated thereafter (100-150 mL/hr in an adult), while carefully watching for fluid overload, monitoring fluid input and output. If a first aid bandage is in place over the bitten limb, leave on until the blood tests, venom detection and full examination are complete and results available. To perform venom detection cut away the bandage immediately over the bite area, to swab for venom. Keep the cut-away bandages, in case they are needed later for venom detection.

If the patient presents with envenoming clearly established, or blood tests show evidence of systemic envenoming (defibrination coagulopathy with elevated PT/INR, aPTT, low or absent fibrinogen, often grossly elevated FDP/XDP; developing flaccid paralysis, even just ptosis; developing myolysis, elevated CK >1,000; secondary renal damage, elevated creatinine & urea), then commence antivenom therapy prior to removal of first aid. Initial dose should be at least 2 vials of CSL Tiger Snake AV IV, diluted up to 1:10 (less in children, to avoid volume overload), with adrenaline ready to treat anaphylaxis, should this occur.

Repeat blood tests 3 and 6 hrs after completion of initial dose of antivenom therapy, to determine if coagulopathy or myolysis has been arrested (fall in INR or rise in fibrinogen; no further or minimal rise in CK). If the CK has continued to climb dramatically, or the fibrinogen has failed to rise or has fallen further, give further antivenom (usually another 2 vials). If the 6 hr tests suggest improvement then wait 2-3 hrs and retest. If there is continued improvement, it is likely no further antivenom will be needed. If, however, there has been no improvement or worsening, give further antivenom. The role of antivenom in reversing myolysis is controversial, but given the potential lethality of severe myolysis, further antivenom therapy should always be considered.

For cases where initial blood tests show no sign of either myolysis (CK) or defibrination coagulopathy, and there is no evidence of flaccid paralysis on examination, no antivenom is required at this stage, and first aid should then be removed, but repeat tests 2-3 hrs and 5-6 hrs later and if these show developing envenoming, treat with antivenom, as discussed earlier.

For cases with myolysis, CK may rise to very high levels, >100,000 IU/L. This is accompanied by muscle pain, tenderness and weakness in most cases. There will also be gross myoglobinuria (red to black urine) and the risk of renal failure. Hyperkalaemia can develop secondary to myolysis and is more severe if there is secondary renal failure. It can cause cardiotoxicity and is potentially lethal and may be difficult to treat.

The defibrination coagulopathy is associated with major bleeding, but not commonly. Beware cases with recent trauma or surgery, or who sustain trauma after the bite, particularly a blow to the head, such as caused by a collapse shortly after the bite, as intracranial haemorrhage may develop. However, unlike brown snakes and taipans, rough scaled snake defibrination coagulopathy is only short-lived, reversing even without antivenom 12-18 hrs after the bite. For this reason, resolution of coagulopathy cannot be used to guide antivenom dose or effectiveness if >12 hrs have elapsed after the bite.

Flaccid neurotoxic paralysis is a major feature of rough scaled snake envenoming. Because there are potent presynaptic neurotoxins prominent in the venom, antivenom therapy is very unlikely to reverse paralysis already established. There is no value in continually giving antivenom in the hope of reversing established paralysis, but if paralysis is incomplete, then it is certainly worth giving antivenom to try and prevent progression of paralysis. In this latter situation it is likely that some progression of paralysis will occur over 1-3+ hrs after antivenom, caused by venom already bound to the terminal axon of the neuromuscular junction.

Rough scaled snake bites commonly develop both local pain and bruising, usually with slight swelling. Though local necrosis is quite uncommon, it is a known complication of tiger snake bites, but is usually limited to the skin immediately around the fang marks. More extensive necrosis occurs if first aid is left on for many hours or if a tourniquet is used as first aid. Secondary infection is uncommon, but if there is a strong suspicion of secondary infection with cellulitis, then antibiotic therapy, initially IV, is required, but in most cases of tiger snake bites, antibiotics are not required. Tetanus immunisation status should always be checked and a booster given when indicated, but not until any coagulopathy has been reversed.

### Management in Detail
#### Immediate Effects Management

Establish IV line, IV fluids, secure airway, maintain respiration if imperiled, treat hypotension (if present), do not clean wound until venom detection performed

#### Approach to Management

All cases should be treated as urgent & potentially lethal. Rapid assessment & commencement of treatment including appropriate antivenom (if indicated & available) is mandatory. Admit all cases.

#### Follow Up

Follow up all cases given antivenom therapy, checking particularly for serum sickness

#### Local Effects Management

Do not clean wound

#### Systemic Effects Management

Symptomatic care + IV fluids (as required)

#### Cardiotoxin Effects Management

Monitor for secondary cardiotoxicity due to hyperkalaemia induced by myolysis

#### Haematologic Effects Management

Give appropriate antivenom at first sign of significant coagulopathy or pathologic bleeding, monitor coagulation, be prepared to give further doses

#### Haematologic Other Effects Management

Not applicable

#### Important Laboratory Test

Coagulation (WBCT or PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen, FDP/XDP); FBC/CBP (platelets, Hb, WCC, absolute lymphocyte count); renal function (creatinine, urea); CK; electrolytes (K, Na, Cl, Glucose). (*** fibrinogen level is only useful if it can be reliably measured to very low levels; many labs are unable to do this or may use indirect measurement and in this setting fibrinogen is not a useful test and could be omitted)

#### Myotoxic Effects Management

Ensure good renal flow (adequate IV fluids), consider appropriate antivenom, consider alkalinising urine, watch for secondary renal damage & hyperkalaemia

#### Necrotoxin Effects Management

Local necrosis unlikely

#### Neurotoxic Excitatory Effects Management

Excitatory neurotoxic effects not likely

#### Other Neurotoxic Effects Management

Psychological support of paralysed patient

#### Neurotoxic Paralytic Effects Management

Support respiration, protect airway, intubate & ventilate if required, give appropriate antivenom at earliest sign of paralysis

#### Other Issues in Treatment

Not applicable

#### Other Specific Effects Management

Ensure tetanus immunisation, after any coagulopathy reversed

#### Renal Effects Management

Monitor renal function & output, consider IV fluid load, treat renal failure as required

### Haematological Haemorrhagins

Millar (1963) ( Ref : R000077 ) examined the effects of the crude venom in several animals and concluded it affected coagulation. Trinca et al (1971) ( Ref : R000078 ) reported the presence of a powerful coagulant and potent PLA. Mebs (1982) ( Ref : R000015 ) isolated a coagulant factor and a phospholipase A, which in combination only, produced haemoglobinuria in rats ( but not in mice ). Masci et al (1987) ( Ref : R000079 ) isolated a procoagulant ( Mol. Wt. 55,000 ) and proved it was similar to the procoagulant present in Notechis scutatus venom.

Prothrombin activating enzyme : Mol. Wt. approx. 41,500. This prothrombinase was dependent on Factor Va, phospholipid and calcium ions for its activity ( Type II class prothrombinase ). It had no PLA activity and did not cleave substrates BApNa ( N-~a benzoyl-L-arginine p-nitroanilide ), BTEE ( N-~a benzoyl-L-tyrosine ethyl ester ), azocollagen or azocasein indicating a lack of amidolytic, esterolytic and broad spectrum protease activity. Marsh et al (1997) ( Ref : R000836 ).
