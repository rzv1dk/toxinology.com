## Clinical Effects

Unknown. Most likely effect: Fatal outcome likely unless urgent & specific treatment instituted

This species of taipan is known from only a few specimens and there are no known bites reported. While this means there is no clinical data to draw on, similarities between other taipan species may indicate this new species will likely share most or all clinical effects of the previously known species. The following is based on this assumption.

Taipan bites are associated with a high rate of envenoming, probably due to several factors, including their large size, long fangs, high volume of available venom, highly toxic venom and accuracy of strike. In Australia, prior to availability of specific taipan antivenom, almost all known cases were fatal. However, while both the venom and clinical effects of inland taipans appears similar to other taipans, there are few recorded bites, all of which are in herpetologists, and none were fatal, though several were very severe. The following is based on general experience with taipans, rather than specifically inland taipans.

The bite site is variable in appearance, but fang marks are likely to be obvious, though local reaction is not always present. In some cases there will be local pain, swelling and bruising, but this need not occur for major envenoming to ensue. Systemic envenoming will develop quickly in most cases, unless effective first aid is used.

Systemic envenoming is associated with non-specific symptoms, including headache, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, collapse, convulsions (especially in children). In cases with significant envenoming, symptoms may develop within minutes and usually will be evident in the first 1-3 hours, though delayed envenoming may rarely occur.

The hallmark features of taipan bite are coagulopathy and neurotoxic paralysis. There is usually rapid development of defibrination coagulopathy, with spontaneous bleeding noted in most New Guinea cases, though not all Australian cases. Bleeding from the bite site, venepunctures and gums is common. Without antivenom it appears the coagulopathy may spontaneously resolve over 24+ hrs, but prior to resolution the patient is at risk of potentially lethal haemorrhage, notably intracranial bleeding.

Both presynaptic and postsynaptic neurotoxins are present in the venom, but the former dominate the clinical picture, causing severe flaccid paralysis which will not reverse with either antivenom or anticholinesterases and may require prolonged periods of ventilation (up to a month or more). Early signs, such as ptosis and ophthalmoplegia, may develop in 1-3+ hrs after the bite, occasionally more rapidly, uncommonly many hours later. Full respiratory paralysis is likely, though does not occur in every case. Bulbar palsy with drooling may force early intubation and ventilation long before full respiratory paralysis has occurred.

Though the venom is myolytic, and a few cases with moderate myolysis are reported, myolysis is usually either not present or only minor.

Secondary renal damage occurs in some cases and rarely may be permanent (renal cortical necrosis).

Cardiac abnormalities have been described as common, at least in New Guinea taipan bites. In 36 of 69 taipan bites in one New Guinea series (52%) there were ECG changes. Septal T wave inversion and bradycardias, including atrio-ventricular block were the most common findings. The ECG abnormalities found were the following (numbers of cases in parentheses); septal T wave changes (22); ST segment changes (3); sinus bradycardia (9); bradycardia with 1st degree atrioventricular block (2); bradycardia with left bundle branch block (1); intraventricular conduction defect (2); complete heart block (1); atrial arrhythmia's (2); frequent ventricular ectopics (1).

#### Children

Risk to children from local or systemic envenoming is similar in nature to the risk for adults, but a childs smaller body mass implies that the comparative venom concentration will be higher, so more severe envenoming is likely, the increased risk being inversely proportional to the size of the child.

#### Pregnancy

Potential risk to foetus from systemic envenoming, similar to risk to mother

#### Elderly

The higher incidence of pre-existing diseases in the elderly and associated frailty place them at higher risk should envenoming occur. The nature and extent of increased risk will largely be determined by the nature and extent of pre-existing disease and by any medications used to treat such disease.

## Description

### Adult Length

1.52 m

Large in length (mean 1.52 metres), slender and robust snake with a relatively long whip-like tail. Total length 1.3 to 1.66 metres in four adults specimens. Head is elongate, rectangular and distinct from narrow neck. Snout is broadly rounded when viewed from above. Eyes are moderately large in size with round pupils. Head and body scales are smooth and relatively glossy. Scales in the neck region are small. Dorsal scale count 24 - 21 - 17.

### Coloration & Markings

Dorsal surface body colour is medium tan-brown (turning darker in cooler months to chocolate brown). Towards the rear half of the body some scales have darker or lighter ventral edges that align to form discontinuous diagonal dark or light lines that produce a faint 'herring-bone pattern. Top and sides of the head from the level of the eyes to the rear of the parietals slightly lighter, more yellow brown, and the anterior scales, sharply paler beige-brown, the colour extending over the lower labials, chin shields and gular scales. Eyes are are black with a black iris.
Ventral scales light yellowish grey, with a weak scattering of small orange spots on some of the more anterior ventrals. Lateral-most extremities of all ventrals brown, matching the adjacent dorsal colouring. Each subcaudal scale becomes browner posteriorly.

### Head Scales

The usual 9 large supracephalic head scales, semi-divided nasal, loreal absent, subocular absent, preocular in contact with nasal and about twice as high as wide, 2 postoculars, temporals 1 + 2 ( temporolabial scale absent appears to be fused with 6th supralabial ), 6 supralabials ( 3rd and 4th in contact with eye, 5th and 6th the largest ) and 6 ( or 7 if a small scale is counted ) infralabials. Supraocular scale sharply shelved.

## First Aid

Keep victim still, apply pressure immobilisation bandage & splint, do not wash bite area, transport to medical care

First aid for bites by Elapid snakes which do not cause significant injury at the bite site (see Comments for partial listing), but which may have the potential to cause significant general (systemic) effects, such as paralysis, muscle damage, or bleeding.

1. After ensuring the patient and onlookers have moved out of range of further strikes by the snake, the bitten person should be reassured and persuaded to lie down and remain still. Many will be terrified, fearing sudden death and, in this mood, they may behave irrationally or even hysterically. The basis for reassurance is the fact that many venomous bites do not result in envenoming, the relatively slow progression to severe envenoming (hours following elapid bites, days following viper bites) and the effectiveness of modern medical treatment.
1. The bite wound should not be tampered with in any way. Wiping it once with a damp cloth to remove surface venom is unlikely to do much harm (or good) but the wound must not be massaged. For Australian snakes only, do not wash or clean the wound in any way, as this may interfere with later venom detection once in a hospital.
1. All rings or other jewellery on the bitten limb, especially on fingers, should be removed, as they may act as tourniquets if oedema develops.
1. If the bite is on a limb, a broad bandage (even torn strips of clothing or pantyhose) should be applied over the bitten area at moderate pressure (as for a sprain; not so tight circulation is impaired), then extended to cover as much of the bitten limb as possible, including fingers or toes, going over the top of clothing rather than risking excessive limb movement by removing clothing. The bitten limb should then be immobilised as effectively as possible using an extemporised splint or sling.
1. If there is any impairment of vital functions, such as problems with respiration, airway, circulation, heart function, these must be supported as a priority. In particular, for bites causing flaccid paralysis, including respiratory paralysis, both airway and respiration may be impaired, requiring urgent and prolonged treatment, which may include the mouth to mask (mouth to mouth) technique of expired air transfer. Seek urgent medical attention.
1. Do not use Tourniquets, cut, suck or scarify the wound or apply chemicals or electric shock.
1. Avoid peroral intake, absolutely no alcohol. No sedatives outside hospital. If there will be considerable delay before reaching medical aid, measured in several hours to days, then give clear fluids by mouth to prevent dehydration.
1. If the offending snake has been killed it should be brought with the patient for identification (only relevant in areas where there are more than one naturally occurring venomous snake species), but be careful to avoid touching the head, as even a dead snake can envenom. No attempt should be made to pursue the snake into the undergrowth as this will risk further bites.
1. The snakebite victim should be transported as quickly and as passively as possible to the nearest place where they can be seen by a medically-trained person (health station, dispensary, clinic or hospital). The bitten limb must not be exercised as muscular contraction will promote systemic absorption of venom. If no motor vehicle or boat is available, the patient can be carried on a stretcher or hurdle, on the pillion or crossbar of a bicycle or on someone's back.
1. Most traditional, and many of the more recently fashionable, first aid measures are useless and potentially dangerous. These include local cauterization, incision, excision, amputation, suction by mouth, vacuum pump or syringe, combined incision and suction ("venom-ex" apparatus), injection or instillation of compounds such as potassium permanganate, phenol (carbolic soap) and trypsin, application of electric shocks or ice (cryotherapy), use of traditional herbal, folk and other remedies including the ingestion of emetic plant products and parts of the snake, multiple incisions, tattooing and so on.

## Medical Treatment

Taipan bites have a very high lethality potential and require urgent assessment. Most cases will develop systemic envenoming, often rapidly, requiring antivenom therapy.

As this is species has been described relatively recently, we can only speculate about clinical effects and treatment, but it is most likely similar to the inland taipan. The following is based on that assumption:

There are still relatively few bites recorded by inland taipans, many of which have been severe, but none fatal. Both this limited clinical experience and venom studies indicate bites are essentially the same as for common taipans.

Bites by taipans are characterized by no to mild local pain, no or slight swelling, no or slight local bruising, and systemic flaccid paralysis (pre- & post-synaptic), myolysis (often absent, rarely more than moderate), defibrination coagulopathy, and secondary renal failure. Most bites will develop at least some systemic envenoming, so most cases will require antivenom therapy. Admit all cases of definite or suspected bites.

On presentation, establish a good IV line, commence IV fluids, take bloods for initial tests ("extended coagulation tests" = PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen level***, FDP/XDP, platelet count; CK, urea, creatinine, WCC, K+), perform venom detection on the bite site (if uncertain of identity of snake). (*** fibrinogen level is only useful if it can be reliably measured to very low levels; many labs are unable to do this or may use indirect measurement and in this setting fibrinogen is not a useful test and could be omitted)Unless contraindicated by pre-existing medical conditions, give an IV fluid load (1L over 2-3 hrs in an adult; volume determined by weight in a child), then keep well hydrated thereafter (100-150 mL/hr in an adult), while carefully watching for fluid overload, monitoring fluid input and output. If a first aid bandage is in place over the bitten limb, leave on until the blood tests, venom detection and full examination are complete and results available. To perform venom detection cut away the bandage immediately over the bite area, to swab for venom. Keep the cut-away bandages, in case they are needed later for venom detection.

If the patient presents with envenoming clearly established, or blood tests show evidence of systemic envenoming (defibrination coagulopathy with elevated PT/INR, aPTT, low or absent fibrinogen, often grossly elevated FDP/XDP; developing flaccid paralysis, even just ptosis; developing myolysis, elevated CK >1,000; secondary renal damage, elevated creatinine & urea), then commence antivenom therapy prior to removal of first aid. Initial dose should be at least 1-3 vials* of CSL Taipan Snake AV or CSL Polyvalent Snake AV IV, diluted up to 1:10 (less in children, to avoid volume overload), with adrenaline ready to treat anaphylaxis, should this occur. *In New Guinea, where antivenom is generally in short supply, such high doses are not practical and in all but the most severe situations, only a single vial of antivenom is given.

Repeat blood tests 3 and 6 hrs after completion of initial dose of antivenom therapy, to determine if coagulopathy or myolysis has been arrested (fall in INR or rise in fibrinogen; no further or minimal rise in CK). If the CK has continued to climb dramatically, or the fibrinogen has failed to rise or has fallen further, give further antivenom (usually another 1-2 vials). If the 6 hr tests suggest improvement then wait 2-3 hrs and retest. If there is continued improvement, it is likely no further antivenom will be needed. If, however, there has been no improvement or worsening, give further antivenom. The role of antivenom in reversing myolysis is controversial, but given the potential lethality of severe myolysis, further antivenom therapy should always be considered.

For cases where initial blood tests show no sign of either myolysis (CK) or defibrination coagulopathy, and there is no evidence of flaccid paralysis on examination, no antivenom is required at this stage, and first aid should then be removed, but repeat tests 2-3 hrs and 5-6 hrs later and if these show developing envenoming, treat with antivenom, as discussed earlier.

For those few cases with significant myolysis, CK may rise to mild to moderate levels, 10-15,000 IU/L. This may be accompanied by muscle pain, tenderness and weakness in severe cases. There may also be gross myoglobinuria (red to black urine) and the risk of renal failure. Hyperkalaemia can develop secondary to myolysis and is more severe if there is secondary renal failure. It can cause cardiotoxicity and is potentially lethal and may be difficult to treat. However, such severe myolysis is rare for taipan bites.

The defibrination coagulopathy is associated with major bleeding, but not commonly. Beware cases with recent trauma or surgery, or who sustain trauma after the bite, particularly a blow to the head, such as caused by a collapse shortly after the bite, as intracranial haemorrhage may develop.

Flaccid neurotoxic paralysis is a major feature of taipan envenoming. Because there are potent presynaptic neurotoxins prominent in the venom, antivenom therapy is very unlikely to reverse paralysis already established. There is no value in continually giving antivenom in the hope of reversing established paralysis, but if paralysis is incomplete, then it is certainly worth giving antivenom to try and prevent progression of paralysis. In this latter situation it is likely that some progression of paralysis will occur over 1-3+ hrs after antivenom, caused by venom already bound to the terminal axon of the neuromuscular junction.

Taipan bites uncommonly develop both local pain and bruising, sometimes with slight swelling. Though local necrosis is quite rare, it is a potential complication of taipan bites, but could be expected to be limited to the skin immediately around the fang marks. More extensive necrosis might occur if first aid is left on for many hours or if a tourniquet is used as first aid, though tourniquets are still widely used in New Guinea, yet taipan bites with necrosis are not reported. Secondary infection is uncommon to rare, but if there is a strong suspicion of secondary infection with cellulitis, then antibiotic therapy, initially IV, is required, but in most cases of taipan bites, antibiotics are not required. Tetanus immunisation status should always be checked and a booster given when indicated, but not until any coagulopathy has been reversed.

The venom also abolished responses to exogenous acetylcholine (1 mM, 30 s) and carbachol (20 uM, 60 s), indicating the presence of postsynaptic neurotoxins. Barber et al (2012) ( Ref : R009524 ).
Based on t90 values, the crude venom of the Central Ranges Taipan was more neurotoxic than other taipan venoms with the following rank order of potency: Central Ranges Taipan (15.1 ± 2.0 min, n=5, 3 ug/ml) > Inland Taipan (21.2 ± 0.7 min, n=3, 10 ug/ml) > Coastal Taipan (81.4 ± 6.6 min, n=4, 10 ug/ ml) > Papuan Taipan Saibai Island (86.0 ± 2.5 min, n=3, 10umg/ml) > Papuan Taipan Merauke (129.3 ± 14.9 min, n=3, 10 ug/ml) ( Ref : R009524 ).
