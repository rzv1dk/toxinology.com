## Summary

### Dry Bite

<10%

### Rate of Envenoming

>80%

### Venomous

High Risk

Severe envenoming likely, high lethality potential

## Antivenom

### Antivenom Therapy

Antivenom is the key treatment for systemic envenoming. Multiple doses may be required.

### Antivenom Dosage

Refer to dosage schedule for relevant antivenoms listed on this site

### Antivenom Reactions

Full range possible; see relevant section in antivenom pages

### Adverse Antivenom Reaction Management

Cease antivenom, resuscitate, adrenaline, IV fluids, then cautiously restart antivenom

## Case Studies

Flecker H. (1940) Snake bite in practice. Med. J. Aust., July 6, pp 8-13.

General paper on snakebite, including 3 cases of definite or probable taipan bite.
A 49 year old man was bitten on the right hand by a large snake, thought to be a taipan. He was initially treated by incision and tourniquet. At 1.1/2 hours post bite he developed nausea and vomiting, but remained otherwise well, except for the bitten limb. This remained with a tourniquet in place for 7.1/2 hours, leaving the arm swollen and numb. He remained drowsy and weak for several days, without paralysis being noted, and lost both taste and smell, neither of which returned to normal. He made an otherwise complete recovery. No antivenom treatment.

A 35 year old Aboriginal man was bitten on the leg while on a bicycle, by a snake thought to have been a taipan. Shortly thereafter he had a convulsion from which he recovered, but then commenced vomiting and 6 hours post bite had 3 more convulsions, and died. No antivenom treatment.

A 39 year old man was bitten on the leg while fishing. On presentation at hospital 5 hours later he was cyanosed, comatose, with severe paralysis and dilated pupils. Death occurred 6 hours post bite. No antivenom treatment.

Flecker H. (1944) More fatal cases of bites of the Taipan ( Oxyuranus scutellatus ). Med. J. Aust., October 7, pp 383-384.

Adult male bitten through trousers on right leg by 2.3 m taipan, applied tourniquet, and remained symptom free until 1.1/2 hours after the bite, when he suddenly had a seizure, followed by continual seizures and profuse perspiration, continuing until his death, 5 hours after the bite.

Adult male bitten on the leg, while working in cane fields, by a large brownish snake thought to be a taipan (never confirmed). Initially symptom free, but after a few hours (time not stated) became unwell, with vomiting, then paralysis of tongue and pharynx, then had convulsions and died.

A 20 year old female Aborigine was bitten at 11 pm while walking down a town street, by a large brown snake thought to be a taipan. She was bitten on the dorsal aspect of the foot, immediately ran for home, and collapsed unconscious 90 seconds later, and had convulsions. She died shortly afterwards. Death was reported due to asphyxia, with blood issuing from nose and mouth.

Reid C.C. and Flecker H. (1950) Snake bite by a Taipan with recovery. Med. J. Aust., January 21, pp 82-83.

A detailed case report with recovery. A 19 year old Aboriginal boy bitten on the right ankle while stacking timber. Snake killed and confirmed as taipan. Bite occurred through boot and thick socks, initially treated with tourniquet and incision. Between 15-30 minutes post bite was unable to open eyes, then developed nausea (?vomiting), dyspnoea, and drowsiness. By 4 hours post bite he was semiconscious, shocked, restless, and tiger snake antivenom was sought and used without great success. By the following day he was drowsy, restless, not shocked, breathing spontaneously, but with ptosis, facial weakness, dysphagia, and tongue paresis, although no ocular paresis was detected, and pupils were reactive to light. DTRs sluggish, and limb weakness was present. He made little improvement over the next 24 hours, and thereafter a slow improvement until on day 7 he developed serum sickness, from which he recovered over several days, being discharged 19 days after the bite.

Benn K.M. (1951) A further case of snake-bite by a Taipan ending fatally. Med. J. Aust., January 27, pp 147-148.

A detailed case report, ending fatally. A 20 year old amateur herpetologist was bitten on his left hand by a taipan (confirmed ID), the bite initially treated with a tourniquet. He remained well initially, with continued use of a tourniquet, and received tiger snake antivenom. About 2 hours post bite the tourniquet was dispensed with, and at 4.1/2 hours post bite he developed blurred vision, vomiting, headache, ptosis, and facial weakness. At this time the hand was red and swollen. Paralysis extended over the next 4 hours, with medial rectus palsy and severe ptosis, tongue paralysis, dysphagia, dysarthria, and some decreased intercostal movement, and by 9.1/2 hours post bite there was complete facial paralysis and minimal respiratory capacity, requiring attempts at artificial respiration (in a respirator). The patient resisted this treatment, and maintained some respiration overnight, but with developing cyanosis. At 23 hours post bite he had a rigor. At 26 hours post bite respiratory movements ceased, and he was again put in the respirator. At 27 hours post bite he died, possibly of cardiac arrest. At autopsy the only finding noted was an area of dry gangrene around the bite site.

Lester I.A. (1957) A case of snake-bite treated by specific Taipan antivenene. Med. J. Aust., September 14, pp 389-391.

A detailed report, with the first successful use of the newly available taipan antivenom.
A 10 year old boy was bitten on the right knee by a snake thought to be a taipan (not confirmed). He became unconscious almost immediately, recovering consciousness spontaneously within 30 minutes, at which time he was noted as pale, drowsy, without paralysis, and was given tiger snake antivenom. He remained apparently stable until 19 hours post bite, when severe ptosis, dilated pupils, marked drowsiness, abdominal pain, vomiting, and blood oozing from the wound were noted. The paralysis progressed such that by 24 hours post bite he had almost complete ptosis, almost complete ophthalmoplegia, dilated pupils almost unreactive to light, dysarthria, but no respiratory distress. At 24.1/2 hours post bite he received taipan antivenom, suffering an allergic reaction controlled by adrenaline. Within 1.1/2 hours a definite clinical improvement was apparent, though marked ptosis remained. The bite site wound continued to ooze. The opthalmoplegia and ptosis had virtually resolved by day 5 post bite, though the wound still oozed, this resolving the following day. He made a complete recovery.

An adult male, aged 19 years, suffered a bite from a taipan (confirmed ID), subsequently developing vomiting of "coffee grounds and bright blood", then ptosis, generalised weakness, and finally respiratory paralysis ending fatally.

An adult male, aged 52 years, suffered a bite from a possible taipan (ID not confirmed), remaining symptom free for 4 hours, then developing respiratory distress, dysarthria, and ptosis, progressing to death some 12 hours post bite.

Sutherland S.K., Coulter A.R., Harris R.D. and Halberstater L. (1980) Rapid death of a child after a taipan bite. Med. J. Aust., Vol. 1, No. 3, p 136.
A 4 year old boy sustained multiple bites from a taipan (positive venom ID), with rapid development of drowsiness, vomiting, then collapse, death occurring within 60 minutes. No specific findings were noted at autopsy, other than evidence of multiple fang punctures (12), and venom in tissues (2 mg/g of tissue).

Brigden and Sutherland, 1981

A 39 year old man presented with a one hour history of nausea and vomiting, at no stage believing he might have been bitten by a snake. Six hours later he developed fixed dilated pupils, ptosis, and progressive paralysis, ultimately requiring intubation and artificial ventilation. No snake bite marks were found. Coagulation studies revealed a severe coagulopathy. He was hypertensive. He was given 4 ampoules of polyvalent antivenom. Over the ensuing 12 hours he developed oliguria, and a grossly elevated creatine kinase (19,600 iu/L), but the coagulopathy reversed. He required ventilatory support for 19 days, and peritoneal dialysis for renal failure, but made a complete recovery, being discharged 27 days post bite. Subsequent testing of early urine samples was positive for taipan venom and myoglobin (serum samples negative for venom).

A 10 year old boy was bitten on his left middle finger by a large snake (later shown to be a taipan by venom ID from bite site). Pressure/immobilisation first aid was applied 40 minutes later. By 1.1/2 hours post bite he complained of nausea, the bite site was swollen and black, but no evidence of paralysis was found. At 4.1/2 hours post bite he commenced vomiting, had axillary adenopathy, and a marked defibrination type coagulopathy was noted. He was given polyvalent antivenom at 5 hours and 7 hours post bite, but by 10 hours post bite the coagulopathy had worsened, now with thrombocytopenia. At 13 hours post bite he was given one ampoule each of taipan and polyvalent antivenom. At 15 hours post bite the coagulopathy showed substantial resolution, and platelets were now normal. The child made a continued recovery, the swelling and discolouration of the bitten finger settling after 24 hours. Paralysis was not detected at any stage. Of important note in this case is testing for venom. Initially blood was tested for venom, without success. Later the bite site was tested, giving a positive result for taipan venom. Blood is often unreliable as a test sample for venom detection using the CSL Venom Detection Kit. Using the VDK to test blood for systemic envenomation is not advisable practice.

Covacevich, Pearn, White, 1988

A 29 year old female amateur herpetologist was bitten on her right thenar eminence by a 1.7 m newly purchased O. scutellatus. No first aid was used. On presentation to hospital about 15-30 minutes later she was vomiting, with frank haematemesis (note removal of wisdom teeth 2 days previously) and agitated, with developing ptosis. Shortly thereafter she lapsed into unconsciousness, despite normal BP (130 systolic), lasting 30-40 minutes, by which time she had been intubated and taipan antivenom commenced. Samples taken at 30 minutes post bite already showed severe defibrination type coagulopathy, and she continued to have significant blood loss from failed IVT insertion sites and her mouth over the next 3-4 hours until the coagulopathy was reversed by sufficient taipan antivenom (6 ampoules). Shortly thereafter, about 7 hours post bite, urine was negative for venom (VDK). Despite prompt aggressive specific antivenom therapy she developed severe paralysis requiring artificial ventilation for 4 weeks. Ptosis and ophthalmoplegia resolved after 2 weeks, with return of limb muscle power, respiratory muscle power being the last to satisfactorily resolve. A mild degree of myolysis was noted. No renal problems developed. After resolution of the coagulopathy, and not before, a mild thrombocytopenia developed, resolving within one week. Due to significant blood loss in the early stages as described above, a significant anaemia developed, requiring transfusion. Steroids were given to reduce the chance of serum sickness, which did not occur. Intermittent chest pains during the third to fifth week were thought possibly due to mild pulmonary embolism, hence the patient was commenced on a three month course of warfarin. Eventual recovery was complete. The patient was discharged after 5 weeks.

A 72 year old female amateur herpetologist was bitten on the right index finger by a 2.1 m O. scutellatus while feeding the snake. She arrived in hospital shortly thereafter, apparently well, and at 30 minutes post bite no coagulopathy was present. She was observed over the next 2 hours by staff who noted development of a headache and vomiting, but no paralysis, and as she also had a "flu" like illness, the significance of the symptoms was not appreciated. At 4 hours post bite, when further advice was sought, she was hypertensive (170 systolic), with mild ptosis, intractable vomiting, and tests revealed a mild coagulopathy. She was promptly treated with taipan antivenom (2 ampoules), with rapid resolution of the coagulopathy. However it was then noted she was anuric, and over the next 24 hours anuric renal failure was clearly established. This became her major problem, necessitating prolonged dialysis and hospital stay. Renal biopsy showed renal cortical necrosis. However, over a period of 6 months, she showed a slow resolution, with return of sufficient renal function to allow cessation of dialysis. At no stage was significant paralysis or myolysis noted, the coagulopathy was mild, and apart from the severe renal failure, this case would be described as minor envenomation for a taipan bite. As she had no past history of renal dysfunction the cause of the renal failure remains uncertain, though a primary effect of the venom would have to be considered. More likely is a combined effect of dehydration, influenza, possible mild shock, and a local angiopathic effect of the mild venom induced coagulopathy.

Southern D.A., Callanan V.I. and Gordon G.S. (1996) Severe envenomation by the taipan ( Oxyuranus scutellatus ). Med. J. Aust., Vol. 165, pp 662-664.

Series of 5 taipan bites, retrospective, (positive ID by venom detection) from Townsville General Hospital, Queensland, Australia from 1986 to 1994. Details for all 5 cases are given. All were adults, all developed neurotoxic features, but only 3 had coagulopathy. No cases developed thrombocytopenia, myolysis, but lymphopenia was either not assessed or not reported. 4 cases received antivenom within 2 hrs of symptoms of paralytic envenoming. 4 cases developed low serum magnesium levels.

Case 1. 19 yr old male bitten on foot, rapidly developed severe paresis, haematuria, coagulopathy, with partial defibrination (fibrinogen 0.7 g/l). Given 1 ampoule of CSL Taipan Antivenom with adrenaline premed 2 hrs after the bite. 15 mins later his paresishad resolved, except for diplopia (resolved by 24 hrs). He was also given 2 units of FFP, but continued to have a mild coagulopathy, so was given a further ampoule of taipan antivenom.

Case 2. 46 yr old female bitten on her ankle, immediately applied PI bandage, but by 1.5 hrs after the bite she was vomiting, had ptosis and diplopia. She was then given 1 ampoule of CSL Taipan Antivenom with adrenaline premed. By 3 hrs after the bite she was dyspnoeic and was intubated and ventilated. She also developed defibrination coagulopathy with bleeding from venepuncture sites. She was given a further 2 ampoules of antivenom and about 45 mins later had regained good respiratory effort, so was extubated. Ptosis, dysarthria and coagulopathy continued, so 2 further ampoules of antivenom were given. Full motor power only returned 4 days after the bite.

Case 3. 29 yr old female bitten on her toe, delayed presentation. 10 hrs later noted to have unsteady gait and diplopia. By 29 hrs after the bite, she had reduced power in both legs and lymphadenopathy. Given 1 ampoule of CSL Polyvalent Snake Antivenom with adrenaline premed. Her paresis recovered by day 4. No coagulopathy.

Case 4. 39 yr old male bitten on the wrist, immediately applied PI bandage. 50 mins later asymptomatic and no coagulopathy. First aid removed at 2 hrs and 15 mins later he developed diplopia, nausea, vomiting. Given 1 ampoule of CSL Taipan Antivenom with adrenaline premed. 4 hrs later he developed mild wealness of right forearm, but decreased diplopia. He also had haematuria and a defibrination coagulopathy (fibrinogen 0.5 g/l). He was given 3 units of FFP. His diplopia improved by 20 hrs, but there was incomplete resolution of his coagulopathy, so given 2 units FFP.

Case 5. 69 yr old male bitten on calf, immediately applied PI bandage. Promptly given 1 ampoule of CSL Taipan Antivenom with adrenaline premed. By nearly 2 hrs after the bite he had generalised paresis, ptosis, diplopia but no coagulopathy. First aid removed 3 hrs after bite, with prompt worsening of paresis, requiring intubation and ventilation. Given 1 ampoule of antivenom. A further ampoule given 3 hrs later. Extubated 11 hrs later. Though fibrinogen remained normal, he did develop mild elevation of d-dimers.

These cases showed varying degrees of envenoming, with predominance of neurotoxicity over coagulopathy. The doses of antivenom used were small and may not be enough to reverse coagulopathy rapidly. The authors suggest FFP should be used once sufficient antivenom has been given but fail to show they have given adequate antivenom. This recommendation for use of FFP should not be adopted.

King G.K. and Smith I.M. (1991) Taipan envenomation. Med. J. Aust., Vol. 155, p 850.

A single case report of a 28 yr old man bitten by a taipan (positive ID by venom detection) at Mount Molloy, near Mossman, Queensland, Australia in 1991. Poor first aid was used and at 15 mins he developed a headache, diziness and nausea. When first seen at a hospital, 40 mins after the bite, he had ptosis, ophthalmoplegia, profuse sweating, diziness, vomiting, headache and loin pain. He was given 1 ampoule of CSL Polyvalent Snake Antivenom. Whole blood clotting time was grossly prolonged. He was transferred to another hospital, where he was given a further 2 ampoules of taipan antivenom. He later developed serum sickness and cellulitis of the bitten foot.

## Clinical Effects

Fatal outcome likely unless urgent & specific treatment instituted

Taipan bites are associated with a high rate of envenoming, probably due to several factors, including their large size, long fangs, high volume of available venom, highly toxic venom and accuracy of strike. In Australia, prior to availability of specific taipan antivenom, almost all known cases were fatal.

The bite site is variable in appearance, but fang marks are likely to be obvious, though local reaction is not always present. In some cases there will be local pain, swelling and bruising, but this need not occur for major envenoming to ensue. Systemic envenoming will develop quickly in most cases, unless effective first aid is used.

Systemic envenoming is associated with non-specific symptoms, including headache, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, collapse, convulsions (especially in children). In cases with significant envenoming, symptoms may develop within minutes and usually will be evident in the first 1-3 hours, though delayed envenoming may rarely occur.

The hallmark features of taipan bite are coagulopathy and neurotoxic paralysis. There is usually rapid development of defibrination coagulopathy, with spontaneous bleeding noted in most New Guinea cases, though not all Australian cases. Bleeding from the bite site, venepunctures and gums is common. Without antivenom it appears the coagulopathy may spontaneously resolve over 24+ hrs, but prior to resolution the patient is at risk of potentially lethal haemorrhage, notably intracranial bleeding.

Both presynaptic and postsynaptic neurotoxins are present in the venom, but the former dominate the clinical picture, causing severe flaccid paralysis which will not reverse with either antivenom or anticholinesterases and may require prolonged periods of ventilation (up to a month or more). Early signs, such as ptosis and ophthalmoplegia, may develop in 1-3+ hrs after the bite, occasionally more rapidly, uncommonly many hours later. Full respiratory paralysis is likely, though does not occur in every case. Bulbar palsy with drooling may force early intubation and ventilation long before full respiratory paralysis has occurred.

Though the venom is myolytic, and a few cases with moderate myolysis are reported, myolysis is usually either not present or only minor.

Secondary renal damage occurs in some cases and rarely may be permanent (renal cortical necrosis).

Cardiac abnormalities have been described as common, at least in New Guinea taipan bites. In 36 of 69 taipan bites in one New Guinea series (52%) there were ECG changes. Septal T wave inversion and bradycardias, including atrio-ventricular block were the most common findings. The ECG abnormalities found were the following (numbers of cases in parentheses); septal T wave changes (22); ST segment changes (3); sinus bradycardia (9); bradycardia with 1st degree atrioventricular block (2); bradycardia with left bundle branch block (1); intraventricular conduction defect (2); complete heart block (1); atrial arrhythmia's (2); frequent ventricular ectopics (1).

### Special Risk Groups
#### Children

Risk to children from local or systemic envenoming is similar in nature to the risk for adults, but a childs smaller body mass implies that the comparative venom concentration will be higher, so more severe envenoming is likely, the increased risk being inversely proportional to the size of the child.

#### Pregnancy

Potential risk to foetus from systemic envenoming, similar to risk to mother

#### Elderly

The higher incidence of pre-existing diseases in the elderly and associated frailty place them at higher risk should envenoming occur. The nature and extent of increased risk will largely be determined by the nature and extent of pre-existing disease and by any medications used to treat such disease.

## Description

### Adult Length

1.20 m

Large in length, slender, cylindrical bodied snake with a relatively long whip-like tail. Specimens can reach a maximum length of over 3.30 metres. Head is elongate, rectangular and distinct from the narrow neck. Supraocular scale is sharply shelved exaggerating the long brow ridge ( resulting in the "scowling expression" ). From above, the eye is almost obscured from view. Eyes are relatively large with round pupils. Fangs are long for an elapid. They can exceed 12 mm in length. Dorsal scales are feebly keeled, particularly on the neck and vertebral region ) and quite glossy.

### Coloration & Markings

Dorsal surface body colour is yellow brown, light brown, dark brown to black, coppery red or olive. Head is usually paler in colour, particularly the anterior head region, labials and throat and often a pale yellowish cream colour. The relatively large eye has a blackish brown iris. The thick rim around the pupil is orange brown. Ventral surface is pale cream to yellow often with irregular darker or orange spots or flecks. They undergo seasonal colour variation ( lighter during summer months ).

### Head Scales

The usual 9 large supracephalic head scales, loreal absent, subocular absent, preocular in contact with nasal, 2 postoculars, temporals 1 + 3 ( some authors refer to the temporolabial scale wedged between the 5th and 6th supralabial as a primary temporal and report the formulae 2 + 2 ), 6 supralabials ( 3rd and 4th in contact with eye, 5th and 6th the largest ) and usually 7 ( or 8 if a small scale is counted ) infralabials. Supraocular scale sharply shelved.

### Breeding

Mating peak period is from July to October. Oviparous, usually about 15 ( range of 3 to 22 recorded ) eggs laid sometimes in two clutches, the first clutch in about 8 to 12 weeks followed by a second clutch some 6 to 10 weeks later again.

### Dentition

Front fangs located at anterior end of maxillary bone ( proteroglyphous ), A single maxillary tooth following the fang. Anterior end of palatine bone splint-like and toothless. Fang length approximately 10.0 mm ( varies between 7.9 to 12.1 mm ).

### Habits

Diurnal and crepuscular snake tending to nocturnal in hot weather. Extremely efficient hunter. Its bite is fast and accurate and its venom potent. It usually releases its prey after one or more jab-like bites and follows by sense of smell until the prey succumbs to the venom. Shelters in abandoned animal burrows ( especially those under tree roots and logs ), hollow or rotting logs and in deep leaf litter. Shy and alert species which will make a brisk escape if approached and is seldom seen. If cornered or provoked it will coil in loose, open loops with the forebody slightly raised and strike and release rapidly more than once if within range.

Juveniles

Unlike adults, juveniles do not release their prey after biting. They maintain their prey in the jaw until they can be ingested.

### Prey

Feeds mainly on small mammals, particularly rats, mice and bandicoots, but will also eat birds and small lizards. Its preference for rats attracts them to rubbish dumps and sugar cane plantations near human settlement in the eastern range of its distribution.

Sexual Dimorphism

Males tend to grow significantly larger than females.

## First Aid

Keep victim still, apply pressure immobilisation bandage & splint, do not wash bite area, transport to medical care

First aid for bites by Elapid snakes which do not cause significant injury at the bite site (see Comments for partial listing), but which may have the potential to cause significant general (systemic) effects, such as paralysis, muscle damage, or bleeding.

1. After ensuring the patient and onlookers have moved out of range of further strikes by the snake, the bitten person should be reassured and persuaded to lie down and remain still. Many will be terrified, fearing sudden death and, in this mood, they may behave irrationally or even hysterically. The basis for reassurance is the fact that many venomous bites do not result in envenoming, the relatively slow progression to severe envenoming (hours following elapid bites, days following viper bites) and the effectiveness of modern medical treatment.
1. The bite wound should not be tampered with in any way. Wiping it once with a damp cloth to remove surface venom is unlikely to do much harm (or good) but the wound must not be massaged. For Australian snakes only, do not wash or clean the wound in any way, as this may interfere with later venom detection once in a hospital.
1. All rings or other jewellery on the bitten limb, especially on fingers, should be removed, as they may act as tourniquets if oedema develops.
1. If the bite is on a limb, a broad bandage (even torn strips of clothing or pantyhose) should be applied over the bitten area at moderate pressure (as for a sprain; not so tight circulation is impaired), then extended to cover as much of the bitten limb as possible, including fingers or toes, going over the top of clothing rather than risking excessive limb movement by removing clothing. The bitten limb should then be immobilised as effectively as possible using an extemporised splint or sling.
1. If there is any impairment of vital functions, such as problems with respiration, airway, circulation, heart function, these must be supported as a priority. In particular, for bites causing flaccid paralysis, including respiratory paralysis, both airway and respiration may be impaired, requiring urgent and prolonged treatment, which may include the mouth to mask (mouth to mouth) technique of expired air transfer. Seek urgent medical attention.
1. Do not use Tourniquets, cut, suck or scarify the wound or apply chemicals or electric shock.
1. Avoid peroral intake, absolutely no alcohol. No sedatives outside hospital. If there will be considerable delay before reaching medical aid, measured in several hours to days, then give clear fluids by mouth to prevent dehydration.
1. If the offending snake has been killed it should be brought with the patient for identification (only relevant in areas where there are more than one naturally occurring venomous snake species), but be careful to avoid touching the head, as even a dead snake can envenom. No attempt should be made to pursue the snake into the undergrowth as this will risk further bites.
1. The snakebite victim should be transported as quickly and as passively as possible to the nearest place where they can be seen by a medically-trained person (health station, dispensary, clinic or hospital). The bitten limb must not be exercised as muscular contraction will promote systemic absorption of venom. If no motor vehicle or boat is available, the patient can be carried on a stretcher or hurdle, on the pillion or crossbar of a bicycle or on someone's back.
1. Most traditional, and many of the more recently fashionable, first aid measures are useless and potentially dangerous. These include local cauterization, incision, excision, amputation, suction by mouth, vacuum pump or syringe, combined incision and suction ("venom-ex" apparatus), injection or instillation of compounds such as potassium permanganate, phenol (carbolic soap) and trypsin, application of electric shocks or ice (cryotherapy), use of traditional herbal, folk and other remedies including the ingestion of emetic plant products and parts of the snake, multiple incisions, tattooing and so on.

## Medical Treatment

Taipan bites have a very high lethality potential and require urgent assessment. Most cases will develop systemic envenoming, often rapidly, requiring antivenom therapy.

Bites by taipans are characterized by no to mild local pain, no or slight swelling, no or slight local bruising, and systemic flaccid paralysis (pre- & post-synaptic), myolysis (often absent, rarely more than moderate), defibrination coagulopathy, and secondary renal failure. Most bites will develop at least some systemic envenoming, so most cases will require antivenom therapy. Admit all cases of definite or suspected bites.

On presentation, establish a good IV line, commence IV fluids, take bloods for initial tests ("extended coagulation tests" = PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen level***, FDP/XDP, platelet count; CK, urea, creatinine, WCC, K+), perform venom detection on the bite site (if uncertain of identity of snake). (*** fibrinogen level is only useful if it can be reliably measured to very low levels; many labs are unable to do this or may use indirect measurement and in this setting fibrinogen is not a useful test and could be omitted)Unless contraindicated by pre-existing medical conditions, give an IV fluid load (1L over 2-3 hrs in an adult; volume determined by weight in a child), then keep well hydrated thereafter (100-150 mL/hr in an adult), while carefully watching for fluid overload, monitoring fluid input and output. If a first aid bandage is in place over the bitten limb, leave on until the blood tests, venom detection and full examination are complete and results available. To perform venom detection cut away the bandage immediately over the bite area, to swab for venom. Keep the cut-away bandages, in case they are needed later for venom detection.

If the patient presents with envenoming clearly established, or blood tests show evidence of systemic envenoming (defibrination coagulopathy with elevated PT/INR, aPTT, low or absent fibrinogen, often grossly elevated FDP/XDP; developing flaccid paralysis, even just ptosis; developing myolysis, elevated CK >1,000; secondary renal damage, elevated creatinine & urea), then commence antivenom therapy prior to removal of first aid. Initial dose should be at least 1-3 vials* of CSL Taipan Snake AV or CSL Polyvalent Snake AV IV, diluted up to 1:10 (less in children, to avoid volume overload), with adrenaline ready to treat anaphylaxis, should this occur. *In New Guinea, where antivenom is generally in short supply, such high doses are not practical and in all but the most severe situations, only a single vial of antivenom is given.

Repeat blood tests 3 and 6 hrs after completion of initial dose of antivenom therapy, to determine if coagulopathy or myolysis has been arrested (fall in INR or rise in fibrinogen; no further or minimal rise in CK). If the CK has continued to climb dramatically, or the fibrinogen has failed to rise or has fallen further, give further antivenom (usually another 1-2 vials). If the 6 hr tests suggest improvement then wait 2-3 hrs and retest. If there is continued improvement, it is likely no further antivenom will be needed. If, however, there has been no improvement or worsening, give further antivenom. The role of antivenom in reversing myolysis is controversial, but given the potential lethality of severe myolysis, further antivenom therapy should always be considered.

For cases where initial blood tests show no sign of either myolysis (CK) or defibrination coagulopathy, and there is no evidence of flaccid paralysis on examination, no antivenom is required at this stage, and first aid should then be removed, but repeat tests 2-3 hrs and 5-6 hrs later and if these show developing envenoming, treat with antivenom, as discussed earlier.

For those few cases with significant myolysis, CK may rise to mild to moderate levels, 10-15,000 IU/L. This may be accompanied by muscle pain, tenderness and weakness in severe cases. There may also be gross myoglobinuria (red to black urine) and the risk of renal failure. Hyperkalaemia can develop secondary to myolysis and is more severe if there is secondary renal failure. It can cause cardiotoxicity and is potentially lethal and may be difficult to treat. However, such severe myolysis is rare for taipan bites.

The defibrination coagulopathy is associated with major bleeding, but not commonly. Beware cases with recent trauma or surgery, or who sustain trauma after the bite, particularly a blow to the head, such as caused by a collapse shortly after the bite, as intracranial haemorrhage may develop.

Flaccid neurotoxic paralysis is a major feature of taipan envenoming. Because there are potent presynaptic neurotoxins prominent in the venom, antivenom therapy is very unlikely to reverse paralysis already established. There is no value in continually giving antivenom in the hope of reversing established paralysis, but if paralysis is incomplete, then it is certainly worth giving antivenom to try and prevent progression of paralysis. In this latter situation it is likely that some progression of paralysis will occur over 1-3+ hrs after antivenom, caused by venom already bound to the terminal axon of the neuromuscular junction.

Taipan bites uncommonly develop both local pain and bruising, sometimes with slight swelling. Though local necrosis is quite rare, it is a potential complication of taipan bites, but could be expected to be limited to the skin immediately around the fang marks. More extensive necrosis might occur if first aid is left on for many hours or if a tourniquet is used as first aid, though tourniquets are still widely used in New Guinea, yet taipan bites with necrosis are not reported. Secondary infection is uncommon to rare, but if there is a strong suspicion of secondary infection with cellulitis, then antibiotic therapy, initially IV, is required, but in most cases of taipan bites, antibiotics are not required. Tetanus immunisation status should always be checked and a booster given when indicated, but not until any coagulopathy has been reversed.

### Management in Detail
#### Immediate Effects Management

Establish IV line, IV fluids, secure airway, maintain respiration if imperiled, treat hypotension (if present), do not clean wound until venom detection performed

#### Approach to Management

All cases should be treated as urgent & potentially lethal. Rapid assessment & commencement of treatment including appropriate antivenom (if indicated & available) is mandatory. Admit all cases.

#### Follow Up

Follow up all cases given antivenom therapy, checking particularly for serum sickness

#### Local Effects Management

Do not clean wound

#### Systemic Effects Management

Symptomatic care + IV fluids (as required)

#### Cardiotoxin Effects Management

Monitor for secondary cardiotoxicity due to hyperkalaemia induced by myolysis

#### Haematologic Effects Management

Give appropriate antivenom at first sign of significant coagulopathy or pathologic bleeding, monitor coagulation, be prepared to give further doses

Not applicable

### Myotoxins

Harris et al (1977) ( Ref : R000049 ) showed that Taipoxin was myotoxic. Sutherland (1981) ( Ref : R000025 ) and Sutherland (1981) ( Ref : R000026 ) demonstrated a marked elevation of plasma CK after 3 hours in monkeys injected with the crude venom. Brigden and Sutherland (1981) ( Ref : R000050 ) confirmed the experimental results when myoglobinuria was detected in a snake bite victim.

Harris and Maltin (1982) ( Ref : R000083 ) showed that taipoxin caused necrosis and degeneration of rat hind limb muscle by infiltration of phagocytic cells and edema. Maltin et al (1983) ( Ref : R000084 ) demonstrated that the plasma membrane of muscle cells was disrupted and the myofibrils disorganised. Only the basic alpha subunit by itself was myotoxic but that its potency was enhanced by the gamma subunit.

### Neurotoxins Channel Toxins

Taipoxin : Fohlman et al (1976) ( Ref : R000040 ) isolated this presynaptic neurotoxin, Mol. Wt. 45,600 and reported that it accounted for about 16% of the crude venom. It is a moderately acidic sialo-glycoprotein with 3 subunits. The alpha and beta subunits are 120 AA residues each cross-linked by seven disulfide bridges. The gamma subunit is 135 AA with 8 disulfide bridges. Only the alpha subunit has lethal toxicity, but the subunit is less toxic than whole Taipoxin. Fohlman et al (1976) ( Ref : R000040 ). The specific activities for taipoxin, ~a, b and ~g subunits were 0.4, 3.8, 0, and 0.7 µmoles free fatty acids liberated / min / mg protein respectively. Evidence suggests the ~a -subunit is responsible for the disruption of synaptic transmission. Fohlman et al (1979) ( Ref : R000082 ). The main effect on mouse phrenic nerve-diaphragm preparation was a presynaptic action to reduce spontaneous and evoked transmitter release. Kamenskaya and Thesleff (1974 ) ( Ref : R000417 ). Taipoxin caused omega-shaped indentations in the axolemma at the motor nerve end plates and suggested the toxin interferes with the recycling of synaptic vesicles resulting in the neuromuscular block. Cull-Candy et al (1976) ( Ref : R000031 ). Separation of taipoxin into the ~a, ~b ( ~b-1 and ~b-2 ) and ~g subunits revealed molecular weights 13,750, 27,000 ( both 13,500, each 120 AA residues ) and 18,500 respectively. The ~a subunit was most toxic ( lethal dose < 10 µg / mouse im ), ~g subunit moderately toxic ( lethal dose < 20 µg / mouse im ) and the ~b-1 and ~b-2 subunits were non toxic ( lethal dose > 50 µg / mouse im ). ~b-1 and ~b-2 subunits were shown to be identical for biological properties and found to be mitogenic, having neurotrophic activity on PC12 cells in culture similar to nerve growth factor. Lipps (2000) ( Ref : R000904 ).

Mebs et al (1979) ( Ref : R000041 ) found a postsynaptic neurotoxin.

Taicatoxin : [ Synonym : TCX ] An oligomeric toxin comprising an ~a-neurotoxin like peptide ( Mol. Wt. about 8,000, 65 to 68 AA residues, neurotoxic phospholipase ( Mol. Wt. about 16,000 ) and a serine protease inhibitor ( Mol. Wt. about 7,500, 63 to 67 AA residues ) linked by non-covalent bonds. It blocked the high threshold calcium channel current of excitable membranes in heart but does not effect the low threshold calcium channel current. The block is selective for calcium channels, reversible, does not affect single channel conductance, but only changes channel gating. 1 or 2 µg is lethal to 20 gm mice within 2 hours. Possani et al (1992) ( Ref : R000718 ).

### Haematological Haemorrhagins
