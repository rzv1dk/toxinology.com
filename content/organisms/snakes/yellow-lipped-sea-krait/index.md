## Clinical Effects

No case reports for this species, but related species have caused fatalities. Time from bite to death in confirmed sea snake fatalities ranges from 2 hours to several days, but most deaths occurred in the first 4-18 hours.

For most species of sea snakes there are no clinical reports of envenoming, but this may only reflect the paucity of documented cases, not necessarily a paucity of actual cases. In general, a significant number of sea snake bites will be "dry bites", but the rate of such trivial bites varies from species to species and for most is not documented. The larger the snake, the longer it has hung on, the more likely significant envenoming will occur. Multiple bites are also more likely to be severe. Sea snake bites are characterised by systemic rather than local envenoming. Bite marks are usually present and visible, sometimes quite extensive, involving not just fangs, but many non-fang teeth, however there is usually no major local reaction. Swelling, if present at all, is usually minor. Local necrosis, bruising and blistering do not occur. In those cases where sufficient venom has been injected to cause systemic envenoming, first symptoms and signs occur from one to 6+ hours post bite. In addition to non-specific symptoms such as headache, nausea, vomiting and abdominal pain, there may be one of two principal systemic envenoming effects. One or both may be present. The first effect is progressive post synaptic flaccid neurotoxic paralysis, usually first manifest as ptosis, then ophthalmoplegia (initially partial, with diplopia on lateral gaze), dysarthria, fixed dilated pupils, drooling, difficulty swallowing, then generalised weakness of voluntary muscles in limbs, trunk, finally affecting the diaphragm, with respiratory difficulty and eventual respiratory paralysis. Without ventilatory support this will prove lethal. The time course from bite to first signs of paralysis (usually ptosis) is variable, from 30 minutes to several hours. Similarly, progression of paralysis may occur over 2-3 hours or may take more than 24 hours. The second effect is systemic myolysis, presenting first as either muscle pain or red or brown urine (myoglobinuria), first seen usually several hours after the bite, but when present, will usually be obvious within the first 8-12 hours. As muscle damage progresses there is an associated muscle weakness, which may mimic mild paralysis. CK levels are elevated, sometimes to very high levels (>100,000 IU/L) and there may be hyperkalaemia, especially if there is also secondary renal failure, a common accompaniment of severe myolysis. Coagulopathy is not a feature of sea snake envenoming.

### Special Risk Groups
#### Children

Risk to children from local or systemic envenoming is similar in nature to the risk for adults, but a childs smaller body mass implies that the comparative venom concentration will be higher, so more severe envenoming is likely, the increased risk being inversely proportional to the size of the child.

#### Pregnancy

Potential risk to foetus from systemic envenoming, similar to risk to mother

#### Elderly

The higher incidence of pre-existing diseases in the elderly and associated frailty place them at higher risk should envenoming occur. The nature and extent of increased risk will largely be determined by the nature and extent of pre-existing disease and by any medications used to treat such disease.

## Description

### Adult Length

0.90 m

Medium to large in length, medium bodied snake. The neck is slender and uniform in size and only slightly compressed. The body is elongate and rounded ( only slightly compressed ). The tail is strongly laterally compressed posteriorly and distinctly paddle shaped. Can grow to a maximum of about 2.00 metres. The head is short, rounded and slightly distinct from the neck. Eyes are moderately small in size with round pupils. Iris is blackish brown. Body scales are smooth and imbricate. Ventral scales are smooth, broad and at least 3 times as wide as adjacent dorsal scales ( ventrals more than half the body width ). Some specimens may have a small but distinct lateral keel on each side. Some specimens ( not common ) have a median keel on posterior ventral scales. Dorsal scale count ( 21 to 23 ) - ( 23 to 25 ) - ( 18 to 21 ).

### Coloration & Markings

Dorsal surface body colour is grey or blue grey with 20 to 65 distinct black crossbands each of which usually encircles the body and a further 3 to 8 black crossbands on the tail. Tail tip is usually white. Head dorsum is black, with the snout, supralabials and a bar extending back above the eye to the temporals all whitish, pale greyish, cream or yellow. Ventral surface colour is cream or yellow and occasionally brownish.

### Head Scales

Head shields enlarged, symmetrical and unfragmented. Nostrils are small, valvular and positioned laterally on snout. Rostral not divided and usually higher than wide, nasals separated by internasals, no loreal, usually 3 ( sometimes 2 ) prefrontals ( a large azygous shield between the prefrontals ), single preocular, 2 postoculars, temporals usually 1 + 2 ( occasionally 2 + 2 or 1 + 3 ), usually 7 ( 6 to 8 ) supralabials ( usually 3rd and 4th in contact with eye ) and 5 to 10 infralabials.

## First Aid

Keep victim still, apply pressure immobilisation bandage & splint, gently wash bite area, transport to medical care

First aid for bites by Elapid snakes which do not cause significant injury at the bite site (see Comments for partial listing), but which may have the potential to cause significant general (systemic) effects, such as paralysis, muscle damage, or bleeding.

1. After ensuring the patient and onlookers have moved out of range of further strikes by the snake, the bitten person should be reassured and persuaded to lie down and remain still. Many will be terrified, fearing sudden death and, in this mood, they may behave irrationally or even hysterically. The basis for reassurance is the fact that many venomous bites do not result in envenoming, the relatively slow progression to severe envenoming (hours following elapid bites, days following viper bites) and the effectiveness of modern medical treatment.
1. The bite wound should not be tampered with in any way. Wiping it once with a damp cloth to remove surface venom is unlikely to do much harm (or good) but the wound must not be massaged. For Australian snakes only, do not wash or clean the wound in any way, as this may interfere with later venom detection once in a hospital.
1. All rings or other jewellery on the bitten limb, especially on fingers, should be removed, as they may act as tourniquets if oedema develops.
1. If the bite is on a limb, a broad bandage (even torn strips of clothing or pantyhose) should be applied over the bitten area at moderate pressure (as for a sprain; not so tight circulation is impaired), then extended to cover as much of the bitten limb as possible, including fingers or toes, going over the top of clothing rather than risking excessive limb movement by removing clothing. The bitten limb should then be immobilised as effectively as possible using an extemporised splint or sling.
1. If there is any impairment of vital functions, such as problems with respiration, airway, circulation, heart function, these must be supported as a priority. In particular, for bites causing flaccid paralysis, including respiratory paralysis, both airway and respiration may be impaired, requiring urgent and prolonged treatment, which may include the mouth to mask (mouth to mouth) technique of expired air transfer. Seek urgent medical attention.
1. Do not use Tourniquets, cut, suck or scarify the wound or apply chemicals or electric shock.
1. Avoid peroral intake, absolutely no alcohol. No sedatives outside hospital. If there will be considerable delay before reaching medical aid, measured in several hours to days, then give clear fluids by mouth to prevent dehydration.
1. If the offending snake has been killed it should be brought with the patient for identification (only relevant in areas where there are more than one naturally occurring venomous snake species), but be careful to avoid touching the head, as even a dead snake can envenom. No attempt should be made to pursue the snake into the undergrowth as this will risk further bites.
1. The snakebite victim should be transported as quickly and as passively as possible to the nearest place where they can be seen by a medically-trained person (health station, dispensary, clinic or hospital). The bitten limb must not be exercised as muscular contraction will promote systemic absorption of venom. If no motor vehicle or boat is available, the patient can be carried on a stretcher or hurdle, on the pillion or crossbar of a bicycle or on someone's back.
1. Most traditional, and many of the more recently fashionable, first aid measures are useless and potentially dangerous. These include local cauterization, incision, excision, amputation, suction by mouth, vacuum pump or syringe, combined incision and suction ("venom-ex" apparatus), injection or instillation of compounds such as potassium permanganate, phenol (carbolic soap) and trypsin, application of electric shocks or ice (cryotherapy), use of traditional herbal, folk and other remedies including the ingestion of emetic plant products and parts of the snake, multiple incisions, tattooing and so on.

## Medical Treatment

Sea snake bites vary from trivial to lethal envenoming, so urgently assess all cases, looking especially for paralysis and/or myolysis. These usually develop in first 6 hrs. They require good hydration/renal flow, respiratory support (for respiratory paralysis), IV antivenom (CSL Sea Snake AV).

While most sea snake bites, particularly by smaller species, are likely to be minor, severe, even lethal envenoming can occur. All cases should be managed as potentially lethal, requiring urgent assessment & admission.

Insert an IV line and give an IV fluid load. Monitor fluid input/output, looking for evidence of renal failure and myolysis (red or black/brown urine, positive for haemoglobin). Ensure good renal flow. Alkalinisation of urine is advocated by some, if there is myolysis, to reduce the chance of secondary renal damage due to myoglobinuria, but this is an unproven treatment. Carefully monitor for evidence of developing paralysis or myolysis, if the patient is initially well. At the first sign of either paralysis or myolysis, consider giving IV antivenom. CSL Sea Snake AV is the preferred choice, with an initial dose of 1-3 vials, depending on degree of envenoming. In the absence of this AV, try either CSL Tiger Snake or Polyvalent AV, at a ratio of 3 vials of either for each 1 vial of Sea Snake AV that would have been used. If the patient already has established major paralysis, requiring intubation & ventilation to support respiration, higher doses of AV may be needed. Be prepared to give more than 3 vials of Sea Snake AV, if the paralysis fails to reverse with the initial dose of 3 vials. Also consider performing a tensilon test, to determine if the postsynaptic paralysis will respond to anticholinesterases. The latter may reduce, short term, the extent of paralysis, but should only be considered as an adjunct to antivenom, not as a replacement. AV remains the key treatment for envenoming.

In most cases, at least the first mild signs of envenoming will be present by 6 hrs post bite (providing no effective first aid has been used). However, because later development of envenoming is possible, it is advisable to observe all cases for a minimum of 18 hrs post bite. If initial CK levels are normal, ensure repeat testing is performed, to pick up late developing myolysis.
