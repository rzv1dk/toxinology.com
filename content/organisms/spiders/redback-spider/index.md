Red-backed spider antivenom

IAuCSL05 by CSL
#### Description

Salt precipitated, pepsin digested. Liquid final product 0.76 to 1.1 ml. 500 units / ampoule. 1 unit neutralizes venom from one spider in vitro.

#### Comments

In the past there has been a preference for IM administration of this antivenom. IM absorbtion of antivenom is generally considered poor. Therefore IV administration of this antivenom may give a more rapid therapeutic response, but safety of IV administration versus IM has not been fully established. Nevertheless, many Australian hospital emergency departments now routinely give this antivenom IV, not IM.

#### Recommended Dose

2 vials. Further doses of 2 vials each may be required, up to 6 vials total.

## Case Studies

Couser G.A. and Wilkes G.J. (1997) A red-back spider bite in a lymphoedematous arm. Med. J. Aust., Vol. 166, pp 587-588.

Case Report.
55 yr. old female presented to hospital for follow-up, after Patey's mastectomy with full axillary clearance two and a half years previously had left her with lymphoedema of (R) arm. On her way a small black spider with a dash of red on its back climbed out of her umbrella and bit her on dorsum (R) hand. Spider identified as a Latrodectus hasselti by patient and nurse accompanying her. On examination in emergency department: mild pain at bite site - no other signs and symptoms of envenomation. Given oral analgesia; observed and appeared to have settled - discharged to attend her clinic. She was otherwise well and taking only Tamoxifen.
Patient returned to emergency department around 2 hours later with: increasing pain; profuse sweating of entire arm; erythema extending up arm in streaks; nausea; headache. Given: premedication of I/ V promethazine 25 mg; 1 ampoule Latrodectus hasselti antivenom I/ M, 2 hours post bite - little effect. Further antivenom administered 2 hours 30 minutes, 3 hours 15 minutes and 3 hours 45 minutes post bite. Pain localised to (R) hand; arm sweaty and erythematous. On advice from Dr. Struan Sutherland (AVRU), a further 2 ampoules given 6 hours post bite and 1 ampoule 7 hours post bite. Pain settled considerably but still present at bite site. Venom appeared to be remaining in the lymphatics of (R) arm and antivenom could not access the venom to neutralise it. Admitted to hospital and given morphine infusion for analgesia and observed overnight. FBC, urea, electrolytes,LFTs, creatinine kinase and coagulation profile were all normal. Following morning patient felt much better, with only mild pain and sweating confined to bite site. Discharged with follow-up appointment 1 week later.
At follow-up reported: localised pain and sweating had settled by 2 days post bite; now had heaviness of arm; intermittent headaches; occasional nausea. It was felt that this represented residual envenomation - a further ampoule antivenom was injected subcutaneously into bite site on the premise that the antivenom would enter the same lymphatics as the venom. No immediate result; patient discharged home. 2 days later: arm felt much better; had developed a 'flu-like illness, with arthralgia and myalgia, which had resolved spontaneously. 5 days later her only complaint was a worsening of the lymphoedema. 3 weeks later: markedly increased lymphoedema (describing her arm as easily fatigued and "heavy"), presumably in response to inflammation initiated by the bite. Referred to physiotherapy and underwent a programme of bandaging and exercise. After 3 weeks of treatment, nearly 2 months after initial presentation, she felt that her arm had returned to its previous level of function and degree of lymphoedema.

Case Report.
43 yr. old female with past history of rheumatic fever was admitted to hospital with flushing, sweating and severe myalgia. 14 days earlier she had been bitten on her (R) arm by a green ant - next day developed (R) axillary tenderness and a week later (R) hand became inflamed and 2 nodules were seen involving (R) 4th finger. At this time treated with antibiotics.
Next day developed generalised myalgia, lancinating limb pains and profuse sweating with neck and facial swelling - admitted to local hospital. Antibiotic treatment was changed; treatment with antihistamines and corticosteroids commenced, with no effect over next 6 days.
On transfer to Ballarat Base Hospital: flushed; unwell but afebrile; blood pressure - 140/ 90 mm.Hg; erythema to inner aspect (R) arm; 2 hyperplastic nodules consistent with orf infection on the (R) hand; angioedema of neck without lymphadenopathy. Pulse - varied from 52 - 83 b.p.m; blood pressure - varied from 110/ 60 - 150/ 80 mm.Hg. over next 5 days. Investigations revealed WCC - 22.6 X 10 exp.9/ L . Medications other than analgesia were withdrawn.
During 5 days observation her myalgia and autonomic symptoms persisted. These symptoms (which would not be related to either green ant bite or orf infection) and the possibility of exposure to Latrodectus hasselti led to the decision to try Latrodectus hasselti antivenom, particularly in view of its safety. In view of the uncertainty of diagnosis, placebo I/M normal saline injection was given with no effect, and 2 hours later a dose of antivenom was given with a reduction of pain and cessation of flushing in 1 hour and complete resolution of symptoms in 2 hours.
The putative Latrodectus hasselti bite could have occurred up to nearly 3 weeks before administration of antivenom, which is not known to influence the symptoms of any other bite or condition. The resolution of symptoms suggests that the patient had unknowingly been bitten by a Latrodectus hasselti and confirms that late treatment is effective.

Case1.
34 yr. old male sustained bite to abdomen from a Latrodectus hasselti. While removing the spider he may have been bitten again on back of (L) hand. After 45 minutes: local swelling; nausea; vomiting. Presented to a teaching hospital emergency department where he was given 1 ampoule Latrodectus hasselti antivenom I/ M. An I/V cannula had been sited in dorsum (L) hand for giving antihistamine premedication. Next day developed pain and swelling of dorsum of this hand which was initially treated with antibiotics, first orally then intravenously. After discharge from hospital the pain, swelling and burning in hand continued. On 2 separate occasions stellate ganglion block relieved the pain but not completely; hence a brachial plexus catheter was inserted for pain control. He continued to experience pain, swelling of the hand and bruising of the forearm and hand.
Assessed by neurologist who diagnosed sympathetic dystrophy, possibly secondary to infection associated with the intravenous cannula; suggested antibiotics and continuing analgesia. An aspirate from the dorsum of the hand grew no organisms.
Because of lack of a reflex dystrophy, the patient was referred to one of the authors (GAJ), who considered an alternative diagnosis of persisting envenomation and gave patient more antivenom (one ampoule I/ M) 10 days post bite. Little immediate change, but over ensuing days swelling localised to dorsum of hand and pain levels fell so that narcotics were no longer required. Subsequently, the patient became asymptomatic.

Case 2.
58 yr. old female presented at hospital with malaise and (R) shoulder and arm pain. Symptoms had developed suddenly 1 month earlier when she awoke with severe (R) subscapular pain radiating down (R) arm of such intensity that she called doctor for analgesia. No history of trauma.
Since then continuing pain of varying intensity in (R) shoulder and arm, intermittent migratory frontal headache and episodic parasthesia of middle 3 digits of (R) hand. No muscle weakness. One day after onset of pain developed pain in both ankles, which made it difficult to walk. One day later pain migrated to both upper shins and then to both knees, resolving after several days. She had not experienced sweating or fever. Hospitalised for neck traction and analgesia; a concurrent test for Ross River virus was negative. Nausea and vomiting were attributed to the oral and parenteral analgesia (paracetamol, codeine and pethidine).The pain in arm (which was not exacerbated by movement) persisted despite the analgesia. On day prior to presentation, had developed pain in (R) side of face, which on day of presentation had also involved the (L) side and jaw. Patient remembered that the day before onset of symptoms she had been at an outdoor wedding and had felt an insect crawling across her shoulder; it had been flicked off by a bystander and not identified. Felt no bite but later noticed irritation of (R) shoulder which she attributed to the strap of her dress. There was an area of erythema noted by doctor who initially saw her.
On examination:(R) wrist deformity from previous Colles' fracture; temperature - 37.1°C; pulse - 88 b.p.m. and regular. (R) shoulder, arm and neck were not tender; neck movement and (R) arm movement were full and painless, both active and resisted. Neurological examination of (R) arm showed no abnormalities; no palpable lymphadenopathy; lower limbs and gait were normal. Latrodectus hasselti bite with systemic envenomation was diagnosed and the patient was given 1 ampoule of antivenom by intramuscular injection. Over next hour her pain diminished markedly and she was discharged the same day.
Next day had only very occasional twinges of pain and did not require analgesia. 2 days later all symptoms had resolved except for parasthesia of (R) middle 3 digits, which had become more noticeable once her pain had resolved. No evidence of wasting of hands; normal power; normal senation to pinprick and light touch in all areas. Response to Phalen's test and Tinel's sign were negative. Carpal tunnel syndrome was diagnosed and patient referred to a hand surgeon.
10 days after being given antivenom, patient had persisting parasthesia of middle 3 digits and mild hand pain. Her symptoms and signs were suggestive of carpal tunnel syndrome but nerve conduction studies performed 1 week later, when patient's parasthesia had improved, were within normal limits.
22 days after being given antivenom most of the neurological symptoms had resolved and at review five and a half weeks later patient was totally asymptomatic.

Case 3.
21 yr. old male sustained bite to (R) index fingertip form a Latrodectus hasselti . Within 30 minutes of bite: nausea; sweating; headache; abdominal cramps and parasthesia of (R) forearm.
Presented at hospital and was given 1 ampoule Latrodectus hasselti antivenom I/ M, 40 minutes post bite. At that time: mildly dyspnoeic, anxious, had difficulty balancing; parasthesia of (R) upper limb distal to elbow. After several hours his symptoms had largely resolved and he was discharged with mild (R) arm pain.
2 days later: dyspnoea on exertion which progressed until it was present at rest; mild pleuritic chest pain on deep inspiration; tremor; poor balance; loss of use of (R) arm.
After 3 months: referred to a private pain clinic where delayed Latrodectus hasselti envenomation was diagnosed. Further dose of antivenom given I/ M, with some improvement of symptoms for 24 hours only. He was then given an infusion of 2 ampoules antivenom diluted to 40 ml. with normal saline into (R) upper limb using an intravenous regional technique (as for a Bier's block).
2 days later: considerably less pain and parasthesia of (R) upper limb; slight sensory blunting to pain over most of (R) hand, with associated mild weakness of grip strength. Respiratory symptoms continued although lung function was assessed as normal.
22 months after being bitten he had continuing mild disability, with weakness of grip strength and intermittent parasthesia and pallor but little reisdual pain.

11 yr old girl was referred with persistent severe pain in her (R) arm after a possible spider bite 10 days previously. 12 day old female infant (weight - 2650g) was admitted to Princess Margaret Hospital for Children,Perth, with a suspected Latrodectus hasselti bite. while being breast fed in bed, she suddenly stopped feeding and began screaming. Shortly afterwards, her mother felt 2 bites on the lower part of her body and, on pulling back the bedclothes, found and killed a female Latrodectus hasselti .
When first examined, almost 30 minutes after suspected bite, the baby was irritable and had an area of erythema over the (R) elbow. Within 2 hours of admission to hospital she had become extremely irritable, was crying unconsolably, and her skin was suffused and patchily erythematous. There was erythema, swelling, and a 1 cm. wheal with a central haemorrhagic spot on the lateral aspect of the (R) elbow. Pulse - 200 b.p.m, and she was dribbling copious amounts of saliva. When attempt made at bottle feeding, she was unable to swallow and gagged.
In view of the rapid progression of signs of envenomation, a test dose of antivenom (0.1 mL) given S/ C followed 20 minutes later by 1 mL antivenom I/ V over 15 minutes. Within 20 minutes of antivenom administration: colour had returned to normal and she was asleep. Observation was contiued for 48 hours and, during this time, she fed normally and her behaviour and activity remained normal. At follow-up 12 months later, no evidence of any abnormality was found.
This baby had many signs of Latrodectus hasselti envenomation - wheal, patchy erythema of the skin, irritability, and tachycardia. She also showed signs of bulbar involvement with marked gagging and refusal to feed, which was aggravated by excessive salivation. Although local and generalised sweating are very common signs of latrodectism, these were absent in this baby. This is an expected finding in neonates, who are unble to sweat. The absence of sweating in neonates makes the diagnosis of latrodectism more difficult, and it is suggested that this possibility should be considered in any baby with sudden onset of intractable crying.

## Clinical Effects

Fatal outcome very unlikely

The syndrome of envenoming by widow spiders (Latrodectus), including the Australian red back spider, L. hasseltii, is known as Latrodectism. Most widow spider bites will be minor (80%). Significant bites are usually felt as a mild "sting". A variable time later (10mins to many hours) local pain develops, becoming severe, sometimes associated with local sweating, erythema, central blanching or petechiae. The pain may become very severe and extend proximally (for limb bites) to become regional. It may extend further to become more generalised pain, such as chest pain (mimicking myocardial pain) or abdominal pain (mimicking acute abdomen) or head/neck pain (contorted "facies latrodectisma"). There may be regional or generalised profuse sweating. Hypertension is common, occasionally severe. Nausea is common, vomiting uncommon. Depending on species, there may be detectable muscle spasms associated with the pain. Most patients are moderately to severely debilitated by the envenoming, with malaise. Pain may gravitate away from the bite site to involve other areas. Untreated bites may result in leg pain, worse in the feet, associated with profuse lower limb sweating, even for bites to the upper limb. Many other symptoms may occur, but less frequently. Pulmonary oedema is very rare. Generalised muscle paralysis is also rare. Untreated, major systemic envenoming can cause significant symptoms over an extended period of 2-5 days up to many weeks. Death is a very rare outcome, most likely due to secondary factors.

### Special Risk Groups
#### Children

Risk to children is similar to risk for adults, but childs smaller body mass implies comparative venom concentration will be higher, so more severe envenoming likely, the increased risk being inversely proportional to the child's size.

#### Pregnancy

Potential risk to foetus from systemic envenoming, similar to risk to mother

#### Elderly

The higher incidence of pre-existing diseases in the elderly and associated frailty place them at higher risk. Increased risk will largely be determined by nature and extent of pre-existing disease and by any medications used to treat such disease.

## First Aid

Keep victim still, apply local cold pack, transport to medical care.

First aid for Widow Spider Bites (includes Australian red back spider)

1. After ensuring the patient and onlookers are no longer at risk of further bites by the spider, the bitten person should be reassured and persuaded to lie down and remain still. Some will be terrified, fearing sudden death and, in this mood, they may behave irrationally or even hysterically. The basis for reassurance is the fact that many bites do not result in envenoming, death is a very rare outcome, and the effectiveness of modern medical treatment.
1. The bite wound should not be tampered with in any way.
1. Some victims find the application of a local cold pack may relieve local pain.
1. Do not apply a local bandage, tourniquet, or cut or suck or incise the wound or apply electric shock. Application of local heat has not proved beneficial.
1. If there is any impairment of vital functions, such as problems with respiration, airway, circulation, heart function, these must be supported as a priority. In particular, both airway and respiration may be impaired, requiring urgent and prolonged treatment, which may include the mouth to mask (mouth to mouth) technique of expired air transfer. Seek urgent medical attention.
1. If the offending spider has been killed or caught it should be brought with the patient for identification.
1. Avoid peroral intake, other than clear fluids, in the first 6 hours, absolutely no alcohol. No sedatives outside hospital. If there will be considerable delay before reaching medical aid, measured in several hours to days, then give clear fluids by mouth to prevent dehydration.
1. Most traditional, and many of the more recently fashionable, first aid measures are useless and potentially dangerous. These include local cauterization, incision, excision, amputation, suction by mouth, vacuum pump or syringe, combined incision and suction ("venom-ex" apparatus), injection or instillation of compounds such as potassium permanganate, phenol (carbolic soap) and trypsin, application of electric shocks or ice (cryotherapy), use of traditional herbal, folk and other remedies including the ingestion of emetic plant products and parts of the snake, multiple incisions, tattooing and so on.

## Medical Treatment

Widow spider bites are mostly minor and even significant envenoming is unlikely to be lethal, so after a 6 hr observation, asymptomatic patients can be discharged. Symptomatic patients should be considered for IM or IV antivenom therapy, as this is the only effective treatment for envenoming.

Most cases of latrodectism will be minor, requiring only simple analgesia. The minority of cases will develop significant distressing regional or generalised effects, most commonly pain, sweating, hypertension, nausea & malaise. These cases usually do not respond well to even IV analgesics, antiemetics, but do respond to appropriate antivenom, either IM or IV. Antivenom is therefore the treatment of choice in such cases. Dosage will depend on choice of antivenom; for Australian CSL Red Back Spider Antivenom (works for all widow spiders) initial dose is 1 vial IM, wait 2hrs, if inadequate response give 2nd vial, wait another 2hrs, if required give 3rd vial. 4+ vials occasionally required, especially if long delay (days) between bite & treatment, but allow longer between doses and consider IV route.
