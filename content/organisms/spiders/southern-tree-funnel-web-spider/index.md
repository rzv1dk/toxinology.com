## Case Studies

Dieckmann J.,Prebble J.,McDonogh A.,Sara A.,Fisher M.(1989)Efficacy of funnel-web spider antivenom in human envenomation by Hadronyche species. The Medical Journal of Australia. Vol. 151, pp 706-707.

Case Report.
4 yr. old boy sustained bite to (R) hypochondrium from a Hadronyche cerberea (positively identified by Lyn Abra from Australian Reptile Park, Gosford.) Taken to local doctor and then to Wyong Hospital. In ambulance: vomited; extremely sweaty - especially around face; piloerection of upper body but not of legs. Immediately transferred to Gosford District Hospital - in course of transfer, 2 brief episodes of ventricular tachycardia occurred. No first aid applied. On arrival at 1130 hrs: sweaty; agitated; bradycardia; ventricular escape beats. Blood pressure - 160/ 110 mm.Hg; chest clear; abdomen soft; obvious bite marks on lower chest. At 1135 hrs: given atropine 200 micrograms - developed a sinus tachycardia of 150 b.p.m and ventricular ectopic beats disappeared. Given: clemastine 0.25 mg; hydrocortisone 100mg; 2 X ampoules funnel-web spider antivenom by intravenous infusion over 15 minutes. Chest remained clear; piloerection and sweating disappeared.
By 1145 hrs: blood pressure - 140/ 104 mm.Hg; piloerection reappeared in (R) thigh and nipple - given further ampoule antivenom. At 1150 hrs: chest clear; blood pressure - 120/ 100 mm.Hg; piloerection disappearing; alert; good peripheral perfusion. Blood gas analysis showed mild metabolic acidosis with pH - 7.31 (normal, 7.35 - 7.45) and base excess -6.8 mmol/ L (normal) -2 to + 4mmol/ L).
At 1300 hrs. circumoral sweating retuned as did slight piloerection; blood pressure - 110/ 100 mm.Hg. Given further ampoule funnel-web antivenom - apart from remaining slightly sweaty, had no further problems.

## Clinical Effects

Rate of fatality unclear, due to inadequate reporting, but fatalities cannot be excluded

Only a few bites with systemic envenoming are recorded for this species. Funnel web spiders have a low rate of effective bites, but when systemic envenoming does occur it is often very rapid in onset, with tingling of the lips or tongue, followed by tongue fasciculation within 10-30 minutes. There is then rapid progression, with nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, tachycardia, hypertension, piloerection, hypersalivation, hyperlachrymation, increased sweating, anxiety and development of often severe pulmonary oedema, with hypoxia and coma. Convulsions, though not common, may occur. Untreated, this progression may result in death in 1-2 hours (shortest time 15 minutes), or longer in adults. If this early phase is not lethal, the patient may show subsidence of the symptoms, followed by muscle fasciculations, progressive hypotension and cardiac failure or arrest. Death is a result of either fulminant pulmonary oedema or intractable hypotension and cardiac arrest. No deaths are recorded so far for this particular species.

### Special Risk Groups
#### Children

Risk to children is similar to risk for adults, but childs smaller body mass implies comparative venom concentration will be higher, so more severe envenoming likely, the increased risk being inversely proportional to the child's size.

#### Pregnancy

Potential risk to foetus from systemic envenoming, similar to risk to mother

#### Elderly

The higher incidence of pre-existing diseases in the elderly and associated frailty place them at higher risk. Increased risk will largely be determined by nature and extent of pre-existing disease and by any medications used to treat such disease.

### Coloration & Markings

Glossy dark brown to black on the carapace and brown to black, with light maroon-brown to dark brown dorsolateral abdomen.

### Habits

Tree-dwelling species that is widely distributed in open forest habitats. Burrows are associated with tree-trunk fractures, rot-holes and borer holes, with the entrance sheet and trip-lines often disguised by bark/leaf detritus and frass.

Sexual Dimorphism

Carapace. Broad, about as long as wide, strongly raised (females), and slightly longer than wide, moderately raised (males).

## First Aid

Keep victim still, apply pressure immobilisation bandage & splint, gently wash bite area, transport to medical care

First aid for Funnel Web Spider Bites (Australia)

1. If the spider is still attached, immediately remove it, being careful not to sustain further bites, and place to spider in a jam jar or other container, so it may be brought to the hospital for identification.
1. After ensuring the patient and onlookers are no longer at risk of further bites by the spider, the bitten person should be reassured and persuaded to lie down and remain still. Many will be terrified, fearing sudden death and, in this mood, they may behave irrationally or even hysterically. The basis for reassurance is the fact that many bites do not result in envenoming, and the effectiveness of modern medical treatment.
1. The bite wound should not be tampered with in any way.
1. If the bite is on a limb, a broad bandage (even torn strips of clothing or pantyhose) should be applied over the bitten area at moderate pressure (as for a sprain; not so tight circulation is impaired), then extended to cover as much of the bitten limb as possible, including fingers or toes, going over the top of clothing rather than risking excessive limb movement by removing clothing. The bitten limb should then be immobilised as effectively as possible using an extemporised splint or sling.
1. If there is any impairment of vital functions, such as problems with respiration, airway, circulation, heart function, these must be supported as a priority. In particular, both airway and respiration may be impaired, requiring urgent and prolonged treatment, which may include the mouth to mask (mouth to mouth) technique of expired air transfer. Seek urgent medical attention.
1. Do not use Tourniquets, cut, suck or scarify the wound or apply chemicals or electric shock.
1. Avoid peroral intake, absolutely no alcohol. No sedatives outside hospital. If there will be considerable delay before reaching medical aid, measured in several hours to days, then give clear fluids by mouth to prevent dehydration.
1. If the offending spider has been killed or caught it should be brought with the patient for identification. Many relatively harmless spiders may appear similar to the funnel web spiders and there are several species of funnel web spiders, so identification of the spider is important.
1. The spider bite victim should be transported as quickly and as passively as possible to the nearest place where they can be seen by a medically-trained person (health station, dispensary, clinic or hospital). The bitten limb must not be exercised as muscular contraction will promote systemic absorption of venom. If no motor vehicle or boat is available, the patient can be carried on a stretcher or hurdle, on the pillion or crossbar of a bicycle or on someone's back.
1. Most traditional, and many of the more recently fashionable, first aid measures are useless and potentially dangerous. These include local cauterization, incision, excision, amputation, suction by mouth, vacuum pump or syringe, combined incision and suction ("venom-ex" apparatus), injection or instillation of compounds such as potassium permanganate, phenol (carbolic soap) and trypsin, application of electric shocks or ice (cryotherapy), use of traditional herbal, folk and other remedies including the ingestion of emetic plant products and parts of the snake, multiple incisions, tattooing and so on.

## Medical Treatment

Funnel web spider bites, though mostly minor, can cause severe, even fatal envenoming rapidly, so all cases should be managed as a medical emergency. At the first sign of systemic envenoming, IV CSL Funnel Web Spider Antivenom should be given, 2-4 vials initially, depending on severity, followed by further doses if envenoming persists or recurs.

Most cases of funnel web spider bite will not show systemic envenoming and will not require antivenom. While systemic envenoming usually develops rapidly, occasionally it is delayed. All suspected funnel web spider bites should be observed in hospital for at least 8hrs, or overnight for children. First aid should be left on for 3-4hrs as this may assist local inactivation of venom. Systemic envenoming by funnel web spiders is a medical emergency requiring urgent IV antivenom therapy. At the earliest sign of systemic envenoming, such as perioral tingling or tongue fasciculation, give antivenom. The initial dose should be 2 vials CSL Funnel Web Spider Antivenom (4 vials if major envenoming already present), but be prepared to give further doses. 6-8 vials is common for severe envenoming. Higher doses have occasionally been required. Continued pulmonary oedema requires further antivenom (unless it is certain the oedema is purely due to iatrogenic IV fluid overload). Relapse requires further antivenom. As late relapse can occur, any patient requiring antivenom must be admitted and observed for at least 24hrs.

### Management in Detail
#### Immediate Effects Management

Establish IV line, IV fluids, secure airway, maintain respiration if imperiled, treat hypotension (if present), clean wound

#### Approach to Management

All cases should be treated as urgent & potentially lethal. Rapid assessment & commencement of treatment including appropriate antivenom (if indicated & available) is mandatory. Admit all cases.

#### Follow Up

Follow up all cases given antivenom therapy, checking particularly for serum sickness
