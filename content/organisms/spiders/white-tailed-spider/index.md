## Case Studies

Pincus S.J., Winkel J.D., Hawdon G.M. and Sutherland S.K. (1999) Acute and recurrent skin ulceration after spider bite. Med. J. Aust., Vol. 171, No. 2, pp 99-102

Case 1.
27 yr. old Queensland woman was bitten on leg by a female Lampona cylindrata (positively identified by one of the authors, SKS). She developed a pimple-like lesion that blistered and broke down to form a 2 X 2 cm. ulcer. She was treated with doxycycline, and healed over 1 month.

Case 2.
38 yr. old Victorian male was bitten on the calf by a female Lampona cylindrata (positively identified by the Victorian Museum). The bite was painful, itchy, erythematous and blistered, and progressed to shallow ulcers, while he became feverish. He was treated with doxycycline and antihistamines, and the original lesion healed over 10 days. He has since had multiple episodes of similar lesions, with a gradual decrease in frequency.

Case 3.
33 yr. old New South Wales male was bitten on the leg by a Lampona cylindrata (also sighted by the local medical officer).Initially the bite produced a small, red, painful lesion. Culture produced a scant growth of S. aureus. The patient was treated with doxycycline, and the lesion healed, then broke down at one month into a 6 X 6 cm. ulcer that healed over 4 months.

Skinner M.W. and Butler C.S. (1995) Necrotising arachnidism treated with hyperbaric oxygen. Med. J. Aust., Vol. 162, No. 1, pp 372-373.

Case 1.
25 yr. old male bricklayer presented to G.P. with painful, swollen ulcerated lesion on (R) calf, 24 hrs. post spider bite. His description was consistent with that of a Lampona cylindrata, but no specimen was provided. Prescribed oral amoxycillin. Initial lesion enlarged considerably over following 24 hrs.; admitted to hospital for I/ V antibiotic therapy. On admission: painful, swollen erythematous with central ulceration on (R) calf; afebrile; tender (R) popliteal and inguinal lymph nodes.
WCC - 14.4 X 10 exp.9/ L of which 9.6 X 10 exp.9/ L were neutrophils. Results of other routine investigations were normal and both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures showed no growth. Wound swab from ulcer base grew Staphylococcus aureus.
Treated with I/ V penicillin, flucloxacillin and gentamicin - no initial improvement and hyperbaric oxygen therapy was started 5 days post bite. He had 5 treatments over 4 days (2 treatments with 100% oxygen at 2.8 bars for 60 min., with a 30 min. ascent, followed by 3 treatments of similar length at 2.4 bars). Wound was debrided and biopsied between treatments.
Biopsy revealed deep dermal necrosis, with an appreciable inflammatory response. No acid-fast bacilli or other bacteria were seen.
Patient progressed well and was discharged after 7 days for outpatient follow-up. Lesion had completely healed at his final 3 month review.

Case 2.
38 yr.old male who bred Lampona cylindrata spiders for a hobby, sustained bite to (L) hand. Admitted to hospital with a 4 day history of pain and swelling. No known previous spider envenomation. Treatment with oral flucloxacillin for 48 hrs. prior to admission had resulted in no improvement.
On examination: necrotic area 2 cm. diameter on dorsum (L) hand; surrounding erythema and swelling; tender axillary lymph nodes.
Haematology and biochemistry results were normal. No growth on anaerobic and aerobic blood cultures. Wouns swab - moderate growth Staphylococcus aureus - sensitive to gentimicin and erythromycin.
No improvement with I/ V antibiotics over 48 hrs. - commenced on hyperbaric treatment. Underwent 5 treatments over next 3 days, each with 100% oxygen at 2.4 bars for 60 min., with 15 min. ascent.
Pain and swelling resolved, and wound subsequently healed.

Subsequent information relayed to a member of the Clinical Toxinology Resources Website staff casts doubt on the validity of at least one of these cases. It is our opinion that neither case represents proven primary venom necrosis from a white tailed spider bite.

Gray M. (1989) A significant illness that was produced by the white-tailed spider, Lampona cylindrata. Med. J. Aust., Vol. 151, No. 2, pp 114-116.

Case Report. (From Tasmania, Australia)
Previously healthy 33 yr. old female, sustained bite to inner (R) thigh from a spider thought to be a Lampona cylindrata (never properly identified; considerable doubt that it was this species), whilst asleep in bed. Was awoken by pain at bite site and, on lifting nightdress, found a small, brown, cigar-shaped spider with a white dot on its tail, in the act of biting her. Spider flicked off and killed. Bitten area became progressively more painful over following 2 days, she felt generally unwell and lethargic with bouts of nausea and progressive lack of coordination.
On admission to hospital on day 4 post bite: in obvious distress; lesion on thigh consisted of vesicles at bite site surrounded by erythema extending up to 8 cm. Temperature - 37.1°C; regional lymph nodes enlarged and tender; whole area extraordinarily painful - patient could not bear to have it touched or covered in any way. Because of possibility of infective component, commenced on doxycycline 200 mg stat. then 100 mg. daily. Swab of largest vesicle showed no growth; occasional white cells and small number of epithelial cells. Pain not relieved with Panadeine Forte, or later, Pethidine.
Within 5 days: coordination had improved; vesicles had ruptured leaving an area of necrosis the size of a 20 cent coin and a zone of cyanosis the size of the palm of a hand. Pain remained severe over whole region. Within 3 weeks the lesion had granulated and required no further treatment.

## Clinical Effects

Bites cause minor short term local effects only.

White tailed spiders (Lampona cylindrata) are native to Australia but have traveled elsewhere, particularly New Zealand. In Australia they have, in recent years, developed a reputation for causing severe local effects, including necrosis, often associated with systemic illness and allegedly a few cases of amputation of bitten limbs or even deaths. This entire clinical picture is essentially based on misinformation and should be considered as at best, speculative, at worst, completely incorrect.

There is a growing body of clinical experience with absolutely confirmed bites and venom research, not all published yet (major papers in preparation) which gives a clear picture of bites by white tailed spiders. The bite is painful, sometimes quite severely so. The spider is therefore likely to be seen by the victim. The local area may develop a red lump of variable size, which will subside after 24 hours approximately, sometimes longer. There are generally no significant systemic effects. The bite does not ulcerate. It does not become necrotic. It does not recur after a period of time. It generally does not even become infected. Bites by white tailed spiders are essentially a short term local problem, not usually requiring medical attention. They are quite different in presentation to cases of suspected "necrotic arachnidism" resulting in local tissue injury or necrosis.

There are several single or multiple case reports of alleged white tailed spider bites in the literature, resulting in tissue injury or necrosis, most listed here, but those experts with considerable clinical experience of envenoming consider probably all these cases are suspect. That is, there is very considerable doubt that the cases are valid confirmed bites by white tailed spiders or that the local effects are venom mediated. There is a growing experience in Australia of patients causing deliberate self harm, resulting in local tissue injury or necrosis, then claiming this is due to a white tailed spider bite. The reasons these patients undertake such actions are various and not always certain, though a common theme appears to be a desire to seek compensation through courts or media attention. In some cases ascribed to bites by white tailed spiders, it is clear another disease process is responsible. Diseases to consider are peripheral vascular disease, diabetes, autoimmune diseases and pyoderma gangrenosum, as well as infections, including infections by unusual bacteria, atypical bacteria and fungi.

A bite should only be ascribed to a white tailed spider if it is quite clear that the spider was seen to cause the bite, was caught and reliably identified (which usually means by a museum arachnologist), and the pattern of clinical effects fits with known effects of this spider. Should all these criteria be met in a case developing local tissue injury or necrosis, all other causes of such injury must be first excluded, which may include local skin biopsy, before considering the injury is due to venom. Studies on the venom of the white tailed spider have failed to show evidence that it can cause tissue injury or necrosis.

## First Aid

First aid for spiderbite (other than noted species, such as Australian funnel web spiders, mouse spiders, widow spiders, recluse spiders, banana spiders).

1. After ensuring the patient and onlookers are no longer at risk of further bites by the spider, the bitten person should be reassured and persuaded to lie down and remain still. Some will be terrified, fearing sudden death and, in this mood, they may behave irrationally or even hysterically. The basis for reassurance is the fact that many bites do not result in envenoming, death is a very rare outcome, and the effectiveness of modern medical treatment.
1. The bite wound should not be tampered with in any way.
1. Some victims find the application of a local cold pack may relieve local pain.
1. Do not apply a local bandage, tourniquet, or cut or suck or incise the wound or apply electric shock. Application of local heat has not proved beneficial.
1. If there is any impairment of vital functions, such as problems with respiration, airway, circulation, heart function, these must be supported as a priority. In particular, for bites causing flaccid paralysis, including respiratory paralysis, both airway and respiration may be impaired, requiring urgent and prolonged treatment, which may include the mouth to mask (mouth to mouth) technique of expired air transfer. Seek urgent medical attention.
1. If the offending spider has been killed or caught it should be brought with the patient for identification.
1. Avoid peroral intake, other than clear fluids, in the first 6 hours, absolutely no alcohol. No sedatives outside hospital. If there will be considerable delay before reaching medical aid, measured in several hours to days, then give clear fluids by mouth to prevent dehydration.
1. Most traditional, and many of the more recently fashionable, first aid measures are useless and potentially dangerous. These include local cauterization, incision, excision, amputation, suction by mouth, vacuum pump or syringe, combined incision and suction ("venom-ex" apparatus), injection or instillation of compounds such as potassium permanganate, phenol (carbolic soap) and trypsin, application of electric shocks or ice (cryotherapy), use of traditional herbal, folk and other remedies including the ingestion of emetic plant products and parts of the spider, multiple incisions, tattooing and so on.
1. If the spider is known to be harmless, it may not be necessary to seek medical aid, but if in any doubt, either seek urgent advice from the regional poisons information service or regional hospital, or transport the patient to a hospital.

## Medical Treatment

Bites by white tailed spiders cause only mild local effects, not requiring medical treatment, unless secondary infection develops (rare).

In general, the only treatment required for white tailed spider bites is reassurance that the many stories told over back fences and in the media, that this spider causes necrosis and loss of limbs, are entirely untrue. While secondary infection is rare (if it occurs at all) with white tailed spider bite, patients should be advised to return if the bite site becomes painful, tender, or inflamed or if it discolours or blisters, or if they develop systemic symptoms.

There is no antivenom available or required. No venom detection is available. Should secondary infection develop, test for unusual bacteria and fungi. If local tissue injury occurs, consider other causes, such as peripheral vascular disease, diabetes, autoimmune disease, pyoderma gangrenosum.
