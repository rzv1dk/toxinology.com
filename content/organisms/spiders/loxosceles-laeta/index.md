## Clinical Effects

Fatal outcome rare

The syndrome of envenoming caused by Loxosceles spiders is known as Loxoscelism. CUTANEOUS LOXOSCELISM is by far the most common type. The bite is often minor, occurring when putting on clothing, more usually not even noticed, occurring at night. A variable time later, 6-24 hours, local discomfort and redness develops, often with oedema. This lesion evolves into mottled dark or haemorrhagic areas, sometimes with local blistering. Pain may become moderate to severe. The local lesion is usually associated with general symptoms, including fever, malaise, or a rash, which subside after 2-3 days. The local lesion continues to evolve such that by 5-7 days an area of necrosis or ulceration is evident, often with a dry grey or blue-black eschar. The area of necrosis may extend slightly over the next few weeks, with recurrent breakdown, pain & ulceration, lasting 4-6+ weeks, resistant to early treatment or skin grafting. Occasionally in more severe cases, the area of necrosis can extend well beyond the initial area, rarely affecting wide areas of skin. Necrosis & ulceration is deep, sometimes extending well beyond the dermis. VISCERO-CUTANEOUS LOXOSCELISM is uncommon to rare (more common in South America than North America) and in addition to all the features of the cutaneous form, is also associated with potentially lethal systemic effects, including haemolysis, thrombocytopenia, disseminated intravascular coagulation, secondary renal failure and shock.

### Special Risk Groups
#### Children

Risk to children is similar to risk for adults, but childs smaller body mass implies comparative venom concentration will be higher, so more severe envenoming likely, the increased risk being inversely proportional to the child's size.

#### Pregnancy

No information available

#### Elderly

The higher incidence of pre-existing diseases in the elderly and associated frailty place them at higher risk. Increased risk will largely be determined by nature and extent of pre-existing disease and by any medications used to treat such disease.

### Coloration & Markings

Males and Females: Colour and pattern is quite variable with a weak fiddle shaped pattern. General colour may be grey to blackish, tawny, brown or yellowish to reddish brown. Carapace colour ranges from bright yellow to orange to reddish brown with a pattern of dark longitudinal lines and sometimes with a pair of pale spots on posterior pars cephalica. Abdomen is white to greyish with black hairs resulting in a dusky appearance.

## Diagnosis

Bite usually minor or not noticed; progressive local erythema, pain, mottled haemorrhagic colour, blisters, eschar formation (5-7 days), systemically unwell.

## First Aid

No effective first aid available.

First aid for Recluse Spider Bites (includes fiddleback spiders)

1. Most bites go unnoticed at the time, only becoming evident later, once local tissue injury commences, by which time first aid is useless.
1. If the bite is witnessed, capture the spider and take it with the victim to a hospital or doctor.
1. Most traditional, and many of the more recently fashionable, first aid measures are useless and potentially dangerous. These include local cauterization, incision, excision, amputation, suction by mouth, vacuum pump or syringe, combined incision and suction ("venom-ex" apparatus), injection or instillation of compounds such as potassium permanganate, phenol (carbolic soap) and trypsin, application of electric shocks or ice (cryotherapy), use of traditional herbal, folk and other remedies including the ingestion of emetic plant products and parts of the snake, multiple incisions, tattooing and so on.

## Medical Treatment

The treatment of loxoscelism is controversial. Except for Brazil, no antivenom is available and even in Brazil, its role is uncertain. Good wound care, targeted treatment of secondary infection, avoidance of early surgical intervention and pain relief are important. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy remains an unproven treatment, though some experts believe it can assist pain relief and healing. Patients should be aware that local skin necrosis may take many weeks or months to heal. Patients with viscerocutaneous loxoscelism require urgent medical attention and full supportive therapy.

The management of loxoscelism is controversial and varies with country and availability of antivenom. Many other diseases are frequently misdiagnosed as loxoscelism. Ensure other diseases have been excluded before treating as loxoscelism. Good wound care and the treatment of secondary infection, if present, is crucial. Early surgical debridement is contraindicated and should be delayed at least 5 weeks from the time of the bite. Steroids are of no proven benefit. Dapsone, though effective if given early, is toxic and no longer recommended. Hyperbaric oxygen is controversial, with conflicting evidence for effectiveness, but proponents claim it speeds healing, reduces extent of necrosis and dramatically reduces local pain. Antivenom is available in parts of Sth. America, notably Brazil, where it is claimed to be effective if used early, in the first few days after the bite. It may not completely reverse the local effects of venom, but may significantly reduce the extent of necrosis. It may also halt or reverse the effects of viscero-cutaneous loxoscelism. Particularly in Sth. America, patients should be checked for development of viscero-cutaneous loxoscelism, notably looking for haemolysis, thrombocytopenia, DIC and secondary renal failure. In the absence of antivenom, these must be treated as from any other cause.
